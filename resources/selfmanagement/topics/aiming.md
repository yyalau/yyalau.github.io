# Aiming & Question Quality
> Aim is the first encoding step of the Bear Hunter System: scope a topic, collect keywords, and drive inquiry with high-quality "why/how" questions to hypothesise a relevance backbone that primes the brain for fast, sticky learning.

## Overview
Aiming points the brain at a topic for maximum relevance. It consolidates the earlier inquiry, order-control and chunking skills into the **Aim** step of [bear-hunter-system](bear-hunter-system.md) (Aim → Shoot → Skin), and good Aim priming makes higher-order learning easier, faster and less overwhelming — Aim quality directly determines Shoot quality. This note covers the Aim procedure, question quality (asking AND answering), the traffic-light inquiry drill, pre-study, the common barriers, scoping, efficient sourcing, and subject-specific adaptations. Chunking mechanics live in [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md); the underlying memory science (cave theory, survive & thrive, order control, knowledge networks, layers/Bloom's, curiosity conditions, relevance-first scoping of unstructured resources) in [learning-science](learning-science.md); note representation (including blind mapping and segmental mapping) in [note-taking](note-taking.md).

## How to Aim (the Aim Step)
The first encoding step of BHS: collect keywords across all resources, then drive inquiry with two questions — **"Why is this important?"** and **"How does this relate to ___?"** — to hypothesise a backbone chunk structure that primes the brain for relevance and retention.

**Key principles**
- Questions determine how you think → which relationships you discover → what you evaluate/prioritise → which chunks you form. Two focused questions replace the many curiosity-driven traffic-light questions: fewer rabbit holes, more predictable structures, easier to coach.
- Aim at higher-order learning from the very first sentence — never start with memorisation or wait to "learn the basics" first. The brain fills in lower orders automatically; leftover odd facts go to flashcards afterwards. Memorising first learns information in isolation, which makes it harder to remember (Clinic 14). See [learning-science](learning-science.md).
- Never lead with "what is X?" — higher-order learning meets lower-order needs by default (you cannot compare two ideas without learning what they are). Learning definitions first in isolation wastes short-term memory because nothing integrates in time.
- "Why is it important?" triggers evaluative thinking that produces chunk structures transferable to most retrieval needs; it effectively subsumes every other question you could ask. See [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md) for importance-based chunking.
- Everything is a hypothesis: multiple possible relationships and groupings, constantly tested and revised through Aim, Shoot and even Skin. Being certain this early is bad; avoid the sunk-cost fallacy (don't keep a structure just because you invested effort).
- **Order control:** start down the list (early keywords have nothing to relate to yet), then let curiosity bounce you between keywords as candidate maps form — you direct the learning, not the resource order. Collect keywords deliberately out of the taught order to break framing bias. Never read linearly for long (~30 seconds max); an hour of linear reading boxes your framing (Clinic 18). Chunk for how YOU learn best, then practise retrieving in whatever format you'll be tested — never compromise encoding quality to fit a curriculum, textbook or teacher's layout (Clinics 19, 21). Underlying theory in [learning-science](learning-science.md).
- The Aim step is **purely textual**: keyword collection + high-quality chunking. Visuals can be added later during encoding (Clinic 18).
- Chunk refinement (intuitive/logical + importance-based) belongs in Aim as well as Shoot — make chunks accurate from the get-go to improve Aim–Shoot cycling (Clinic 20).
- Build a **shallow but wide net**: backbone + main chunks + main concepts, no key details. A wide net with holes catches far more than a tiny, tightly woven net; knowledge is built in layers.
- The evaluative thinking is desirable difficulty; keyword collection is the easy part — speed it up (skim video/captions, use transcription AI, or have AI extract concepts). With raw data, a lookup source and evaluative skill, learning becomes independent of teacher quality, resource format and how interesting the subject seems.

**Technique / steps**
1. **Collect keywords:** quickly skim all resources (video, book/linear notes, slides, course guide, textbook, Google/AI). Write an unordered list — deliberately out of the taught order. Invent your own keywords for unnamed concepts/diagrams. Target ~80% of major ideas (typically 15–30 per topic), not 100% — about 25 minutes, not 2 hours.
2. **Unknown terms:** read just enough for a general idea (≤30–40 seconds); if still foreign, ask Google/AI for a single-paragraph explanation. Never deep-dive here.
3. **Ask the two questions per keyword** (and per heading/group): "Why is this important?" and "How does it relate to [other keywords]?" Skim/read resources only to answer these — never consume information for its own sake.
4. Generate multiple relationships and candidate groupings; represent them non-linearly so the backbone is visible.
5. Hypothesise several possible chunk structures; keep revising as later keywords correct earlier ideas; go out of order once maps start forming.
6. Stop at a big-picture backbone: main ideas, main chunks, main concepts — enough to prime, not certain, not detailed.

**Common mistakes**
- Importance checklisting (one-word answer, then move on) — see Question Quality below.
- Getting sucked into details / pursuing maximum depth on every keyword.
- Committing early to one structure (sunk cost, framing bias); chunking in the exact order/headings the material was taught.
- Being a 100% completionist on keyword collection.
- Adding visuals during Aim (it is a textual keyword/chunking step) (Clinic 18).

**Practical tips**
- Self-check: you should feel heavy higher-order thinking (comparison/evaluation), lots of back-and-forth, and multiple hypotheses about relationships AND groupings. If not, raise your standard or think deeper; if it takes forever, you're too deep in the details.
- If keyword collection drags: you're collecting too many words, the volume exceeds your skill, or you're anchored in details.
- Across all subject demonstrations the common trend is: scope → order control → compare/evaluate/prioritise → hypothesise the scaffold; surface execution varies by subject.
- As skill grows, Aim bigger topics — goal: one entire topic in a single go.
- Aim and pre-study overlap but are not the same (see Pre-Study). Aim can double as pre-study; the cycling mechanics (Aim→Shoot→new questions) belong to [bear-hunter-system](bear-hunter-system.md).
- Reading a lot during Shoot to answer your questions is normal; don't chase speed (Clinic 18).

## Question Quality
Not all questions produce equal learning. The highest-quality questions ask **why/how** (forcing connections), are **genuinely unknown** to you, and ideally tap emotion, personal relevance or real curiosity.

**The quality hierarchy**
- "What is X / the definition / the process" questions are isolated and shallow — single nodes without connections (lower-order learning).
- "Why is this important / why does this happen / how does it relate" force big-picture, comparative, evaluative, higher-order thinking. Retention of individual facts is a by-product of strong relationships, not of drilling the fact (the train-station/knowledge-network mechanism — see [learning-science](learning-science.md)).
- **Jeopardy questions:** repackaging an answer you already know as a question ("this person wrote 1984" → "who is George Orwell?"). No curiosity, no new thinking; the brain's "this solves a problem" checkbox never fires for known answers.
- Best combination: why/how framing + genuinely unknown answer + genuine curiosity. The brain prioritises emotional, personal, curious material — it "cares more", so connections form easily. Creating such questions is a trainable skill; beginners default to what- and Jeopardy questions. Engineer the conditions for curiosity ([learning-science](learning-science.md), Clinic 40).
- Bloom's mapping: "how does it relate?" = Analyse (L4); "why is this important?" = Evaluate (L5) — the course's target level. See [learning-science](learning-science.md).

**Broad vs narrow; importance checklisting (Clinics 16, 24, 28, 29, 30)**
- "Why is this important?" must yield **MULTIPLE** reasons (A, B, C, D, E…). Another concept yields (S, T, U, V, W, C…); the overlap (C) is a shared reason for importance → a chunk. With one answer per concept there is nothing to compare. Example: a stylus (draw/explain/teach) and a mouse (navigate faster) share no importance reason → different chunks; a microphone (audio quality for teaching) shares "facilitates teaching" with the stylus → a "teaching" chunk (Clinic 17).
- **Importance checklisting** = asking the broad question but answering with a single linear answer and moving on (phone → "communication" only). It collapses the relationship pool and makes importance-based chunking fail. The question is a prompt to start thinking, not a checkbox. Build a schema instead: X is important because of Y, Z, Alpha, Beta — Z leads to Alpha, Alpha to Beta, B+C+D together influence Y (Clinic 24).
- **Importance is broader than cause-and-effect and broader than "so what"** (consequentialism): some things matter as prerequisites of understanding, not because of downstream effects. Cause-effect-only chunking risks single-node chains; "so what" narrows thinking and hurts later subtle conceptual work (Clinic 16).
- **Division of labour:** the broad "why is it important" creates the wide relationship pool for major chunks; specific "how does X relate to Y" questions close in, clarify pertinent relationships and rule out invalid ones. Ask both — but "how is X related to Y" can itself be too narrow if there is literally only one link (Clinic 25).
- **Lower-order inquiry** = a narrower rephrasing ("why is the pericardium important *to the heart*?") or any question yielding only one or two points. Same question, reduced scope, fewer relationships — it tricks you into thinking you did importance-based chunking, and is a fundamental ceiling on technique quality. Avoid it; ask the broad version. Genuine personal-curiosity questions outside textbook scope are fine and worth satisfying.
- Subtopics within a topic virtually ALWAYS have relationships — if none are visible, inquiry hasn't elucidated them yet; the relationship can be a simple contribution to the whole (Clinic 18).
- **"Spamming" the same question is GOOD:** the same thinking pattern applied to different information yields different learning each time and builds the habit/framework (like driving the same way in different cities — same navigation, different roads). The ASKING should feel repetitive; the ANSWERING should not. If answering feels repetitive or easy, something is wrong (checklisting / too surface) — push for more relationships and cognitive load (Clinic 24).
- ~100 keywords should collapse into ~5 major chunks, not 100 chunks. A keyword that fits no chunk: don't rebuild the whole structure for 1–2 odd keywords — note them as details to resolve later (Clinic 24).
- Just "asking why" is not the technique: the Ascent I version is more advanced — how you handle the volume of relationships and prioritise them matters more than the answers. Naive why-asking without deep-processing skill can make things worse (Clinic 30).
- If finding shared reasons feels easy/straightforward early on, cognitive load is probably too low — you're not going deep enough. Early stages should feel more overwhelming than easy.
- People who struggle to "bring it together" at Aim usually under-practiced the traffic-light drill — they never learned to use questions to create chunks (Clinic 28). If stuck, submit work for expert review WITH a rigorous COBES; the struggle is usually checklisting or shallow COBES (Clinic 28).

**Answering inquiry questions (Clinics 25, 26, 27)**
- The worst thing you can do is think of one answer and stop. Answer each question fully (all plausible answers), then compare/contrast them to find shared importance and form chunks. The chain is: question → multiple answers → relationships → comparison → chunks.
- **The same question can be good or bad depending on the cognitive process it engages:** if the answer is explicitly taught in the material, it's mere fact recall (bad); if you must explore anatomy/physiology/physics to reason it out, it builds relationships (good). Example: "why is ventilation highest at the base of the lung?" is a good question only if the why isn't given and you must reason it out (Clinic 25).
- Want evaluative, **"chaotic" thinking** — comparing/contrasting back and forth between ideas — not straight-line "why? → because X → done". Do the relating mentally, go back and forth, then express it through chunking. If it feels chaotic and confusing, you are probably doing it right (Clinic 10).
- Worked example (Clinic 27): "why is ptosis / entropion / ectropion important?" — each has several answers (a sign for diagnosis, a consequence if untreated, a need for intervention). Compare these across the three conditions to find a shared reason for importance → chunk them together, rather than three isolated condition lists.
- Hold the reasons mentally as a few keywords — **never full sentences**. Writing them out limits thinking and invites laziness; training the brain to hold them is part of the skill (Clinics 10–12).
- Don't adapt the technique until you've fully mastered it; every change increases inaccuracy (Clinic 27).

**The Question → Think → Process flow**
- Inquiry sits ON TOP of existing note-processing skills. Questions steer the brain involuntarily (ask "what did you have for lunch — don't answer" and you've already thought of it). "Brain, chunk this" is directionless; a question is a lens (Clinics 16, 22).
- Aim questions at chunks, trends, similarities/differences, importance, how things work, real-world applications — and keep them relatively **OPEN** so the brain can survey the material and find many relationships at once. Narrow questions (single fact/process/relationship) can't produce meaningful chunks. Test: "If answered, would this give me one fact, or show me the big picture and how things group?"
- Correct flow: **question → look through material → think/answer in your head (see relationships and groupings) → process directly into non-linear notes** (mind map / chunk map / processed notes). No separate "collecting" step. Never type linear answers underneath the question — it funnels rich relationships into a narrow text channel, kills chunking, and produces messy/oversimplified/borrowed-textbook maps. See [note-taking](note-taking.md).
- **Protect the inquiry process (Clinics 16, 17):** no written answers beneath questions, and NEVER put traffic-light inquiry questions into Anki's scheduler. Linear Q&A has no research support beyond generic generation/output effects — it is no better than doing a past-paper question, and it directly blocks the chunk creation inquiry exists for. The metric is time taken to produce LEARNING, not time taken studying.
- Holding relationships in your head without writing is difficult — that's the point: it trains **cognitive load tolerance** and processing ability, which all later techniques depend on. The discomfort is intrinsic cognitive load from higher-order relational encoding; it gets faster and easier.
- Missed details are fine — later revision, flashcards and future sessions pick them up. Don't let detail inundation slow the scaffold. Inquiry leverages (doesn't replace) visualization, processing, mind mapping and chunk mapping.
- The readiness chain is questions → relationships → chunks → clean non-linear notes → quality COBES; improving question quality alone fails if downstream links are broken ([course-guide](course-guide.md), Technique Training final checkpoint).

**Generating questions from chunks (Clinic 14)**
- Prerequisite: you must already know how the information is chunked and how the chunks relate — the arrows you drew are question seeds. Without that encoding you can't generate questions that are both legitimate and difficult (a novice's "hard" rocket-science question may be nonsense).
- Formula: pick concept X1 from chunk X and Y2 from a RELATED chunk Y → ask **"If X1 is affected/changed, what is the effect on Y2?"** Too easy? Add a variable ("if X1 changes AND Z1 changes, what happens to Y2?") or chain it ("how would that influence Z1?"). You can pick concepts almost randomly as long as their parent chunks are related.
- Evaluate the question: (a) very difficult → work on chunks/inter-chunk relationships first; (b) doesn't make sense → try another combination or fix the chunks; (c) good question → use it.

**Unanswered questions (Clinic 28)**
- Valid reasons to leave an Aim question unanswered: the topic is out of scope for the problem you're solving; the question doesn't make sense; you already know/covered the answer. Questions create potential relationships that may turn out invalid — not all must be answered.
- The requirement: questions must propel you to find MULTIPLE relationships that can be compared to yield chunks. Going back from Shoot to Aim when you discover missed keywords is normal; the switching penalty only matters if you constantly miss "a truckload" of keywords — then Aim more comprehensively up front.

**Practical tips**
- Audit questions you've written: how many are why/how, genuinely unknown and curiosity-sparking vs what/Jeopardy? For each major concept ask "why is this important?" and "how does it relate to everything else?" — like fitting a jigsaw piece into the picture (the piece gets memorised as a by-product of comparison).
- **Monotony is fine:** ~90% of study questions are the standard "why is it important / how is it related". "What's your name?" is the same question for everyone but every answer differs — the thinking work is in answering, not in novel questions. Let occasional curiosity-driven questions branch out; the standard questions carry the rest (Clinics 16, 18).
- Write questions down when starting — it lets you evaluate question quality. Drop the written step ONLY if your chunks, question quality and retention remain good; otherwise keep it (Clinic 20). Train the mentality with the traffic-light drill first.
- Judge question time by the learning happening, not time per question — what would you do instead, and would it learn as much? Expect speed to grow with practice (Clinic 15).
- Creating your own challenge/curveball questions ("think like an examiner") is often more effective than past papers — see [exam-technique](exam-technique.md).

## Inquiry & the Traffic-Light Drill
A beginner drill (not a full study system) for training inquiry-based learning: the light is **RED** by default (stop — no consuming); generating questions you're curious about turns it **GREEN** (go answer them); answering them turns it **RED** again (stop and ask what's next).

**Key principles**
- Red = stop and create curiosity/questions; green = you have questions, so go learn to answer them; answered = red again. The rhythm trains question-creation, curiosity calibration and purposeful consumption.
- It is scaffolding: once the questioning way of thinking is comfortable you no longer need the explicit traffic light. **AIM (Ascent I) is the upgraded version** — same principle, higher question quality; it replaces the traffic-light drill, NOT your processed notes (Clinic 25). BHS replaces it (and basic collect-and-process). If you can't do it comfortably yet, keep drilling — it's the prerequisite for the advanced systems.
- Each cycle connects new information to what you've learned from multiple angles; question volume grows as knowledge grows (2–3 → 5–10+). The broader inquiry cycle (question → seek → express → new questions) and the conditions for curiosity are covered in [learning-science](learning-science.md).

**The inquiry → chunking chain (Clinics 23, 24)**
- The core chain: **question → multiple answers → relationships → comparison → chunks.** It must be strong end-to-end: increasing question quality only improves chunk quality if the chain flows through. A gap (questions don't yield answers/relationships) means better questions won't help — the benefit "leaks out". Build the chain strongly first; optimise it later.
- If you find relationships but not meaningful chunks, your questions are probably too curiosity-driven/off-topic — use more questions about relative importance and how concepts relate; these keep you in scope naturally (Clinic 24).
- Intuitive chunking (grouping by intuition) is fine for simple information but error-prone for new/complex topics — it's only as good as your existing knowledge, and for new topics is more often wrong than right (Clinic 23). See [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md).
- The traffic-light gives fairly good chunks systematically, not necessarily the best — it bridges to advanced inquiry-based chunking at Ascent 1+.

**Question volume by stage (Clinic 15)**
- Traffic-light stage: LOTS of questions is good — it trains correct inquiry.
- Ascent I: clean up quality, drop unnecessary questions, prioritise relational ("how is it related") questions that explore the most meaningful relationships most efficiently. Questioning efficiency is a key Ascent-1 skill; don't carry traffic-light volume into Aim.
- ~100 questions from 10 keywords is realistic, but many get answered as you go — the first 20 take longest; the next 50 partly answer themselves. Don't generate "how is it related" questions for every possible pair when the relationship doesn't make sense.

**Technique / steps**
1. RED: with material in front of you, ask what you're curious about; write questions ("What is a wave?" "Why do I need to know about waves?").
2. GREEN: go into the material and answer exactly those questions; take basic notes in your own words.
3. RED: stop, ask "what now?" (next heading: diffraction → "What is diffraction? How does it relate to waves?").
4. Repeat, letting questions dictate the path through the material.

**Common mistakes**
- Reading on autopilot while the light is red (no active questions).
- Treating the drill as the permanent system instead of training wheels.
- Writing the answer directly beneath each question, or scheduling inquiry questions in Anki (see Question Quality).
- Relying on intuitive chunking for new/complex topics; expecting the drill to give the best chunks.

## Pre-Study
A short upfront step that organises how incoming information will be arranged in your mind — building the "furniture" (categories/groups) before filling the room with facts. Like tapping an arrow at the start of its flight, a small early change massively alters the downstream trajectory. Almost all in-lecture processing problems trace to missing or incorrect pre-study (Clinics 19–21).

**Aim vs pre-study & the system flow**
- Pre-study is a *timing* concept (study done before a main learning event such as a lecture/class); Aim is a *cognitive-order* concept (the first step of encoding, whenever it happens). They overlap but are not identical: **~85% of the time Aim happens inside pre-study** (Clinic 17).
- The full system flow: **pre-study/priming (scaffold of big ideas) → primary learning event (class, lecture or dedicated self-study, maximised by priming) → multiple rounds of spaced, interleaved retrieval** (Clinic 18). See [revision](revision.md).
- Aim should always be your first encoding step. 20-minute pre-study → do as much Aim as fits, possibly a hybrid mini collect/process during class, complete Aim after class, then Shoot, then Skin. Longer pre-study → full Aim plus part of Shoot. With only 5–10 minutes, use a lighter priming activity instead of a full Aim. No pre-study (thrown into class) → your in-class notes become just one resource to scrape for a proper Aim afterwards; skipping Aim leaves gaps and costs more time correcting structures.
- Priming-only pre-study should never take days — it works with the biggest ideas, major chunks and key relationships only. Once your technique outpaces the cohort, self-studying the entire topic before class is fine (even over days) and you may skip the lecture — but keep a dedicated priming step even on the same day (Justin: 15–20 min scoping → primary event ~5 min later) (Clinic 18).
- If attendance is mandatory, don't pre-study to 100% — you'd waste the 4–5 hours/day you must spend in class; position yourself so class time is maximally beneficial. If optional, self-paced full study may be better (fill blanks with recordings, office hours, other resources). Always pre-study if you can; going in cold is not optimal. In-class optimisation (lecture as revision, leaving details for class, skipping where allowed, earbuds in mandatory classes) lives in [exam-technique](exam-technique.md).
- Pre-study is decoupled from the class's order and pace: get the official learning objectives, use the textbook/syllabus, and study in YOUR order based on your chunking/relationships — even if the teacher provides slides. Course students routinely cover in a weekend what class takes 6 weeks to do (~20× pace). Separating from class pace is a skill you'll need anyway: after school, and as a professional, there is no class order (Clinic 15).

**Purpose, win condition & ROI**
- Purpose is NOT to learn facts, equations, quotes or lists — it's to see where the big groups of information are and how they might relate. Categories evolve as you learn; they don't need to be right, just attempted.
- **Win condition: big chunks + good inter-chunk relationships. "Nothing else matters."** Full-depth understanding is NOT needed to enable later higher-order learning. If pre-study takes too long, you're over-focusing on details/memorisation/understanding (Clinic 14).
- Set the session goal from its function: pre-study success = good-quality inquiry → big quality chunks → an overarching scaffold for the whole topic with meaningful, logical (ideally intuitive) relationships between the broadest chunks. NOT "complete the mind map" — that is a superficial completion goal (Clinic 16).
- Pre-study is **chunk-focused, not detail-focused**. With limited time, do less detail + more chunking, never the reverse: even 2 minutes of big chunks + basic relationships beats reading details (Clinics 19–21).
- Room/furniture analogy: disorganised knowledge is a pile on the floor — slow to search, hard to relate, discarded as useless. Organised knowledge (drawers/wardrobes) increases retention, recall speed and usable results.
- Rule of thumb: **1 hour of good pre-study saves at least 10 hours later** (often more); it can give a ~400% benefit. "Wasted" minutes on keywords that turn out not to be taught still help you understand what is. Priming gives info "a place to belong", drastically shifting the forgetting-curve slope and raising retention; it sparks curiosity-driven learning. Never de-prioritise it even when busy.
- Doing it imperfectly beats theorising (photography-class study: the group graded on quantity produced higher-quality photos than the group chasing one perfect photo — real trial-and-error beats untested theories; feed failures into COBES).
- The brain can start at a higher level and automatically fill gaps — you don't need the lower layers first; just try and make mistakes now (Clinic 27).

**Procedure: Scope → Keywords → Groups → Frame**
1. **Scope:** skim the lecture fast (double speed), or use slides/objectives/a provided concept list, or run the recording through speech-to-text and skim the text. A 5–6 minute ultra-quick skim can set the topic boundaries (Clinic 27).
2. **Triage resources:** dense "textbook" (deep/technical, for later) vs summary/course guide (main ideas/relationships, for now).
3. **Collect keywords** for the topic — NOT from chapter/sub-chapter headings (framing bias). Use bolded words, important diagrams, process names, and key explanatory paragraphs turned into keywords; collect Aim targets from any terminology that sounds like a concept (Clinic 21). ~30 obvious ones in 15 min beats 60 in 60 min; missing a few doesn't matter (Clinic 28).
4. **Form intuitive groups** (hypotheses, not final) — compare ideas, note similarities/differences. AIM "organise keywords" means only the most obvious, basic, largest categories — no real thinking required; with ~15 keywords you can skip it or make 2 loose groups (~3 headings is fine). You'll reorganise during Aim questions anyway; never commit to the initial groups or pre-chunk further before answering questions (pre-chunking risk) (Clinics 24, 30).
5. **Unknown terms:** search the densest resource, then the guide, then Google Images first (diagrams give the gist fast); open 1–2 tabs; get only a superficial understanding sufficient to group. Skip terminology freely — give an unknown term 10–15 seconds, note "seems related to X", move on (Clinic 28). Research keywords only enough to grasp main ideas and connections, not details; the time pressure keeps you out of details (Clinic 24).
6. **Label groups** with simple, intuitive keywords; map basic relationships non-linearly (e.g. Concepts app). Reorganise as relationships emerge; subgroup large groups. Ideally frame it as a mind map: it serves as a later reference and tracks your thinking — framing 4–5 ideas means evaluating 20–30 ideas and ~100 relationships, and you may end with only ~12 words on the map (Clinics 19–21). You can keep adding to this pre-study map later so it becomes your notes (messy early; you redo less as you improve) (Clinic 27).

**Resourcefulness & no-material situations**
- Pre-study is essentially always possible to at least a basic level (even 3 keywords + a relationship). If it seems impossible, be resourceful: Google the curriculum, find publicly downloadable learning-objective PDFs, use the textbook/study guide/Wikipedia, or ask the teacher (Clinics 15, 19, 21).
- No reading material (online/video courses): either **DELAY** (wait for slides, study in batch retrospect) or **DEAL WITH IT** (find the usually-public learning objectives, Google the main ideas, build a frame, fill later). Video-only: skim for headings, check the summary below the video. Pure talking-head with no cues: you can't pre-study — capture everything and process/chunk in retrospect (Clinic 22).
- If you truly can't pre-study, frame during the lecture introduction (requires higher skill) (Clinic 19).
- **Blind mapping** — hypothesising a map from a keyword list with NO material, using only logic and prior knowledge — is a low-effort curiosity/pre-study variant; the deliberately wrong map powers targeted, high-relevance learning. Full technique in [note-taking](note-taking.md) (Clinic 40).
- For languages, use the learning objectives to hypothesise relevant dialogues/vocabulary; aim to be good at the domain, not to game the assessment (Clinic 21). For USMLE/question-bank-only study, get the domain guidebooks and pre-study the topics covered (Clinic 21).
- Self-directed learners (autodidacts) don't need a formal curriculum or objectives — you control the purpose; scout resources and extract the relevant learnings ([professional-learning](professional-learning.md), Clinic 23).

**Time-box & speed: the accuracy-over-speed exception**
- **Time-box to ~20 minutes (20–25; roughly a quarter to a third of the lecture time). It "will take" ~20 min, not "should" — there is no option to go longer; never 2 hours.** Exceeding the prescribed time means you're not following the technique (paint-the-house-in-4-hours mindset; treat the limit as non-negotiable stakes — the "hostage" analogy) (Clinics 22, 25–27).
- Pre-study is the **SINGLE technique where deliberate speed improves quality**: the strict limit forces ruthless prioritisation of main ideas, forces out extraneous cognitive processes (getting stuck in details) and increases correctness. Everywhere else accuracy comes first and speed follows naturally — see [habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md) and [course-guide](course-guide.md) (Clinics 22, 24).
- "Study 50 pages in 20 minutes" — impossible to focus on details, so you're forced to main ideas (Clinic 26). 85–90% accuracy is achievable in 20 min (sometimes 5); you can rearrange later, but aim not to need to (Clinic 27).
- A slightly-wrong superficial structure is better than a detailed wrong one; if uncertain, make it **thinner** (more superficial) but still broad (Clinic 27).
- Guideline: ≤~15 min per 1 h of content (45–60 min for 5 h). Expert ~10 min; student ~20–30 min on a large topic; up to ~1 h for a week's content. Don't go deep on every word — taking too long means you're sucked into details (a technique issue).
- **Whole book ≤45 min:** gather ALL keywords (don't separate by chapter), then chunk into ~4 main chunks → sub-chunks of ~4–5 keywords; build a solid skeleton. First keywords take 3–4 min each, later ones ~30 sec (it repeats; you see other keywords as you go) (Clinic 22).
- **Batch-studying exception:** advanced students covering 1–2 weeks in a weekend can reconsolidate/rechunk/reorganise unexpected additions, even rebuilding a map from memory as interleaving (Clinic 26).
- **Thin & wide under time pressure:** keep the frame within its ~20-min limit; with e.g. 5 hours across Mon–Fri for 5 topics, hour 1 = pre-study/frame ALL 5, day 2 = add detail to all, day 3 = more detail, etc. Prioritise breadth and accuracy of each step over depth on one topic — going narrow/deep with limited time risks learning the wrong technique (Clinic 27). Scheduling in [time-management](time-management.md).
- Push to the maximum discomfort you can tolerate: if relationships/chunking in pre-study are too hard, defer them to a later study stage — but tolerance for discomfort predicts growth speed (Clinic 15).

**Topic-wide scope & scheduling**
- Skim the *entire* upcoming topic (maybe weeks of material), not lecture-by-lecture, to avoid "islands". Isolate ~3–4 main ideas/groups and think about how they relate/flow (A enables B → causes C → D) — a basic backbone. Any topic breaks into roughly 3–4 main ideas. A topic ≈ a curriculum unit (4–5 weeks school, 4–5 days uni); concepts within relate strongly, across topics less so.
- Get to topic-wide pre-study as quickly as possible — it frames everything across lectures and prevents the isolation problem at the source. Only make isolated per-lecture maps if the lecture is truly standalone OR your skill can't yet do topic-wide; but topic-wide pre-study (main ideas only) is possible at ANY skill level. Scaling up (e.g. Aim/Shoot across 4–5 chapters at once): just do it and COBES it to find out (Clinics 19–21).
- **Schedule:** weekend ~30 min topic-wide pre-study of the whole week's main ideas + relationships (a big sprawling frame); night before each lecture ~20 min to branch into that lecture's specifics and link it to the topic frame. Batch Layers 1–2 topic-wide so you spend more time on Layers 3–4 (Clinics 19–21). See [time-management](time-management.md) and [learning-science](learning-science.md) for layers.
- **Already have isolated maps?** Start a blank-canvas brain dump: list 4–5 main ideas across the whole topic from scratch (don't reference the existing maps first), reframe, then fit the old maps in. Reframing from the existing isolated maps keeps you trapped in those boxes (Clinics 19–21).
- Example: microbiology — separate Staphylococcus/Streptococcus maps repeat similar work per microbe; a topic-wide frame lets you chunk shared virulence/disease/identification structure across microbes at once (Clinics 19–21). Per-lecture maps merged later are NOT equivalent to topic-wide framing (baking analogy: mixing after the oven is not mixing before) — see [note-taking](note-taking.md) (Clinic 30).
- ~30–60 min for a topic that might normally take ~12 h; no memorisation needed. Carry the frame into class so new info has anchor points. 15 minutes before a lecture identifying 4 main ideas + relationships makes the lecture "pretty easy" to engage with; even a rocket-science PhD with zero prior knowledge can extract main ideas and guess relationships in ~20 minutes (Clinics 19–21).

**Depth, density & video lectures**
- You need only a tiny fraction of full understanding to see how concepts relate (30 minutes across 90 pages may yield five words and a few arrows — yet you've learned how to navigate the topic). Isolating main concepts FIRST activates higher-order learning early; reading everything to understand it first is passive and you won't remember half of it.
- **Logic-layer stability:** with correct broad/superficial pre-study + Aim (wide relationships, shared importance, clear backbone), the frame should be ~80–85% accurate. Major later changes signal faults: too detailed too early, questions not broad enough, scope too narrow, or pre-studying too long (Clinics 28, 29).
- A very superficial 20–30 min pre-study of 2–3 chapters (main ideas only) can cut total study time ~10–15% immediately and free time for interleaved retrieval (Clinic 28).
- **Broad pre-study across the whole paper** with a higher-order lens (Clinic 42): advanced subjects get *deeper, not wider*, so a whole paper may hinge on only 3–4 concepts (~20–30 min, time made back anyway). Flick through for 5–10 min to gauge conceptual density (70 pages in with only ~6 keywords → little conceptual difficulty). Maths chapter names often ARE the concepts ("Riemann integration") — Googling the heading is itself pre-study; a big-picture visualisation (e.g. 3Blue1Brown's ~12-video calculus playlist) covers a syllabus in 1–2 hours. Then layer: main concepts → detail → finer detail.
- **Video/online lectures (Clinic 33):** if slides/objectives/outline/course guide exist, pre-study from those — you don't need the video. Otherwise scrub (hold the seek bar, listen to ~5-second pockets; a 1-hour lecture gist-ed in 3–4 min) or generate a transcript to skim (can be done in bulk). Double speed saves less than it seems — active processing means constant pausing/rewinding/cross-checking; the bottleneck is *thinking quality*, not information-in speed. With good core techniques you could listen at 0.25x and still outperform everyone. Justin's entire master's was online lectures processed this way.

**Common mistakes**
- Treating pre-study as learning all the details; going too deep too early (then a professor skipping ahead "catches you out" because your power came from pre-learned details).
- Skipping it as "a waste of time", because you're behind, or because "the teacher didn't send topics / I can't predict it".
- Pre-studying lecture-by-lecture; building keyword lists from headings; copying someone else's groups; separating keywords by chapter instead of gathering then chunking.
- Making the pre-study mind map a full copy of the topic with facts/examples/specific explanations (Clinic 15).
- Taking longer than the limit because "I can't do it in 20 min" — forcing the pace is the whole point. The long time IS the error, not the cause.
- **Min-maxing relevance:** over-optimising what to skip wastes more time than just learning it (Clinics 19–21).
- **Using short summaries as pre-study:** vague, high-level, not the taught technique (Clinics 19–21).
- Too many points coming off one central node (e.g. 7 off "ventilation") — consider removing the central theme, naming the file by the topic, and working directly with main chunks for a more authentic backbone (Clinic 27).
- Waiting for teacher resources or matching the class sequence before pre-studying (Clinic 15).
- Where to start Aiming: anywhere — the first keyword, the most familiar one, or the top; it all goes back and forth anyway (Clinic 28).

## Barriers to Aiming
**"It takes too long" (studying ≠ learning)**
- The most common objection to BHS/Aim is that it delays "real studying". The rebuttal: studying (the activity) ≠ learning (retained, usable knowledge), and the Aim step *is itself learning* — its first part, not planning for learning.
- Reading, rewriting notes, attending class = studying. If you can't recall and apply the knowledge fluently, meaningful learning didn't happen and the time was wasted. Calibrate on learning outcomes, not pages flicked / words seen / notes written / classes attended. **"Studying without learning is a waste of time"** — if you are not learning effectively while studying, do something else; wasting time on fun beats wasting time on fake study (Clinics 16, 18).
- Distinguish "not driven to study at all" (a procrastination issue — [habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md)) from "not driven to answer your questions" (a purpose/understanding issue: you haven't internalised that questions exist to create chunks and learning) (Clinic 16).
- Turning keywords into curiosity-generating questions makes the brain a sponge — attention spikes when the curiosity is filled. That process IS learning, like turning on the engine is part of driving (hours behind the wheel with the engine off get you nowhere).
- **Judge time by learning returned across the whole timescale of the goal, not session length** (the drill-bit analogy: nobody wants a drill bit, they want the hole → screw → picture → feeling). 5 hours of pre-study that saves 10 hours over the next month is a net 5-hour win; Justin's 1.5 hours of setup saved 30–40 hours across two months. "Taking too long" must be defined: too long per session, or too long to reach the goal? Only the latter matters. Full treatment in [time-management](time-management.md) (Clinics 14, 15).
- Skill-acquisition time is normal (you wouldn't expect violin proficiency after a few hours). Relative to other skills this is fast; relative to mindless studying it feels slow — but you can "study" 20 hours the old way before learning anything. If doing more of what you already do worked, you wouldn't be stressed or stuck.
- Conscious incompetence (knowing you lack the skill) is one step above the note-writing friends' unconscious incompetence — you are ahead of them, not behind. See [mindset-emotion](mindset-emotion.md).

**"Why create questions when past papers exist?" (curiosity as a gap radar)**
- Creating your own questions is a core learning skill, and curiosity is the brain's radar for knowledge gaps — self-generated, connected questions target your weakest points far more efficiently than past papers.
- Exam style always changes; high-mark curveball questions are increasingly common as assessors shift to catch past-paper memorisers. Past papers give only a general ballpark (a 20% chance of unseen question types is a big bet when aiming for the top 1%). Everyone uses past papers — a universal strategy cannot differentiate you.
- Question-creation is itself a skill; outsourcing it to past papers means the brain never develops it (sheltered-life/butler analogy). University has fewer, less reliable past papers — without the skill you'll struggle.
- Questions you already know the answer to teach little (mild reinforcement); maximum learning per unit time comes from genuinely unknown questions. Getting everything right on a past paper after two hours of study is suspicious — the point is to find gaps.
- Curiosity = the brain's radar for weak points. Self-made, highly connected questions are by construction your weak points, and one question revises multiple linked points simultaneously → a full hour purely on weaknesses, vs perhaps 1–2 isolated gaps per hour of past papers. Use past papers for a general feel of assessment style, not as your primary question bank. See [exam-technique](exam-technique.md).

**Rabbit holes & research loops (Clinics 16–18)**
- Getting lost researching answers or fitting concepts into a map is usually a technique/priority issue, not a "too many questions" issue. The win condition: after the time spent, is encoding of the studied material good? If yes, fine-tune the rest.
- Going back and forth between concepts while fitting them into a pre-study map is normal and part of learning; with experience you reuse organisational patterns (shared knowledge schemas) across subjects.
- Searching while priming is fine; but if answering one question requires learning an entire new irrelevant concept, that path may not be worth it — nuance, not a blanket rule.
- There are too many possible causes of research loops to prescribe fixes blind: run COPE cycles, experiment, then submit work for feedback — faster than describing the problem in text. Don't arbitrarily cap questions or time before examining the actual process, and don't stop relevant deep-dives prematurely. Pre-study rabbit holes (e.g. "where does post-modernism fit?") are expected at fundamentals; improvement comes naturally.

**Boredom & lost curiosity (Clinic 12)**
- Boredom during Aim–Shoot is almost always a sign that **attention is not on creating chunks and inter-chunk relationships** — the act of building the simplest, most organised chunk structure is naturally stimulating even without prior curiosity. First diagnosis: are you focusing on high-quality chunks and clear relationships? If your standard for chunk simplicity is too low, the process degenerates into boring coverage. Second diagnosis: you may simply be tired (energy, not technique). See [focus-attention](focus-attention.md).
- Hold yourself to the **simplicity standard**: don't move on until the structure is as simple as the topic can possibly be expressed. Moving on halfway through chunk creation produces a tangled mess that worsens with each redo. The instructor's demos look superficial in information gathered because he is razor-focused on why-it's-important/how-it's-retrieved and refuses to move on until the chunks exist.
- For already-studied topics: look for what you don't know and focus on chunks/inter-chunk relationships; the simplification challenge itself generates engagement. Pre-study should always include chunking — "you should always be chunking all the time".
- **"I can't find the material/information"** is usually not a resource problem — it's not yet knowing WHAT information is needed to create the chunks. Compare your attempts with spot-the-issue demonstrations and other students' work; with experience you realise very little information is actually needed.

**The slow start, inquiry discomfort & "confusion is fuel"**
- Relationship-based learning is slow and effortful at first, then accelerates dramatically as the knowledge web grows ("same as that, just a different example"). Most people quit in the slow phase and miss the exponential payoff — the real loss is the opportunity to ride the efficient curve (loss aversion working against you). See [learning-science](learning-science.md).
- The anxiety, chaos and "what if I miss something / I'm going out of order" feeling when starting inquiry is normal and necessary — **if it feels chaotic and confusing, you are probably doing it right**. Inquiry is a mode most students have never experienced; there is no way to get used to it except going through it (Clinic 10).
- Students asking for "more concrete steps" actually want security and certainty — for the discomfort to go away. The steps already exist (numbered steps, video walkthroughs, dissections of others' attempts); it does not get more concrete. The discomfort only resolves through acceptance and tolerance, and eventually becomes a usable energy — **"harness the energy of confusion"** — and one of your biggest advantages over other learners. Tolerating it is one of the most fundamental requirements of a high-level learner; certain efficiency levels are unreachable without the discomfort-involving techniques. This is the real "short-term pain" of short-term pain / long-term gain: mentally and emotionally challenging work, not long hours (Clinic 10). See [mindset-emotion](mindset-emotion.md).
- Post-Aim overwhelm/confusion is normal and useful: it is fuel — the brain is keen to resolve the primed questions, an ideal state before consuming information in Shoot. Pushing through produces a liberating, confidence-building feeling of flying through topics.
- Reframe panic ("this is taking too long") as anticipation of the accelerating curve rather than a reason to revert to passive studying. Expect the first pass (especially with no prior knowledge, e.g. maths) to be very slow; speed comes quickly afterwards.

## Scoping: Topic Size & Out-of-Scope Learning
**The topic-size sweet spot**
- Aim efficiency follows an inverted-U against the amount of information scoped: too little → too few possible relationships; too much → overwhelming and slow. Aim the largest topic you can tolerate — usually an entire topic or area of study, in one go.
- Don't Aim a fragment (e.g. just "vessels" within the blood system) — not enough material to work with. Even total beginners can usually handle a whole topic; capacity grows with skill. Doing a whole area of study at once (e.g. VCE Chemistry AOS1: acids & bases, properties of water, redox, aqueous solutions, water as solvent) maximises cross-topic relationships.
- A topic is a dense web of inter-related concepts; **where that density drops off is a natural topic boundary**. Decide boundaries with a very quick 5–6 min skim: "this is getting less relevant to the earlier stuff → new topic". Biology vs chemistry: each is a dense internal web with sparser links between → separable topics; an organ system may be one dense topic or two (Clinic 27).

**Narrow scope syndrome & technique capacity (Clinics 26, 27, 28, 30)**
- Danger zone = a single lecture/class (too little information to form chunks and relationships — counterintuitively, bigger topics are EASIER, not harder). Upper bound = cognitive overload. Going too broad is rarely the problem; going too narrow is. **2–3 lectures at once is already okay; 4–5 may be ideal.** A decent default is one chapter (~50–100 pages); split a huge chapter along sensible lines if needed.
- Some technique aspects can only be practised with a near-overwhelming volume; too-small scope may mean you never learn the technique correctly (Clinic 26).
- Stay within your **technique capacity**: find the scope where your technique is near-perfect (e.g. 1/3 of a book), then stretch modestly (e.g. half) — don't triple it. Aim for ~4–5 diagnosable mistakes per attempt, not 40–50. Extend capacity step by step; wait until advanced before pushing scope much bigger.
- If a topic is genuinely huge, break into the largest manageable sections, or use a **backbone map + sub-maps**, or split where connections are superficial. For huge maps prefer one resource with a backbone you extend; only split if it becomes exorbitantly massive (Clinic 27).
- High school: study the entire topic in one go (~4–5 weeks of school content; a university topic ≈ 4–5 days) — the school pace is slow, not the topic big. A map with a good backbone but too little content cannot be fully assessed — bring a bigger topic before asking "am I ready to move on?" (Clinic 28). Batch/bulk studying the whole topic also frees time for extracurriculars ([time-management](time-management.md)).

**Learning slightly out of scope (Clinics 23, 24)**
- Deliberately going a little (~10%) beyond the curriculum when curiosity leads there strengthens in-scope knowledge by adding connection directions, and is faster overall than staying strictly in scope and relearning forgotten material. Many curricula are poorly designed and omit things that would make in-scope concepts much easier.
- At the fringes (the hardest, most specific points), staying in scope limits you to one or two connection angles; a small out-of-scope excursion opens more angles → better retention → less relearning. **10 minutes out of scope can save 3 hours** of struggling with a complex concept; with skill you develop a feel for when it's appropriate.
- **Guardrail: exams must legally test the learning objectives** — assessments are built from them. Let objectives guide you; if a question is way out of scope, refer to them and ask a different question that helps understand the objective. Track time — 5–10 minutes out of scope to anchor an in-scope point is worth it; one hour to consolidate a single point is not. Rigid teacher cutoffs ("that's not examinable") are unhelpful.
- Example: chemistry curiosity "how do I use this reaction to defuse a bomb?" — interesting but almost certainly unassessed; acknowledge it and steer back rather than pursuing it deeply.
- The curriculum's order is not necessarily the most efficient way for YOU to learn the material. Trust the system through the uncomfortable early stages; results become self-evident after the threshold transition (Clinic 23).

## Efficient Sourcing & AI
Source answers fast and use AI to do the *finding/searching* — never the *thinking* (Clinic 41).

**Key principles**
- **30-second rule:** if you can't get the answer from one source within ~30 seconds, flick to another.
- **Source order:** school resources/textbook first (some textbooks are over-simplified "work manuals") → Google for straightforward terms → ChatGPT when Google gives 40 confusing results (use it to get a sense, then make a better, more specific Google search).
- AI does the FINDING, not the THINKING. Your guiding star is a clear question about what you need to know. Using AI to scope possible relationships is fine — but YOU must judge validity, appropriateness and importance. The relational decision-making in your head is the valuable cognitive load you must not skip.
- AI output is "just data/information," like the initial material you'd be taught; the learning is in your relational decision-making.

**Technique / steps**
1. Start at the textbook/school resource; give it ~30 seconds.
2. If no answer, jump to Google (simple term) or ChatGPT (confusing/complex).
3. Ask a narrow, clear question (e.g. "what transformation does this equation allow / in what context is it useful?").
4. Skim to verify it answered YOUR question; if off, refine the prompt ("you answered X, but I'm asking Y") or switch source; read fully if good, then Google further based on the new knowledge.
5. Do the judging/prioritising yourself; save a reusable prompt template and swap in the concept/equation each time.

**Conflicting sources (Clinic 15)**
- When sources conflict, first **validate source credibility**, then **resolve the conflict yourself BEFORE learning either version** — never absorb uncertain information randomly. Learning incorrect and correct versions simultaneously causes confusion that blocks progress.
- Credibility hierarchy: Reddit posts = grain of salt; unvalidated YouTube channels may be wrong; Wikipedia is honestly pretty good most of the time (still with a grain of salt). Credible sources are rarely inaccurate; most "conflicting information" problems trace back to unvalidated low-quality sources.
- If you detect a conflict, it is YOUR job to determine the truth — that investigation is part of studying. With a single credible source you wouldn't even know a conflict exists; conflict detection is a signal to investigate, not to freeze.
- At advanced/professional level, judge conflicting information by testing how it fits your knowledge network rather than adjudicating "which source is true" — network thinking in [learning-science](learning-science.md) (Clinic 40).

**Common mistakes:** using ChatGPT to offload your thinking; accepting AI-suggested relationships without judging validity/importance; reading 40 Google results / going past page one instead of switching source; skipping source validation and treating all sources as equal.

## Subject-Specific Aim Examples
The process is identical across subjects and curricula (VCE, IB, HSC, WACE, etc.): **scope → order control → compare/evaluate/prioritise → hypothesise the scaffold**; only surface execution varies. Know *why* each technique works so you can adapt it — be the engineer designing the system, not just the operator. Sciences adapt easily; English, languages and maths need variations of differing size, all following the same principle. **History/arts are among the EASIEST subjects** — every event has a known precipitating reason, so information comes pre-packaged for grouping, and proportional speed gains are greater; sciences require independent reasoning to find the why/group, and terminology weighs you down. **Coding is largely procedural** → interleaved retrieval is the core; languages/proficiency tests are heavily interleaving-based (Clinics 16, 18).

**Biology (circulation; asexual reproduction)**
- The resource bank is irrelevant (summary sheets, textbooks, course guides, slides, others' notes — ~95% coverage is fine; the missing few percent is picked up later). Group keywords under **self-made** headings as you collect (don't just copy the textbook's).
- "What is it?" is answered at collection time for *recognition only* (glance at the definition so the word isn't random — never memorise). Enough to judge whether a relationship question makes sense.
- Ask why-important and how-related for keywords AND headings. "How does it relate" has many valid variations: compare/contrast (arteries vs veins vs capillaries), structure–function (how does vessel structure allow exchange), efficiency comparisons, and simple "why?" (why do some types have high failure rates?).
- **Shotgun priming:** generate many potential relationships; some will be wrong ("dud" questions) — fine. Primed connections form faster during Shoot even when some hypotheses fail.
- Examples: how are ventricles related to valves? how does double circulation relate to the heart, vessels and blood pressure? how is fission different from budding/fragmentation/spore formation? how is cloning related to vegetative propagation?

**Chemistry (whole VCE AOS1)**
- Whole area of study in one go (information sweet spot). The keyword list can include full reactions/equations: flag them as likely flashcard items later, but keep them on the list for reference and questioning — costs no extra time.
- Underline the bigger main concepts (e.g. solubility) over sub-points; ask why-important for main headings and subheadings alike. "What is it" = recognition only (Brønsted–Lowry must not be a random word); never memorise definitions here.
- ~30–60 minutes for the whole AOS even ~10 years out of practice; by the end you're curious about many things and relationships are primed ("bing, bing, bing"). Overwhelm from the huge question list is normal — confusion is fuel.
- Examples: how is Brønsted–Lowry related to pH calculation? monoprotic vs diprotic vs triprotic? acid-base vs redox? heat capacity vs latent heat? structure of water → heat capacity? solvent–solute interactions → ion–dipole attractions?

**Anatomy (deep dive; memorisation-heavy)**
- Identical process, but be more decisive and selective — choose chunk structures strategically to minimise what must be rote-memorised (anything that can't be integrated must be memorised). Multiple integration routes exist; pick the most strategic (no inherently "correct" chunking). See [professional-learning](professional-learning.md) and [memorisation](memorisation.md). Beginners: start with flashcards + interleaving; the advanced integration techniques are difficult and come later in the course (Clinic 18).
- Chunk patterns repeat heavily across regions; new terms usually just name new parts ("this knob vs that knob"), so cognitive load per keyword stays low despite high counts. **Semi-Aim while scanning:** collect keywords asking "why is this important / how could these integrate?", parking question marks; defer dense labelled diagrams until a higher-level schema exists. Flick through the whole chapter first (seconds) for order control.
- **Leverage comparisons:** upper vs lower limb (mobility gained by sacrificing stability) → hypothesise looser joint capsules, wider range, less bony encasement → every detail becomes hypothesis-testing. Bone is dynamic tissue responding to forces: a tuberosity marks a muscle attachment (deltoid tuberosity predicted as the deltoid insertion on the lateral upper-third humerus); ask "what formed this feature / what travels here?" instead of memorising names.
- **Integrate across systems as you go** (bones ↔ muscles ↔ tendons/ligaments ↔ blood supply ↔ nervous supply) — order control, not "all bones first, then muscles". Integration frameworks per region: (1) surrounding related anatomy; (2) embryological origin (Google ~5–6 min/region); (3) disease/clinical presentation (often highest yield); (4) examination; (5) management/surgery.
- **Dense regions (forearm/hand):** simple structures (anterior/posterior, medial/lateral) fail because the hard details (origins/insertions, nerve branchings, myotomes/dermatomes, vascular supply) end up in the leaves. Choose structures that capture the difficult information — e.g. chunk by characteristic clinical presentations (wrist laceration, swollen elbow, crushed hand, dislocated finger — a few, not hundreds).
- **Landmark strategy:** deeply integrate the most forgettable/complex parts first → they become memorable landmarks; surrounding parts link and infer between them; remaining isolated gaps use memorisation techniques. Full integration of everything isn't time-feasible pre-clinically — pockets of integration are the goal; it snowballs and gets easier during revision.
- Time: integration can double/triple a session (1–2 h/page) but beats passive 15–20 min + separate passes + an hour to consolidate + forgetting half + an hour relearning. Tools: 3D models (BioDigital Human, ZygoteBody), cadaver labs; use clinical-correlate pages (e.g. Moore's) to seed chunk structures.

**Law (open-book) & other linear subjects (Clinics 18, 23, 30)**
- Law is one of the EASIEST subjects for these techniques: you don't need to memorise cases — you need a logic structure and conceptual framework telling you WHAT to look up WHEN. Habitual rote memorisation of case details is largely wasted ("memorising the open book so you can look things up faster").
- Encode per case: the main point/importance, how it relates conceptually to everything else, which argument(s) it supports — not every detail. All law resources are linear, so study becomes linear (cognitively detrimental); restructure anyway — difficulty finding the way is not evidence it doesn't exist.
- Law, history, politics, economics need very little memorisation technique — pure high-efficiency encoding nearly suffices. Sciences need compromise: ~80% from encoding, remaining ~20% still requires flashcards/memorisation layered on.
- **Encode for yourself first, align to assessment later:** priority #1 is deep understanding in the way that makes sense to YOU; align to the assessment/curriculum structure afterwards via interleaved retrieval, not the reverse. For rigid fields with established frameworks (law, education), simplify/re-chunk into your own schema, remember the differences, then remap to the original structures in ~1–2 retrieval sessions (Clinic 23).
- **Institutional templates** (e.g. an attending's syncope diagnosis schema): encode the knowledge YOUR optimal way (fluid knowledge retrieves in many ways), then spend ~15 minutes using their template as retrieval practice. Never encode into the institution's format to please others — that means learning sub-par, forgetting, and relearning yearly. A grouped list is not a chunk map (Clinic 18).

**Reading research papers & unstructured resources (Clinics 22, 29, 35)**
- A research paper is just another information source: apply the same pre-study → layers technique. If domain knowledge is low, build a broad baseline FIRST (systematic reviews, commentaries, books, overview articles) instead of starting with one specific paper.
- Starting inside one paper's constraints is framing bias — look across multiple papers at once for the pre-study. If a detailed version exists, a more basic version exists somewhere; find it. Research produces an ever-growing web, not necessarily one ever-growing mind map — split maps as useful. Struggling with theoretical papers usually indicates a weakness in an earlier technique step, not a special "research reading" problem.
- Journal articles aren't presented for direct study, so even creating a basic frame can take >1 hour: gather many relevant articles at once (~20 open), frame the overlapping themes across the collection, then fill in with your normal techniques; for literature reviews write the synthesis directly as you read. Full workflow in [professional-learning](professional-learning.md) (Clinic 22).
- For unstructured resources with no curriculum/order/objectives (books, Google, courses), you control all direction: start from relevance, skim for curiosity or a link to prior knowledge, hypothesise keywords, build a disposable mini mind map, repeat passes, then scope other resources against a specific gap. The full relevance-first cycle (three relevance types, the 0→2→8→32 snowball, hypercorrection) lives in [learning-science](learning-science.md) (Clinic 35).

## English & Languages Aim
English/languages split into **basic competency** (vocabulary, grammar, syntax) and **advanced competency** (analytic: analysing others' techniques, themes, character development; expressive: applying those techniques in your own persuasive/creative writing). Because English assesses *skill application* (e.g. essay writing) rather than only concept knowledge, BHS needs deliberate variations.

**Competency model & time**
- Bloom's mapping: basic = memorise/understand; analytic = apply/analyse (identify techniques in others' texts, compare and justify why one suits a theme better); expressive = create (choose and justify techniques for new situations — top level).
- **Fixed vs improvable time:** reading the texts, essay-writing/analysis skill development, and (in sciences) rote memorisation are relatively fixed; concept/text understanding is where BHS saves time. Speed up the improvable parts to free time for the fixed parts → less stress.
- Two BHS objectives for English: (1) speed up the variable parts — deep, interconnected text understanding so ~6 practice essays deliver the benefit of ~100, consistently; (2) practise so the skills keep improving — reliable results against curveball questions instead of luck-based memorisation of one essay per prompt. New-language learners start at basic competency; analytic/expressive work is out of reach until relatively fluent (you loop back for advanced/technical vocabulary even after fluency).

**Languages — dialogue scripting**
- Instead of abstract questions, script dialogues or pieces of writing you genuinely want to be able to produce, at **comprehensible-input** level, plus variations — these become your curiosity-driven "questions". Shoot is learning in order to fill the Aim. (Comprehensible-input hypothesis: learn best just above current competence — see [learning-science](learning-science.md).)
- Steps: (1) pick something you genuinely want to say/read (an anime dialogue, a favourite author's manuscript, a shop conversation) — genuine interest is the essential criterion; (2) script the target dialogue in English (if you could write it in the target language you wouldn't need to learn it); (3) check it sits in the comprehensible-input range (some parts known, some not — all unknown → too challenging, all known → too easy); (4) write variations (different replies/responses/interlocutors) — each variation is effectively a question; (5) Shoot: learn the words/grammar/syntax needed to produce the dialogue and variations.
- The real question is "can I say this thing I care about?" — not "what is the word for X" (those mean nothing to you). Works for advanced vocabulary/grammar refinement in a fluent language too (same model, harder input). Examples: Japanese anime dialogue with varied replies; Mandarin shopkeeper ice-cream dialogue scripted in English.
- Keep the Aim/Shoot cycle running on comprehensible input and **chunk vocabulary by use case** as you go (e.g. "that's okay / alright / all good" as one chunk) — use-case chunking and the language Skin variant live in [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md) (Clinic 10). Some rote memorisation is legitimate in languages; more than in other subjects.

**English text response — theme-first analysis**
- Run the Aim questions along a theme-first chain: **author background → themes (why important to the author) → techniques → examples**. Understanding must flow cause→effect, not effect→cause.
- Techniques exist to demonstrate themes; memorising technique/example lists without themes is a random, obscure mess. The technique layer itself needs ~50% memorisation (what each technique is and does, e.g. repetition → emphasis) — learn the teacher's technique list.
- Direction matters: start from themes and ask why they're important (author/director's life, background, perspective, literary influences — often on their Wikipedia page), then relate techniques to themes. Don't learn techniques first and retrospectively pin them onto themes.
- **Bystander analogy:** weak essays list actions then justify each with a theme (effect→cause); strong essays state the themes first (responsibility, limits of confidence, moral obligation) and derive actions from them (cause→effect) — this is why model essays feel clean.
- Expressive = you are the author: creative → themes that resonate with you; persuasive → your most persuasive argumentative points ("why is it important? because it helps me persuade the most"). Goal: understand the text almost as if you were the author — then curveball exam questions become easy. The technical essay-writing skill still needs its fixed practice time.
- The same what→why→how chain applies to **unfamiliar text** (themes → author's agenda/context → techniques serving each theme); the Twitter test is the theme-identification drill ([exam-technique](exam-technique.md)). Example: 1984 (Orwell) — themes → why they mattered to Orwell → techniques demonstrating them; a foreboding theme → lighting technique → specific scenes.

## Maths & Physics Aim
**Problem-first order**
- Flip the usual theory→equations→practice order so learning starts from the problem/purpose ("why is this important?"), then theory and equations are learned *in order to solve it* — building first-principles understanding that makes practice scale.
- Typical maths learning rushes to equations and practice with minimal theory, leaving maths extremely procedural (equations feel like magic). The broken loop: application→application trial-and-error without returning to knowledge (no COBES) → question coverage grows ~1:1 with questions practised; no scalability, no first principles to fall back on.
- Two exam problem types: standard (assessed as learned — usually fine) and applied/contextual (worded scenarios — where students struggle because they only practised exact scenarios seen). Strong students understand how/why equations work → they know when to apply them and can solve unfamiliar scenarios.
- Correct order (cave theory: purposeful, problem-solving learning): what is it → **why is it important (starred — the most important question for maths; real-world purpose)** → how does it relate (big picture) → then equations (what/why/how-related, back and forth). With understanding, 10 questions can unlock ~100 question types and 100 → ~10,000, instead of needing 10,000 questions.
- Steps: collect keywords/concepts/equation types from any resources; ask what/why-important/how-related (e.g. differentiation → applications → integration → derivative → chain rule); if notes don't answer "why important", Google it ("why is differentiation important in real life"); skip resources that don't answer your question; don't re-ask identical questions down the list (earlier reading often covered them); carry the curiosity into Shoot. Expect a very slow start (no prior knowledge), then rapid acceleration.

**Declarative-first: words & transformations before formulas (Clinics 38, 39, 41, 42)**
- For each concept/function/equation, be able to explain in WORDS (no numbers) what **transformation** it allows, in what context you'd use it, and why it's valuable. Build this declarative, big-picture understanding before notation and procedural practice. Top mathematics professors learn maths without numbers/formulas first — pure words/concepts — so equations, strategies, rules and manipulations make sense (Clinics 38, 39).
- Declarative knowledge **"illuminates the path"**: understanding which concepts/equations apply, how to string them together, the general approach. Without it you just pattern-match formulas ("minimum height → minimum formula"). Common high-school mistake: superficially rote-learning the conditions for a formula instead of conceptually understanding what it does to the scenario → can't apply properly (Clinics 38, 39).
- The maths variant of "why is this important?" is narrower and more answerable: "what does this allow me to do with the number / what transformation / in what context is it useful?" Being able to explain it in words is a good test of understanding.
- Do high-level (declarative) FIRST, then low-level (procedural practice). Getting sucked into notation/symbols/examples blocks high-level understanding AND wastes time. Look at graphs/equations generally, "from a distance," to sense what they mean (inverse exponential = exponentially diminishing); chunk variations into categories rather than treating every variant.
- Importance-based chunking in maths = understanding the main sequence of transformations for solving a problem (what transforms into what, when each is needed). ~4–5 min of discovery per function; ~half an hour gives a good sense of all main transformations.
- Examples: a logarithmic function transforms one data distribution into another (linearises it), enabling all transformations that apply to linear data — so when you need linear data but have non-linear, you know what to plug in (Clinic 41). A biostatistician works purely declaratively (words, no numbers), asking what data/relationship/variability you expect, then recommends a sequence of analyses — get that words-only knowledge first (Clinic 41).
- Proof-based maths nuance: even proofs (which look like memorisation) have a logical conceptual flow = a **method of thinking**; the value is the thinking unlocked, not the sequence (Clinics 38, 39). A theorem is a super-linear series of steps (still conceptual, not procedural) that may not fit a map; the hard part is understanding and re-deriving the steps — use throwaway "napkin" maps ([note-taking](note-taking.md)) (Clinic 42).

**Concepts before formulas; conditional > procedural (Clinics 28, 29, 30, 32)**
- Learn maths/physics through language and conceptual logic BEFORE formulas: understanding when/why to apply a formula (**conditional knowledge**) matters more than executing it (procedural). Anyone can learn to apply a formula — the mastery is knowing when and how.
- Don't write equations/formulas into notes without keywords expressing conceptual understanding of what they mean (equations without conceptual anchors are a red flag). Needing ~20 minutes of deep study before you can see a concept's relationships (vs ~10) suggests you're trying to understand it in the wrong direction — get process-level feedback (office hours/expert review), not more theory (Clinic 29).
- Physics is declarative-heavy — purposeful importance-based chunking + interleaving is the path (Clinic 32). For maths-specific interleaving: mind-map-of-theory + next-day practice problems + end-of-week revision questions (Clinic 25, repeated in Clinic 28). See [revision](revision.md).
- At Fundamentals 2 keep it simple: group things, ask "how is this related to that?", represent non-linearly; use a larger topic if conductance/conductivity feels too small (Clinic 30).
