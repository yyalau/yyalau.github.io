# Effective Note-Taking & Mind Maps
> A research-based system for representing knowledge — Visual, Processed, RElational, Freehand, Reflective, Efficient (VPREFRE) — primarily through mind maps, so note-taking produces durable, usable understanding instead of passive transcription.

## Overview
Note-taking is most of studying: it is the visible output of the Shoot step of the [bear-hunter-system](bear-hunter-system.md), where the questions created in [aiming](aiming.md) are answered and organised into a connected web of knowledge. Notes are a window into the writer's brain — they reveal what the person believes about learning, which is why coaches diagnose study problems straight from the page. This note consolidates the VPREFRE guidelines, mind-map quality standards and dissection, the backbone-first mapping workflow, subject examples, apps and devices, auditing and checkpoints, and advanced variants (Hipshot, blind mapping, napkin maps). Chunking mechanics, GRINDE maps and the Skin cleanup live in [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md); the layers of learning, cognitive load and Cave Theory behind the workflow live in [learning-science](learning-science.md); rote leftovers go to [memorisation](memorisation.md); retrieval-based revision of maps lives in [revision](revision.md); pre-study and question quality live in [aiming](aiming.md).

## VPREFRE — the Six Guidelines
Six research-based guidelines that make the Shoot step effective; they double as an audit checklist for judging any set of notes (your own or others').

- **V — Visual**: reduce words; represent ideas with doodles, symbols and diagrams.
- **P — Processed**: everything written passes through active thinking first.
- **RE — Relational**: notes represent the relationships between ideas, not isolated facts.
- **F — Freehand**: write/draw by hand (pen or stylus), not typed.
- **R — Reflective**: notes reflect your personal learning order, not the source's order.
- **E — Efficient**: learning achieved per unit of study time (info in, retained, usable).

Key principles:
- Universal foundations, true for every student the instructor has worked with — not personal "styles". Learning styles are a myth (see [learning-science](learning-science.md)).
- A mind map done correctly fulfils all six criteria neatly.
- Adopt gradually: focus on one guideline at a time; don't try to summit the mountain in one go and burn out.
- R and E act as the final self-check: does the order reflect my aim, and did the time spent produce retained, usable learning?
- More than 99% of students write notes horribly; the guidelines are achievable for everyone — struggles trace to a misunderstood step, not inability.

## Visual Notes (V)
Reduce words; represent ideas with doodles, symbols, shorthand and diagrams — preferably abstract rather than scientifically accurate — to engage extra modes of brain processing and make notes memorable.

Key principles:
- Rarely write full sentences; almost never paragraphs. Rewriting in the same format doesn't manipulate knowledge or activate other brain regions.
- Multimodal learning ladder: read→write < read→draw < read→draw abstractly < plus teach aloud / hear explanations / practice as a real skill. Schooling already saturates read/write, so add the visual mode deliberately.
- Abstract beats accurate: critical problem-solving and abstract/artistic expression use different hemispheres; converting a paragraph into a doodle uses both.
- Emphasise the important parts: a red, massive "4" in a string of sixes is memorable; uniform detail is not.
- Self-generation: figuring out how to represent something is itself learning; your own drawing is more memorable to you than an identical drawing someone else made. Sitting and thinking about how to draw something is productive learning, not wasted time.
- Visual processing is vastly faster than reading words (Tolkien spends a page describing a door; the film shows it instantly). A→B→C+D with arrows conveys a relationship far faster than a paragraph.
- Spatial arrangement encodes importance and flow (big topics placed prominently, mini-points tucked between); you can remember WHERE info was even if not WHAT — extra recall cues.
- Physical parameters matter cumulatively: colour, spatial placement, arrow types, font size/width, write vs type.
- Rule of thumb: concepts/big-picture → arrows, spatial layout, diagrams, doodles; pure facts/details → words (until advanced memorisation techniques).
- "I'm not a visual learner" is a trainable preference, not a capability — a fixed-mindset self-limitation (see [learning-science](learning-science.md), [mindset-emotion](mindset-emotion.md)).

Technique:
1. Read a paragraph/chunk.
2. Ask: how could I represent this with doodles/symbols? Which parts matter most?
3. Draw it abstractly, exaggerating the key parts; use stylised shorthand for words.

Examples:
- CRISPR sentence → "CRISPR = gene editing, time + cost efficient vs OLD tech adding genes at random places" (stylised lettering).
- Psychology stress appraisal: eyeball = evaluate stressor; shrug figure = irrelevant; speech bubble = must respond; broken object = harm/loss; telescope + "T" = threat; concentrating face turning into smiley = challenge. Drawing the chain memorised the chunk and made the 3-way branches visible at a glance.
- Chemistry ions: emphasise a distinguishing "O" as a bold ring; "OH" as a speech bubble saying "oh" — uniform icons differing only in tiny detail are not memorable.

Common mistakes: expressing everything in words/bullets/paragraphs; one scientifically accurate diagram does not make notes visual; copying verbatim.

## Visual Craft: Keyword Anchoring, Abstraction & Reuse
Clinic-derived craft rules for making visuals memorable and meaningful (Clinics 16–30, Chunkmapping Demo).

- **Draw the evoked imagery, not the literal word**: think of the concept → notice the imagery that spontaneously comes to mind → draw THAT. "Red eye" → the Eye of Sauron tower, not a literal red eye. More abstract = more processed = more memorable (Clinic 16).
- **Never copy-paste or trace Google Images** ("you are not a human camera"): it removes the chance to process, abstract and visually express, makes passivity easy, and is too detailed to remember. Look, think, then redraw in your own abstraction. Google Images is a fast way to get structural pictures during Aim, but not a source of note visuals.
- **Keyword anchoring**: label every visual (and formula) with a short keyword underneath (e.g. "eye drop") — visual processing power + text precision. Without an anchor, visuals are hard to interpret during revision (Clinics 24, 26).
- **Memorable over accurate**: visuals need not be scientifically accurate; too accurate/detailed can be LESS memorable. Draw the distinctive characteristic: tight junction = coin-purse drawstring; gap junction = a gap; desmosome = button/spot-weld (Clinic 16).
- **Colour in your visuals** (vs line-only): makes them pop as a stronger memory cue; bare outlines and stick figures barely engage the visual brain. Engage enough visual processing to make a real anchor, or don't bother (Clinic 23).
- **Reuse visuals across maps**: a distinctive visual can be retained for years and re-anchored in new maps, as long as it fits the new context meaningfully. "Malaria dude" (black robe, kneeling, cross, beaker, dragged intestine) remembered 9 years later and reused in a hepatosplenomegaly map (Clinic 19).
- **Avoid hieroglyphics**: idiosyncratic symbol-only notes / arbitrary codes that don't represent real relationships. Check the dedicated Spot the Issues challenge; acceptable at Fundamentals, fixed at Briefing/Technique Training (Clinics 18, 29, 30).
- **Scale over-visualization down**: a visual for everything is a good start, but target ~50–60% of items — fewer, better visuals with more effort each, aimed at the parts most likely to be forgotten (especially lists). Simple landmarks that offload a few words can be low-effort (Clinic 28).
- **Effort judged by retention, not session length**: drawing slowly does NOT drop cognitive load; load drops only if thinking becomes lower-level/linear. Justin spends 1.5–2 h on a single painting-like visual and retains it ~3 years — worth it once other techniques are strong (Clinics 23, 28).
- **Marker-pen recipe** (no artistic skill needed): pick a colour → draw the basic shape and fill it → add a few streaks in the dominant colour → outline in black → add dots/highlights. Repeat across the map (e.g. a pool of acid for pH) (Chunkmapping Demo).
- **Drawing is a secondary tool**: it facilitates cognition rather than being a Bloom's mastery level; the cognitive process matters more than the medium or artistic quality. House-building analogy: right blueprint + skilled workers build the house even with shoddy tools (Clinic 14).
- **Mini method of loci for lists**: when a map gets wordy, ask "is there a list here I can memorise with a mini loci?" — draw one absurd character inside the map in place of three bullet points (e.g. a cat in a sombrero riding a lizard). See [memorisation](memorisation.md).

## Processed Notes (P)
Everything written must first pass through active thinking — asking what is it / why is it important / how does it relate — rather than being copied straight from source to page.

Key principles:
- Learning happens in the processing (questions, relationships) and in deciding how to represent it visually/abstractly — NOT at info-in and NOT at writing-out; unprocessed info is immediately ejected.
- Read-then-write with no thinking = no learning; fast "in-and-out" page flicking is fast but completely ineffective. Processing slows studying down (fewer pages flicked) but speeds learning up.
- **Distance-from-source rule**: the more your notes resemble the form in which you originally consumed the information, the worse the learning. Verbatim laptop transcription of a lecturer = terrible learning; a classmate condensing the same lecture onto a napkin with a ballpoint pen — listening, processing, simplifying — learns far more (Clinic 15).
- Always paraphrase to the simplest, most concise form; verbatim copiers have lower retention and worse exam results. Felt mental challenge is a GOOD sign.
- **Processing ≠ paraphrasing**: paraphrasing rewrites in your own words; processing finds the SIMPLEST representation of the MAXIMUM information. It includes restructuring, eliminating/adding words, rearranging, visualising — whatever transforms info to make sense for you.
- Processing is a component technique fitted into wider techniques; once learned, ALL notes should be processed in some way. The Aim step only changes the TYPES of questions — you still process notes and make mind maps. Shoot replaces your processing of notes, NOT your processed notes (Clinics 23, 24).
- Simplifying forces processing; shorter headings, minimised wording and deliberate formatting are higher-order learning acts (Clinic 15).

Common mistakes: transcribing or paraphrasing without asking the three questions; judging productivity by pages covered or words written; passive "oh that makes sense, write it down" note-taking (Clinic 13).

## Relational Notes (RE)
Notes must represent the relationships between ideas — not the ideas in isolation — because relationships are what give information meaning and are the most important part of learning.

Key principles:
- Relationships-focused vs information-focused: "what/definition" notes leave facts isolated, so the brain discards them (Cave Theory — see [learning-science](learning-science.md)).
- Process each chunk with what/why/how; when the next chunk's "why is it important" answer refers to the previous chunk, strengthen that connection and make it super clear on the page.
- Making relationships explicit forces clearer thinking than keeping them implicit in your head; the writer who represents relationships understands them better.
- Mind maps are the natural format: three concepts and their links = three arrows plus a few words/drawings, versus an entire essay in linear text.
- **The information dodges the arrows**: arrows take priority over text blocks; tiny afterthought arrows mean the writer still prioritises information (Thomas Hobbes only matters in relation to John Locke and the English philosophers' mutual influence).
- If notes omit relationships (the most meaningful part of learning), the note-writing time is wasted.

Common mistakes: writing content first and adding arrows afterwards; understanding relationships mentally but never representing them on the page.

## Freehand Notes (F)
Write/draw notes freehand (pen and paper, or digital stylus) rather than typing; freehand forces deeper processing and makes visual, relational expression easy.

Key principles:
- Three reasons freehand beats typing: (1) typing is too easy/fast so we think less — freehand forces more processing → more learning; (2) freehand makes visual representation easy (you can't draw with a keyboard); (3) typed notes are very bad at representing relationships (paragraphs vs a few arrows).
- "Faster to type" misses the point: faster to LEARN by not typing than to type notes without learning. Build the habit early even if the benefit isn't visible yet.
- **Research**: Oppenheimer, "The Pen is Mightier Than the Keyboard" demonstrates the advantage but doesn't conclusively explain why; cognitive load theory + multimodal learning theory provide the more robust explanation (freehand raises cognitive load → deeper processing, improves focus via anchoring, increases visual/abstract expression). Observed across every subject and every student.
- Digital freehand is strongly recommended: infinite canvas removes paper-size limits. Typed mind maps only at a very advanced level — too passive for typical learners.
- Avoid lined paper and small A4 pages — cramped layouts make relationships hard to see and remember; lined paper frames linear thinking.
- **Match medium to purpose**: freehand for NOTES (the medium creates the learning benefit); for logs/journals (e.g. Kolb cycles, COPEs) the benefit is the thinking, so typing is equally valid — store them somewhere referencable (Clinic 12).

## Reflective Notes (R)
Notes must reflect the order in which you actually learned — your personal Aim order — not the order of the textbook, class notes or guidebook.

Key principles:
- If your Aim decided 2→1→3, your Shoot must capture 2→1→3; opening the textbook and starting at item 1 throws the whole aim process out the window.
- The resource's order is almost guaranteed NOT to be the best order for your brain (order control — see [aiming](aiming.md), [learning-science](learning-science.md)).
- Rule of thumb: if your notes are in the same order as the source you learned from, you probably did it wrong.
- Your notes' order, flow, map shape and connections should be relatively unique because your brain is unique — identical notes to everyone else's make no sense. Grid-like, ultra-organised maps that all look the same lose the unique-shape memory cue.

Common mistakes: letting the textbook layout dictate note order (habit takeover at the moment of shooting).

## Efficient Notes (E)
Efficiency = learning achieved per unit of study time, where learning means info in, retained, can apply and use. Notes are efficient if, immediately after writing them, you can recall and apply the content.

Key principles:
- **The 20-minute test**: after 20 minutes of note-taking, is the info retained and usable from recall? If you must go back and re-read/revise it, that is a red flag that the note-taking is inefficient.
- Minutes spent purely documenting (not learning) are wasted; this check applies to any note-taking method.
- A note can be relational yet still inefficient (e.g. too wordy) — E is a separate check, not a duplicate of RE.
- Long definition lists belong in flashcards used the right way, not in 17-page documents; copying definitions into notes is worse than flashcards done wrong (see [memorisation](memorisation.md)).
- **Cornell note-taking**: extremely overhyped, unnecessarily complicated, only partially effective; studies show it is relatively not that great — don't bother learning it.
- Measure learning, not studying: "it takes long" usually means measuring arbitrary pages/font-size/hours instead of actual learning. Verify retention ~a week later, not immediately (see [revision](revision.md)).

Common mistakes: hours of writing with little retained; creating "future reference" documents that merely duplicate the source instead of producing learning.

## Collecting vs Processing: the Split-Page Crutch
A transitional ("crutch") method, not a final form: split the page in half — a messy "collecting" side captures information, a "processing" side re-represents it in the simplest form covering as much information as possible. It exists because most students only know linear notes and need a safe bridge to processed, non-linear note-taking.

Technique:
1. Divide the page into collecting and processing halves.
2. Collect (in class): messy, chaotic, minimal words — keywords and symbols (arrows, plus signs) to cut filler ("the", "and", "leads to").
3. At each concept change (end of paragraph/subtopic, teacher finishes a point), review the collected notes.
4. Process: represent as much information as possible in the simplest way (e.g. a drawing leading to another drawing plus 2–3 key sentences).
5. Keep detail on the collecting side for reference; focus the processing side on clean, simple representation.

Key principles:
- Collecting is just linear note-taking — nothing inherently good about it; you can hardly do it wrong. In private coaching it is skipped entirely.
- Processing has three training outcomes: (1) reduce words/simplify; (2) shift learning from paper into the head — tolerate the discomfort of holding, thinking about, then expressing information mentally; (3) gain first experience of non-linear notes and visualisation.
- **Skip if ready**: if you can already take information in, process it, and go non-linear directly, do that — it's always better. Collecting is one of the rare course steps you may skip (Clinic 19).
- Review checklist (coach): reducing words + using visualisations? genuinely processing in your head BEFORE writing? attempting less-linear layout? Three yeses = continue forward.
- Perfection is NOT required at technique training; heading in the right direction with real experience is what matters.

Common mistakes: treating collecting as the goal and perfecting linear notes; skipping mental processing and rushing to mind maps (the minimal-brain-power habit transfers to a new format → bad maps at Ascent 1–2); writing everything down to feel safe instead of learning it; fear of losing small details (writing something down ≠ having it in your head — later techniques fill the gaps).

## In-Class Note-Taking: Listen First, Then Write
In class, listen and process before writing rather than transcribing while the teacher speaks; this activates learning and trains the auditory mode.

Key principles:
- Writing/typing while listening = "human photocopier": time wasted, no real learning, no auditory-mode training.
- Sequence: listen → think → process → put notes down. Paraphrasing implicitly requires having listened first.
- Training multiple learning modes (spoken/experienced/read/visualised) is enormously beneficial and trainable (even with ADHD/dyslexia).
- If you must use linear lecture notes, keep a pre-set frame and after class ask how each point fits the frame, then re-chunk — viable but less efficient (Clinic 20).
- With proper pre-study framing, the teacher's order doesn't matter; at a high enough skill level you can study the whole class material without attending class (Clinic 19). See [aiming](aiming.md) for pre-study and [exam-technique](exam-technique.md) for in-class technique.

Common mistakes: constantly writing/typing as the person speaks, especially in fast-paced lectures.

## Mind Maps & Chunk Maps: the Core Encoding Tool
Justin's mind-mapping method (within the Bear Hunter System) is the core, most-efficient encoding tool for declarative/domain knowledge, rebuilt from learning-science principles and deliberately different from Tony Buzan's.

Key principles:
- **Buzan comparison**: Buzan designed his method for professionals; Justin learned it, applied it as a student, found flaws when teaching, and rebuilt it from learning-science principles. His method is harder to master but has a higher ceiling and is more transformative; the underlying cognitive processes differ even where visuals look similar. Learn Justin's method directly and purely — no point learning Buzan's first (Clinic 21).
- It is the gateway technique for declarative (domain) knowledge — not procedural/non-declarative memory. Use it for most content, even small pieces and meetings; skip only for truly tiny content (see "When to Skip Mapping").
- **Chunk map vs mind map**: the goal is a CHUNK MAP (inquiry-led relational priority chunk mapping), not a conventional mind map. A conventional mind map maps information non-linearly with basic grouping (by overlap or how it's taught) — hard cap on efficiency, retention relies on memorising where things sit. A chunk map makes explicit chunks + clear backbone + organisational structure the dominant focus, built via inquiry (Clinic 23).
- Correct flow: raw info → questions → relationships → reorder/repackage → compare/contrast/evaluate/synthesise → memorable chunks → non-linear format. Wrong flow: info (maybe pre-chunked) → mind map → relationships added → vague questions popping in (isolated processes, not a chain).
- A specific bad style (taught by a certain "mind map expert" business): branches with things written on lines, meaningless intersections, not explicit about schemas (e.g. 12 things coming off one point). It messes up knowledge schema.
- Mind maps match how the brain naturally thinks (spatial, relational — like knowledge of hobbies/sports, never stored as a manual); a mind map is literally a map of your mind. Linear notes force the brain into an artificial format.
- 100% of students who claimed "mind maps didn't work for me" were doing them wrong; no student who did them correctly found them ineffective. Mind mapping is a surprisingly technical skill (a ~150-page book exists on it).
- Follow only the course's guidelines; don't mix in other YouTube advice — the differences (mind mapping, inquiry-based learning, COLBE structure) are deliberate (Clinic 21).

## Mindmap Dissection: Quality Standards & Review
Systematic evaluation of mind maps against VPREFRE to develop self-appraisal skill: the decisive quality is clean, prioritised relationships — not content coverage or prettiness.

Review order (clinics): (1) **visuals** — present, processed, thought-through? (2) **flow** — do I know where to start and where the structure takes me? (3) **chunk depth** — 2-4 rule violations? (4) **chunk quality** — intuitive test, stage-adjusted. From Ascent 2+, run VPFR(E) until second nature; the clinic shortcut is faster triage, not a replacement (Clinics 17, 18).

Key principles:
- Think relationships FIRST, then place content; arrows added after the fact produce chaos (crossing lines like a "crazy nine-way intersection").
- Group related points and arrange spatially so lines don't cross — imagine pulling tangled strings straight; spatial arrangement + abstract expression activates more of the brain → deeper connections → retention.
- The map must visibly answer "why is it important" and "how does it relate" (the Aim questions); a map showing only process/links without importance reflects shallow aiming and predicts retention problems.
- Emphasise the most important flow of information so the eye knows where to go; good maps can hold enormous content yet look simple (each arrow would be a whole sentence in words).
- Check logic-layer emphasis (e.g. boxes around main chunks so the backbone pops) and chunk-to-chunk relationships, not just concept-to-concept arrows: if every chunk relation passes through individual concepts, the learner is relating at micro level only (Clinic 17).
- Messy first mind maps are normal and expected; redoing/cleaning them later is part of the Bear Hunter system. Still aim for relationships as clean as possible on each pass.
- Use a big canvas: A4/lined paper cramps relationships; lined paper looks chaotic — avoid it.
- Fix the single highest-yield structural issue first (usually a 2-4 violation), then re-evaluate — structure changes cascade. Don't prioritise surface fixes (colours, eye-catching visuals) over structural ones (Clinic 17).
- Test transfer: review the same learner across subjects to see whether mistakes are subject-specific or systematic; some topics are naturally easier to chunk.
- Stage-appropriate judgment: a fundamentals map with a long flaw list can still be 8–9/10 if non-linearity, grouping attempts and relationships are emerging; a briefing map can be excellent while not yet a chunk map. Don't give advanced feedback (arrow semantics, reverse chunking) to fundamentals-stage learners.

Examples:
- Olivia: chaotic first map → two months later a cleaner-looking map containing MORE information with less writing (spatial arrangement + abstraction); straight A+/90%+ on every test since.
- Chemistry map: regroup two-way reactions, reduction reactions and catalysts into a clean triangle instead of crossing arrows.
- Fetal-health map shows importance chains: ultrasound → diagnosis → fetal health; technologies → assisted reproduction → treating infertility → fetal health — the Aim questions are visible in the map.
- (Clinic 17) Arij's statistics map: bar charts belonged under a shared "representing" chunk → reverse-chunking opportunity; flow fine (7/10); visuals weakest but not top priority.
- (Clinic 18) Fundamentals advice: bigger paper, landscape, space things out, group more, cleaner relationships, slightly fewer words, slightly more visuals — 1% marginal gains compound within a month.

## Mind-Map Failure Patterns
A recurring catalogue of named visual/structural errors that signal specific technique failures; used to diagnose maps in live clinics and Spot the Issues (Clinics 14–30). Most trace back to inquiry/order errors, not the topic.

- **Waterfalling**: information only flows outward/downhill (heading → subheadings → details) with no lateral connections; linear in order, non-linear only in appearance. Cause: chunking not independent — following chapter headings/teaching order. Fix: Traffic Light System / inquiry-based chunking (see [aiming](aiming.md)). Forgivable at Fundamentals; broken around Technique Training.
- **Spider-webbing**: related items placed in the wrong chunks, forcing long complicated arrows across the map; too many undifferentiated relationships. Cause: bad order control / poor importance-based chunking. Fix: inquiry-led grouping.
- **Anti-spider-webbing**: too few relationships because you actively avoid drawing them (overwhelming). Cause: pre-chunking + narrow questions.
- **Single-node chains**: a run of nodes where only one thing comes off each (this→this→this). ~70% of the time a simpler chunking exists; signals process/information-based chunking rather than importance-based. Each link has one tether → weak relationships, each follows its own forgetting curve ≈ rote memorisation. Fix: ask "why is each item important?", group into real chunks; if order truly matters, chunk steps into purpose-based stages. 50–60% of the time you do NOT need to memorise order (Clinics 14, 17, 19).
- **Cracked glass**: the logic layer is replaced by a web of multiple concepts/details connecting chunks (looks like cracks meeting); chaotic-looking but well-chunked is distinct from spider-webbing. Cause: chunking not based on importance; information-hierarchy direction reversed. Fix: keep major logic arrows, express importance through chunks, ask "what constitutes A?" going inward (Clinics 15, 26).
- **Islands**: dense isolated bodies oddly joined by a single arrow (two cities with a highway, not one city with suburbs); backbone added after the fact. Cause: not going topic-wide / framing bias. Fix: whole-topic pre-study scaffold first (Clinics 22–27).
- **Wheel-and-spokes**: everything radiating from a central theme, masking disconnected segments. Removing the central theme often reveals 3+ isolated segments. Fix: delete the central theme, lay out main chunks, ask "how is this related to that?" until an organic flow emerges (Clinic 29).
- **Lazy intersections / branching from nothing**: arrows sprouting off other arrows or unlabelled junctions with no meaning. Should be an explicit, labelled chunk; count real labelled sub-chunks, not intersections (Clinics 17, 18, 26).
- **Empty logic-layer chunk**: a "main" chunk with little coming off it → questionable importance; reconsider the chunking (Clinic 26).
- **Details-stripped**: omitting details out of fear of overload, making the work undiagnosable — coaches cannot see which of ~85 possible mistakes you will make. Add details back; you cannot improve on work you did not attempt (Clinic 29).
- **Circuit gridding**: overly symmetrical/uniform lines/arrows (tessellating diagonal or vertical/horizontal) — not memorable because everything looks the same. Use arrows to create a UNIQUE shape reflecting the info structure (Clinics 22–24).
- **Information hierarchy**: a family-tree-shaped map that re-groups the textbook's headings/terminology/definitions. Grouped but not reflective (fails GRINDE "R"); "why is it important?" missing or answered with reverse causality. Fix: order control, build logic first, re-chunk by importance (see [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md)).
- **Hedgehog map**: spikes off every point — more memorable than linear notes but far below what a mind map should be; fixed in Skin (see [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md)).
- **Chain vs network**: lots of visuals + a single sequence but no grouping/relationships = a fragile CHAIN (forget one link and others suffer); build a NETWORK by grouping events and relating points.

## Backbone-First Shoot Workflow
The standard Shoot procedure: build the most basic relationship structure from the major headings first, hang sub-concepts on it, then add full detail in later passes — cycling aim/shoot throughout. Scaffolding = building a basic frame of main ideas and relationships that later detail is layered onto (house-frame analogy: frame → walls → paint → furnishings, never furnishings first).

Technique:
1. Take the big headings/keywords from the Aim step; ask how the headings relate to each other and to the big-picture purpose (ask heading-level questions too: why is the heart important? why are vessels important?).
2. Build the most basic backbone structure (main nodes + relationships only). Expect 20–30+ minutes even for four headings; beginners may need 2–3 hours. Do not rush — everything hangs on this; reaching this stage is ~80% of the battle.
3. Plant the remaining keywords/sub-concepts around the backbone, using the Aim questions to define each relationship; produce a detailed structure. Rearrange when understanding changes.
4. Add all remaining detail in a further pass (e.g. in a different colour) — the same map fleshed out; by now the material has been passed through 3–4 times.
5. Keep arbitrary details (equations, constants, definitions) off the map → flashcards (see [memorisation](memorisation.md)).
6. Hand over to the Skin step for cleanup and consolidation (see [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md)).

Key principles:
- Of ~10 hours on a topic, spending the first hour only on identifying the major chunks and how they relate is reasonable — one of the most important and most difficult steps. Once the frame exists, every later detail "has a box to belong in" (Clinic 19).
- Order of events: collect keywords → inquiry finds relationships → relationships reveal chunks → fill information. Never information-first. "Isolation is evil": never look at a keyword in isolation as step one; if what you're reading doesn't create a relationship, skip it and return later (Clinic 21).
- By the time relationships and chunks are built, ~half the information is already obvious and barely needs "learning".
- Time benchmarks: whole biology topic — beginners ~5–6 h, experienced ~1.5–2 h; chemistry area of study — under 10 h total for 90%+ retention; math calculus conceptual map ~1.5 h from near-zero. Retention after the process: ~95–100% of what is on the map, still intact days later.
- Constant aim↔shoot cycling (every 3–10 min); questions generated by shooting drive the next lookups; Google fills resource gaps. Early keywords take longest; it gets faster as patterns build (100 keywords: first 50 ≈ 10 h, next 50 ≈ 2 h) (Clinic 26).
- If two topic clusters cannot be linked strongly, keep them as separate mind maps rather than forcing a shaky connection.
- Avoid details while building the backbone unless they help create better chunks; many details become obvious once the whole topic is framed (e.g. tight-junction properties follow from its waterproofing role). Process details later (Clinic 18).
- Without scaffold-building ability: no quality relationships, no higher-order learning, no proper inquiry, no time savings, no order control, no multi-pass — every advanced encoding technique fails (Clinic 18).

Common mistakes: rushing the backbone to "get into the detail"; trying to memorise details during structure passes; forcing weak connections; starting with details/furnishings before the frame; chasing colours/pictures while notes remain linear.

## Whole-Topic Scope vs Segmental Mapping
Do the largest topic you can tolerate in one map — never split a topic into separate maps joined by lines. Study topic-wide (not lecture-by-lecture) to avoid "islands".

Key principles:
- Bigger topic = more relationships, more chunks, deeper/lateral thinking, stronger scaffold/schema; working in isolated pockets is the failure mode. Any topic distils to 2–4 main points + relationships; when overwhelmed, reduce detail, not topic breadth (Clinic 16).
- Splitting maps promotes viewing concepts in isolation (good pockets joined by single lines), builds weak schemas, and must be unlearned later.
- **Segmental mapping** (growing a map pocket-by-pocket, each segment developed in isolation) isolates information, reduces relevance, harms memory and transfer, and forces later restructuring. Common trigger: insecurity about "not having the details down" — name the habit and pull back to the big picture (Clinic 30).
- Pre-study scope should match the final mind map's scope: the whole topic, not lecture-by-lecture. Building per-lecture maps and merging them later is NOT equivalent — cognitive order matters (baking analogy: mixing after the oven is not the same as mixing before). The big-picture schema must exist BEFORE details, because it is what makes details memorable (Clinic 30).
- Scope benchmarks (Clinic 16): beginners ~half a lecture to one lecture (uni) or a few classes (school); after 3–4 months: a month+ of school work or a week+ of uni work per session; eventually a semester per time-block. A topic ≈ a curriculum unit (4–5 weeks school, 4–5 days uni); decide boundaries with a quick 5–6 min skim ("getting less relevant → new topic") (Clinic 27).
- Scaffold-first protects time: identify the biggest chunks for the whole topic first, fill in on the second pass; big-picture chunks rarely change unless the topic was fundamentally misunderstood.
- Spatial arrangement forces evaluation: deciding WHERE a chunk sits requires justifying it against other ideas = higher-order learning, not mere design. Planning the whole arrangement mentally only becomes feasible around upper-intermediate; beginners should expect to rearrange (digital makes this easy — lasso/move).
- If you already have isolated maps: start a blank-canvas brain dump — list 4–5 main ideas across the whole topic from scratch (don't reference existing maps first), reframe, then fit the old maps in (Clinic 20).
- For huge maps, prefer one resource with a backbone you extend; only split if it becomes exorbitantly massive, or where connections are superficial (Clinic 27).

Common mistakes: one map per subtopic/lecture joined by lines; keeping maps small because "it takes too long"; treating placement as arbitrary decoration; redrawing from scratch instead of rearranging digitally; pre-studying too narrowly then fixing structure later.

## Spatial Layout, Arrows & Direction
Relationships must be explicit, and layout must create memorable, unique anchors and clear structure.

Key principles:
- **Arrows, not lines**: a line = "these are connected" (can be lazy); an arrow forces you to decide direction/nature of the relationship = higher-order processing, deeper encoding. Beginners' undirected lines are a "cheap way out" — ~7/10 times a better chunking exists (Clinics 17, 21).
- **Arrow direction depends on framing context** (~75–80% of cases): photosynthesis within a plant's life-cycle big picture vs broken into sub-components justify opposite directions. The act of figuring out which makes sense deepens the neural groove; a thoughtful, intuitive choice is probably right. Usually the reason of importance points down to the things it makes important (~75%), but use judgment (Clinics 18, 29).
- Distinguish uni-directional (one way) vs bi-directional (both ways) vs non-directional. Non-directional lines are acceptable only for genuinely non-influential conceptual links (e.g. two antibiotics in the same class), and only at late Ascent 2/3+; don't change your whole chunk structure to represent one (Clinic 21).
- **Hybrid network + flow**: the ideal map is a clean flow between the few main chunks PLUS a network of many relationships between ideas. Pure-network looks messy; pure-flow is linear and hides chunks. Identify the 3–4 biggest chunks, figure out the clean flow between them, then build a network on top. Think about how you'll USE the info; prioritise the main highways between chunks (warehouse/hospital analogy: arrange rooms before furniture) (Clinic 24).
- **Spacing is diagnostic, not cosmetic**: cramped maps hide relationships (you can't imagine fitting an arrow); spreading ideas out (infinite canvas) reveals chunking problems and lets you evaluate connections. If you start cramped, just start again bigger rather than extending awkwardly (Clinics 19, 26).
- Light background, readable contrast (dark text on white makes shapes pop; avoid green-on-blue). Prefer freehand over typed/linear apps — typed arrows/shapes are boxy and artificial, and typing removes the effortful prioritisation of placement (Clinic 19).
- Avoid long sweeping lines that dodge content (signal relationships not planned in advance); rearrange so related items are near each other. The effort of making it clean ("do I need this line? what does it mean? where should this go?") is evaluative, higher-order learning — not aesthetics (Clinic 24).
- Visually distinguish layers (logic vs concepts vs important details) via bigger arrows/headings so the map is followable; strengthen visual emphasis between layers at Ascent 3 (Clinic 25).

## Keywords, Labels & Detail Management
Chunks and keywords should be simplified, intuitive keywords — not literal questions, phrases, or sentences. Manage details deliberately so notes/schemas don't blow out of proportion.

Key principles:
- **No question-chunks**: a chunk literally named "What to aim for instead?" is slower and harder to hold than a keyword ("aims", "alternatives"). Ask the question OF the items, then name the chunk with the simplified answer-concept (Clinic 28).
- **No phrases/sentences as keywords** (e.g. "Hyder Ali broke alliance restored conquered territories") — not intuitive or memorable. Two valid options for required phrases: (a) intuitive chunk (e.g. "liberation") with the phrase as end-detail underneath; (b) keep phrases out of the map and memorise with flashcards. Never sacrifice encoding quality to fit the assessment format (Clinic 28).
- Avoid technical words in the backbone where possible; keep it as simple as possible, especially at later stages. Replace terminology-heavy chunk names with intuitive ones (Clinics 28, 29).
- **Layer split**: layer 3 (important details) → mind map; layer 4 (arbitrary details) → flashcards ± other memorisation tools, plus bits of layer 3 likely to be forgotten. Include a detail on the map only if it helps you understand the concept; otherwise leave it out (saves real estate). Judging this is hard before ~Ascent 2 (Clinic 23). See [learning-science](learning-science.md) for the layers of learning.
- **Reduce rote through encoding**: for heavy-detail subjects, find the angle that makes a detail non-isolated (questions→relationships→chunks) to eliminate the need to rote-memorise. Many "must-memorise" details make sense once you find the angle; you'll need far fewer flashcard reps (Clinic 23).
- **Meaning before memorisation** (lists/clinical facts): make each item meaningful (why it matters, how used in reality, how items relate), reason mechanistically, chunk by shared meaning/function, then rote-learn the residue. Real-world use is reverse of the list (patients present isolated facts, not diagnoses). Memorising a list attached to a label guarantees flashcard failures (Clinic 16).
- **Scope vs depth vs detail**: SCOPE = what's relevant per objectives; DEPTH = how deeply things work, their connections/relationships; DETAIL = individual facts. Priority: depth within scope first, detail second. With good chunking, much detail becomes intuitive and no longer needs memorising (Clinic 12).
- Early stages: use small, easy memorisation aids (flashcards) to manage details to an acceptable level; don't integrate details advanced-style too early — it hinders core skill development.
- **Pre-revision layer-4 detail sweep**: before formal revision, sweep resources for arbitrary details you never captured; for each, decide reconnect/integrate into the map vs rote-learn (modified method of loci vs flashcard). Do the sweep when you make your flashcards/loci map (see [revision](revision.md), [memorisation](memorisation.md)).

## Subject-Specific Shoot Examples
Worked demonstrations of the Shoot workflow across subjects (see [aiming](aiming.md) for the Aim-step examples).

- **Biology (blood system; asexual reproduction)**: build the heading-level backbone first (heart → vessels, heart-rate control → heart, big-picture purpose), then hang sub-concepts and detail, cycling aim/shoot every 5–10 min. Detailed pass: loop shape (heart → arteries → capillaries → veins → heart), medulla/autonomic NS/hormones under control, cardiac cycle linked to heart, blood pressure linked to vessels. Asexual reproduction: types/cloning/advantages-disadvantages/issues as main chunks; vegetative propagation repositioned to encompass all types after learning changed the expectation — restructuring is normal. About half the questions came from shooting; ~95–100% retention days later.
- **Chemistry (acids, water, redox, solubility)**: connect big topics through shared underlying concepts (acids/bases vs redox → both types of reactions: proton vs electron transfer; solubility ↔ water as solvent → both about solutions). Leave weakly connected clusters as separate maps (reactions vs solutions). Keep equations/constants/pH equation/definitions off the map → flashcards (<10% of the subject). Pro tip: few core concepts repeat endlessly — spend a couple of hours on electronegativity, polarity, bond strength and WHY reactions occur; then most reactions are variations of the same logic and need no memorisation. Derive reaction patterns from first principles (e.g. acid + metal hydroxide → salt + water: negative hydroxide attracts the acid's positive proton; metal–OH bond breaks; H+ + OH− → H2O).
- **Math (calculus, problem-first)**: lock in the purpose/application structure before learning any equations — learn in order to solve the problem, not learn information then try to solve problems with it. Build the logical map: functions/inverse → differentiation (rate of change) → derivatives (chain rules) → applications (tangents/normals, min/max, stationary points, signed areas) and anti-differentiation back; integration → indefinite/definite → area, average values. ~1.5 h from near-zero to a strong conceptual map WITHOUT knowing the equations; flips the practice ratio from 1:1 to 10:1. Aim/shoot cycling every 3–5 min.
- **English text response (1984)**: shoot in order themes → why the themes matter (author's background) → techniques/characters that exemplify them. Identify main themes; read the author's biography asking "what in their life made these themes important?"; map characters/techniques to themes; write essay sentences connecting background → theme → technique. Starting from memorised techniques and working back to themes fails when essays demand fluid discussion. The map grows by adding examples per theme, not exponentially more themes. (1984 themes: class/oppression/fascism, government brainwashing, militaristic supervision, ignorance/rejection of truth; Orwell background links: policeman + poor → class insight; WWII soldier, anti-fascist → brainwashing/militarism; rejected early works → resentment of willful ignorance.)
- **English unfamiliar text**: full Shoot order = (1) what are the themes (Twitter test), (2) why important (author's agenda/context), (3) how techniques relate to themes — then Skin and write the essay. **Twitter test**: read as if under a character limit — every word must earn its place — asking why the author chose each sentence/phrase/word/image and what they want you to feel. Function words are neutral; loaded choices carry the agenda. Train until unconsciously competent BEFORE the exam. Techniques are understood as serving a theme/agenda, not memorised pairings.
- **Languages (comprehensible input)**: shoot using material right at — just beyond — the fringe of what you already know. Create/find dialogues, essays, reading or books that genuinely interest you; create variations around things you are curious to say/write/understand — that curiosity is the aim that drives the shoot.

## Formula-Heavy & Procedural Subjects
For problem-solving subjects (math, physics, chemistry, coding) you need two distinct competencies: conceptual understanding (how ideas/equations relate, how to approach problems) and procedural skill (fluency executing calculations). Mind maps build the conceptual side; procedural practice builds the other.

Key principles:
- **Map the concepts, not the formulas**: why is this formula important, how is it used, how does its concept relate to others. Add formulas underneath as reference, or keep a separate categorised formula sheet. Conceptual understanding lets you see how to approach hard multi-concept questions instead of staring at formulas hoping variables match (Clinics 19–21).
- **Equation chunking**: don't go straight from a chunk to a formula, or group formulas merely because they are formulas (equivalent to chunking "things with the letter O in them"). Lead with a verbal/abstract expression of the formula's relevance, then attach the formula as a sub-note (keyword-anchor it like an image). If formulas share a function/conceptual basis, that basis is the chunk name, not "formulas" (Clinic 26).
- **Declarative vs procedural struggle**: procedural = you know the approach but slip on a rule/equation/calculation (fix: volume + repetition with error feedback); declarative = fine when told "use this equation" but struggle with out-of-context "how would you solve this?" (fix: build the conceptual approach first). Declarative gaps make procedural issues worse; maths is a rare big mix of both (Clinics 41, 42).
- Procedural practice is a form of retrieval practice that re-encodes but uses a different part of the brain; conceptual knowledge alone won't give fluency under exam conditions. There is no fixed concept:procedure ratio — the process naturally leads you to the point where a piece of information's only purpose is to execute a concept (a "detail", e.g. the discriminant equation), then you do procedural practice.
- Proofs are sequential but chunkable: each block of sequence has a purpose; chunking famous proofs and comparing their strategic steps to your own approach reveals inconsistencies. Not everything must be visual, but people underestimate how much can be replaced with visuals.
- Domain knowledge → BHS/mind maps; skills → interleaved retrieval practice (see [revision](revision.md)). Learn maths declaratively first (see [aiming](aiming.md)).

Common mistakes: mind-mapping the calculations/formulas themselves; staring at formulas to find matching variables; skipping procedural practice; learning an advanced "no-notes" speed technique before mastering core techniques.

## Apps & Devices for Digital Note-Taking
A good note-taking app (1) facilitates correct learning cognitive processes — convenient, fast freehand drawing with a digital stylus plus infinite canvas (most important) — and (2) has good organisational features (less important). Most dedicated apps are designed to sell to an uninformed public, not for learning research.

Recommended (independently reviewed/tested by the course team):
- **Concepts** (iOS/Android/Windows): originally a design/architecture app; excellent writing experience, full infinite canvas, acceptable organisation.
- **Squid** (Android): the best note-taking app on the market across any device/platform; excellent, intuitive, simple writing; organisation comparable to OneNote; not feature-bloated.
- **Leonardo** (Windows): Windows has no good note-taking apps; fully featured drawing app with infinite canvas but no built-in organisation — save project files and export drawings as images/PDFs into OneNote or Evernote.
- **Explain Everything**: built for online teachers; fully featured with great organisation, more expensive; the app used to record all course tablet videos.

Reasonable but limited: **Notability** (decent organisation, good writing, severely limited by lack of infinite canvas); **OneNote** (good organisation, somewhat infinite canvas but low zoom-out makes notes unreadable; average writing, no palm detection/pressure sensitivity; clunky for mind mapping — not recommended).

Well-known but inefficient: **Evernote** (leading organisation, horrible restrictive linear note-taking); **Notion** (high organisation, many linear options, but all notes locked into being linear — not recommended for efficient/effective note-taking; "trashy for studying", a dependency funnel; worst of both worlds for scheduling) (Clinic 14).

Devices & formats:
- Prefer digital freehand over paper because of the infinite canvas. **iPad / direct-draw tablet** is superior (convenient, portable, lasso-and-move makes scaffold adjustment trivial); **Wacom** drawing tablet is cheaper, high quality (hand-eye coordination makes looking-at-screen/drawing-on-tablet feel natural). A drawing pad is fine; tablets honestly make a big difference (Clinics 10, 25).
- **E-readers** (Kindle, reMarkable) are poor for study reference material — multi-page switching is clunky and reference demands high order control; fine for linear reads (fiction, narrative non-fiction). Match device to reading mode (Clinic 18).
- **Text-to-speech** is an accessibility accommodation (e.g. severe dyslexia), not an efficiency upgrade; otherwise listening is not more efficient than reading + processing with proper order control (Clinic 18).
- **Second brain / PKM**: offload stored knowledge into tools so your first brain doesn't carry it. Justin's stack: Roam Research (core), EndNote/Zotero (references), Connected Papers (literature mapping). Pen-and-paper second brains are insanely difficult; software is the practical medium. Elaborate app setup feels productive but isn't (Clinic 14). See [professional-learning](professional-learning.md), [time-management](time-management.md).
- COPEs/reflection logs can be typed/linear (Notion, Google Keep, Evernote) — the value is answering/engaging the questions, not non-linear encoding (Clinic 25).

## Auditing Notes & Checkpoint Standards
Notes are judged by whether they show how ideas connect and group, not by volume of text. Standards rise by stage.

- **Fundamentals checkpoint** — PASS signals: relationships shown, basic grouping, non-linear layout, reduced wordiness, some imagery, willingness to experiment (even if messy/low-flow). FAIL: wordy, linear, lined paper, isolated points, groups only as-taught (passive), arrows added retrospectively. Copy-pasted images create an illusion of good notes; too little information → add more (volume reveals errors). Keyword anchoring: include keywords under images; watch for hieroglyphics.
- **Briefing** — boxes to tick: non-linear, some relationships, some grouping, reduced wordiness, some visuals. Chunk/relationship quality is NOT yet expected (taught in Technique Training). Super-wordy notes are almost always a problem; visuals are hard for equation-heavy topics but still doable. If boxes tick, move on (Clinics 22–24).
- **Technique Training** — checklist: non-linear? visuals? relationships shown? chunking/grouping present? not too wordy? Waterfall pattern (heading → subheadings → details, no cross-relationships) is a failure mode, often from borrowing the textbook's chunking. Passive linear "human photocopier" notes at this stage are a big red flag — go back to fundamentals.
- **Ascent 2 readiness** — clean core backbone from major chunks; clear relationships between main chunks; importance-based chunking at every level to the edges; good flow and directionality; laterality of connections; no spider-webbing/waterfalling; non-linear, visual, not too wordy; layers clearly distinguishable even in dense semester-scale maps. Work with the biggest chunks FIRST; expanding one chunk fully before the next is an efficiency drain. Technique must be consistent across topics and survive added detail. Biggest limitation: not spending enough time getting the logic layer super clean.
- **Notebook diagnostics** (fast self-audit): "How much time between reading something and writing it down?" → reveals processing. "What type of notebook?" → A4 lined implies linear notes, no mind maps. "How many colours?" → few colours = little visual emphasis on main points. "When you revise, how long between reading notes and recalling?" → most skim first, defeating true recall (see [revision](revision.md)).
- **Perfectionism calibration**: constantly rearranging to "get it perfect" delays progress; high standards are good only if they are the RIGHT standards, which requires feedback to calibrate. Some reorganisation is normal early and decreases with skill; persistent major re-framing points to a technique problem. Measure mastery/retention (e.g. brain-dump retrieval a week later), not prettiness (Clinics 28, 30).
- **Full-system audit** (Summit): verify spacing, interleaving, retrieval method/frequency AND note-taking guidelines. Both sides required: high-efficiency encoding (aim–shoot, Skin, processing, mind maps, chunking) AND spaced interleaved retrieval. Notes always chunked, importance-based where possible; layer 3 on map, layer 4 on flashcards; visualised and processed, never passive. Obvious prohibitions: never copy verbatim; never write before mentally simplifying; never write without thinking first.

## Barriers to Effective Note-Taking
The four common objections that make students revert to comfortable linear note-taking during Shoot, and their rebuttals.

- **"Mind maps don't work for me"**: they were done wrong — most commonly jumping out of the layers into detail and what-memorising. Mind maps mirror natural spatial/relational thinking; no student who did them correctly found them ineffective. A bad past attempt is not a relevant comparison; believing otherwise is a fixed learning-style mindset.
- **"It takes too much time"**: time building structure, concepts, spatial arrangement and wordless expression IS learning (multiple brain modes, deeper connections) — invested, not wasted. The true waste is reading/writing/memorising without relationships. At unconscious competence the technique runs on autopilot, as fast as old habits but with inherently higher retention.
- **"It's too difficult / I can't do it"**: effort and confusion are signs the brain is problem-solving (cognitive load theory — see [learning-science](learning-science.md)); failure is growth and data. Once it clicks, it clicks forever. Drowsiness while studying = brain switched off = no learning.
- **"What if I miss something?"**: the system recovers misses later — learn ~80% via inquiry at high retention, then rote-learn the remaining ~20% with memorisation techniques. Memorising the whole topic instead guarantees relearning and real gaps; nothing is lost, everything is covered by the end.

Practical tips: know which layer you are on and its purpose; commit to the process as laid out, experiment, and bring failures as data to the team/live clinics — the issues are common and have been solved hundreds of times. Ask where the real risk lies: trying a professionally taught method vs staying with one that definitely won't improve.

## Hipshot: Merged Aim–Shoot
The first BHS variation: Aim and Shoot are merged into one fast, mostly mental cycle (Hipshot → Skin), building the mind map as you go instead of writing a full keyword/question list first. It is the Base Camp end-goal (see [bear-hunter-system](bear-hunter-system.md)).

Key principles:
- "Hipshot" = shooting from the hip → faster; not truly simultaneous, but rapid switching between Aim and Shoot.
- Only the MOST important (Layer 1) keywords/themes are collected — the backbone; ignore the rest initially. Questions are asked MENTALLY (same what/why/how-relate), then answered immediately from resources and placed straight on the map.
- The map starts messy; Skin later cleans it into a GRINDE map. Relationships remain the #1 priority; the cycle must feed back to generate better questions, not become a one-directional information dump.
- Prerequisite proficiency: spatial organisation, relational thinking, VPREFRE, layers of learning and order control must be automatic. Do NOT attempt before standard BHS is comfortable; if you compromise relationships/layers/order control, you are not ready — revert to standard.
- Checkpoint standard: Hipshot map quality ≥ a dedicated Aim+Shoot map.

Accounting demonstration tips: environment first (phone away, water/snack ready); constant "why is this important?" gating; constant zoom in/out ("time spent simplifying information is always time well spent"); represent words with non-words/images; skip the obvious (derivable in-exam); don't re-write repeated content (write once); mini-skim before dense sections; arbitrary detail with no conceptual hook → rote technique, not mind map. App used: Explain Everything. Justin speeds up only because he has done it tens of thousands of times — don't feel pressured to skip steps.

## Blind Mapping
A low-effort curiosity/pre-study technique: build a mind map from a keyword list WITHOUT looking at any learning material, using only logic and prior knowledge. The map is deliberately (almost certainly) wrong, which makes you aware of transformative gaps and powers targeted, high-relevance learning (Clinic 40).

Key principles:
- Fills all three curiosity conditions at once: hypothetical network + awareness of gaps + gaps that will transform your view when filled (see [learning-science](learning-science.md)).
- Effort without lower-order recall: because you stripped away the material, you hypothesise connections (puzzle-solving mind space) instead of trying to remember what you just read. Triggers the hyper-correction effect when you later learn the correct information.
- Similar to pre-chunking but more laid-back/hypothetical: pre-chunking aims for accuracy and peeks at material; Blind Mapping goes completely blind.
- Even stopping after the map (when tired) is a win: sleep-dependent consolidation strengthens the networks overnight. Especially useful for unstructured lifelong/professional learning where there is often no material at all.

Technique:
1. Get a keyword list (NOT itself a learning activity). Three sources by effort: (a) AI — paste lectures, ask for all keywords; (b) brain dump — write all concepts that might be important, aim for 7–10 items; (c) scan material — objectives, headings, bold words. If you can't brain-dump 7–10, use another source.
2. Go through the list (any order), ask "how might this be related to the next item?" Build the map BLIND — no material, only logic + prior knowledge. Simplify and group rather than arrows everywhere. Accept the map will be wrong.
3. Identify the highest-priority (most transformative) gap; fill it targeted (Google/ChatGPT for a concise definition), then refine the map. Cycle: find next gap → fill → refine.
4. Let frustration build until you want a full session; or stop when tired and let sleep consolidate.

Common mistakes: "semi-blind" (pre-learning each concept first → slips into lower-order recall, breaks the technique); using it without non-linear/relational skill (map comes out tiny); discarding it because it felt unhelpful early (usually a component-skill gap).

Practical tips: recommended only after finishing Fundamentals 2; use as a crutch when tired/low-mood — if you have good energy and already use AIM/TLS, do the full technique. Good in pre-study; Justin often does only steps 1–2 before writing articles/literature reviews. About the same effort as unwinding with a puzzle/chess/painting — do it in that relaxed mind space.

## When to Skip Mapping: Napkin Maps & Cognitive Offloading
Note-taking exists to offload cognition and facilitate thinking. If the conceptual load is low (few concepts you can hold in your head), you can skip formal mapping; use throwaway "napkin" scribbles to think, and keep a reference map only for dense, interrelated knowledge you'll build on repeatedly (Clinic 42).

Key principles:
- Note-taking = cognitive offloading. If your brain has no trouble holding the information, there's no need to offload it onto notes.
- **Proof-based maths**: a theorem is a super-linear series of steps that doesn't fit a map; few concepts used in near-infinite ways, so a rigid structure isn't needed. The hard part is understanding/re-deriving the steps, not structuring nodes.
- **Napkin maps**: scribble to track ideas, prove things yourself, write questions; throw away (or fold up and forget) once synthesised. Keep only a mini-map/Anki card if a visualisation is genuinely worth retaining. Justin napkin-maps literature reviews/research, then folds it up — the purpose was to help his brain work through the knowledge, not to keep a reference.
- Contrast: dense, interrelated conceptual knowledge you'll refer to again and again → commit to a full reference mind map.
- If mapping feels easy because there are only ~3 concepts AND that's the whole scope → don't flesh it out. But if the topic is actually bigger and you have capacity → EXPAND THE SCOPE (without going beyond the topic). For dense subjects (biology) commit to a full map and challenge yourself with more load; for maths, expanding scope ventures into a different topic where one map doesn't make sense.
- **Offloading tempo**: offloading = dumping information from working memory onto notes so it leaves the mind unprocessed ("in your sight, out of mind"). Mind mapping while simplifying processes far more than typing/linear writing — it is not pure offloading. Early learners: don't worry about offload timing; from ~Ascent 1, attend to when/how you offload. Recommended default early: hold information in mind a bit before writing (Clinic 16).

Common mistakes: spending too much effort structuring nodes for proof-based maths instead of comprehending the proofs; making a beautiful reference map for low-density material you'll never revisit; treating "skip mapping" as a blanket rule; keeping scope tiny when the topic is broad.

## Maintaining & Extending Maps
During the main learning event, consume resources properly and add information to your pre-study frame with purpose, grouping and relationships at every step; clean up later as interleaved retrieval and supplement with other techniques.

Technique (map-based learning):
1. Consume the textbook/guide/video in depth (you can't skip consuming information).
2. Add new info to the map: think where it belongs, connect it to the bigger picture, group it, consider relationships at every step.
3. Keep it simple; use visualisations where wordy or forget-prone.
4. Rearrange/reorganise as needed; messy maps are normal for beginners/intermediates.
5. Later (e.g. weekend), clean up the map — a form of interleaved retrieval; if messy, find a better grouping.
6. Supplement: teach an imaginary student, create hard practice questions, use a question bank + your own answer sheet, study-group methods, and flashcards for details that don't fit the map (parallel microlearning — see [memorisation](memorisation.md), [revision](revision.md)).

Key principles:
- Time saved isn't in consuming info (double-speed/faster reading save only ~30–60 min/session) but in not forgetting — avoiding entire days of relearning.
- Think relationships BEFORE committing info to the page (create a place for it to belong), not arrows retrospectively dodging text blocks. Good grouping → clean relationships; poor grouping → cross-map arrow mess.
- **Retrospective integration**: new information added to an existing map must go through the full encoding process again — inquiry, exploration, relational integration — not just be appended, or it stays isolated. Woven-fiber analogy: don't lay a new string on top of the fabric; weave it in. Warehouse-shelf analogy: decide whether the shelving must change, what moves, where it belongs. Run a mini series of inquiry/exploration exactly as if learning it fresh (Clinic 14).
- The Shoot map is the bulk of learning; Skin later cleans and expands it. A skinned map should be clean (no overlapping arrows/unknown intersects) and fuller (interleaving should have added gaps); if it looks too empty, suspect weak interleaved retrieval (Clinic 25).
- Re-encoding poorly-learned material: if you never found relationships/chunked it, you never learned it — treat it as new and run the technique again (Clinic 27).

Common mistakes: adding info first, relating/grouping afterwards; expecting a clean map immediately; skipping consumption; just drawing the new node onto the map without re-processing.

## Note-Taking Hyperfocus: Strip the Framework
Some students equate "learning" with the physical act of note-taking/mind-mapping and hyperfocus on the notes until it interferes with (or worsens) understanding. Fix: practice the cognitive processes WITHOUT the note-taking framework (Clinic 15).

Key principles:
- May be excessive germane load on the note-taking mechanics (a contested construct); a habit may have formed linking note-taking to a fixed sequence that holds learning back — strip the baggage and run the pure cognitive process.
- Inadequate interleaved retrieval funnels you into the note-taking pathway — interleaving itself exercises the same learning processes.

Technique (for Ascent 2/3-level students with solid technique):
1. Experiment with microlearning and priming/pre-study WITHOUT writing notes.
2. Use Whole-Part-Whole if learned — it interleaves the SKILL of encoding: same pathways, completely different approach, breaking the fixed habit (see [revision](revision.md)).
3. Increase interleaved retrieval diligence.
4. Get exposure to the thinking processes outside the strict note-taking framework.

Common mistakes: believing no notes = no learning; letting note-taking polish consume the cognitive load meant for encoding. Not a universal prescription — aimed at higher-stage students.

## Beyond Studying: Books, Articles & Unstructured Resources
Every course technique applies identically to any information medium — "once it enters your brain it's just data"; what you do with it is up to you (Clinic 15).

- **Books/audiobooks**: for retention goals, treat them as information and apply the full system. For fiction read for enjoyment, just read; don't annotate — enjoyment reading is not a learning-efficiency problem (you wouldn't ask for a 2-minute "efficient" version of a movie). Clarify the goal (retain vs enjoy) first (Clinic 15).
- **Scientific journal articles**: an advanced skill — gather many relevant articles at once (e.g. 20 open), frame out overlapping key frames/themes first (can take >1 hour), then fill in using your normal techniques. For pure learning: build a mind map from that frame. For writing/literature reviews: write the synthesised review directly as you read (you'll use it anyway) (Clinic 22).
- **Unstructured resources** (1000-page textbook, Google, no curriculum): overwhelm is a SYMPTOM; lack of relevance is the DISEASE. Always START with relevance (prior knowledge), then SCOPE for relevance and repeat. Skim for parts that feel relevant (~50 dense pages in 7–9 min); hypothesise a keyword's meaning from prior knowledge, read ~15 s to confirm; collect ~10–15 keywords on a mini mind map; repeat passes; when you plateau, scope other resources. A wrong hypothetical map corrected later is ~100× better than building from nothing (hyper-correction). This is pre-study's cognitive mechanism (Clinic 35). See [aiming](aiming.md), [professional-learning](professional-learning.md).
- **Microlearning system** (tight schedules): cycle Prime → Map → Explore → Dive → Consolidate in discrete short intervals (as short as 5 min). Explore extracts the 3–4 main ideas + relationships in non-linear notes (any device, even a napkin); consolidate after EVERY dive (zoom out, connect new detail). Schedule one big consolidated retrieval session because initial encoding is fragmented. Offload facts you don't need memorised to a PKM/second brain. Not better than extended sessions — a way to make do (see [professional-learning](professional-learning.md), [time-management](time-management.md)).
- **Clinical system** (medically oriented): build a patient flow (knowledge schema + patient's journey and variations), not a diagnostic algorithm (precision decision tool). Chunk early nodes by importance and thought process ("symptom pattern", "pain distribution") rather than binary decisions ("motor affected yes/no"); decisions become branches off the chunk. Pull back hyper-specific classification details to flashcards; branch early and often. After the first 5–10 flows, much less new content appears; shared commonalities become anchor points for first-principles thinking (see [professional-learning](professional-learning.md)).
