# Professional & Clinical Learning
> Adapting the core learning system to working life — where you direct your own relevance, learn in micro-pockets or from patients, and are judged on knowledge mastery rather than fact recall.

## Overview
The cognitive engine is identical to the student course ([bear-hunter-system](bear-hunter-system.md), [aiming](aiming.md), [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md), [revision](revision.md)); what changes is the wrapper — **what** you learn, **when**, and **why**. Professionals carry uncorrected school-era habits, need far less rote memorisation, and must demonstrate higher-order mastery. This note covers the adaptation principles, direction-setting for self-study, the two named professional systems (microlearning and clinical) plus their hybrid, clinical mapping and attachment strategy, and the supporting social/research skills. Two rules govern everything here: relevance comes **before** learning, and every system presupposes core competency — if a system feels impossible, the gap is in the fundamentals, not the system.

## Learning for Professionals — Principles & Adaptations
The fundamental cognitive processes stay the same; professionals adapt those principles and add niche techniques.

- **Remove barriers, don't build workarounds.** School/university learning mistakes rarely get corrected; professionals build workarounds around the same root issues ("the same problems, a different flavour"). Workarounds prevent immediate limitation, but removing the barrier entirely dramatically increases efficiency. Beliefs about learning built up over years of experience may not be true — re-examine them.
- **Mastery over recall.** Professionals rarely need large volumes of immediately retrievable facts (they can look things up). Senior roles expect expertise: critical thinking, first principles, dissecting and applying concepts across nuanced instances. Low-level recall is of limited value at work — "no one cares if you can recall a few facts".
- **Functional learning ≠ exam learning.** You learn for a purpose, so you encode more naturally and do not need every detail (~80% functional coverage vs 90%+ for an exam) (Clinic 22). Needing more lower-order recall is a disadvantage: it is more work.
- **Same processes at every age.** Young professionals and high-school students use identical cognitive learning processes; the main difference is professionals' optimised apps — "a different flavour of vanilla". Everything in the course applies (Clinic 15). Medicine is not a special exception (~20% of students are medical/doctors) (Clinic 28).
- **Learning-to-learn is the most transferable professional strength** — versatile across roles and teams, consistently outputting high value (Justin's strongest skill).
- **Match techniques to the knowledge balance.** Rate the domain's declarative↔procedural balance (1–10) before choosing techniques; system fit is highest for declarative-heavy domains, still good to ~50/50, with reducing returns beyond. Where the declarative component is tiny (dance, woodworking, powerlifting), the full course is overkill — go straight to interleaved practice. See [learning-science](learning-science.md) for the balance framework and the technique-design workshop (Clinics 28, 29, 36, 39).
- **Think like a practitioner while a student.** Progressive curricula integrate early, frequent clinical application; if yours doesn't, do it yourself — versatile, fluent knowledge is a competitive advantage in clinical years (theatre, ED) without last-minute re-revision. The anatomy deep dive in [aiming](aiming.md) models this (integration frameworks: related structures, embryology, presentation, examination, management).

Common mistakes: relying on workarounds instead of fixing root processes; assuming exam-style recall of everything is required; mismatching technique order to the domain's knowledge balance.

## Professional Outcomes: Studying vs Learning & Interleaving as Capital
You can pass degrees by cramming, but the cost is paid later. Effective study emulates on-the-job learning's relevance so you arrive competent with residual knowledge (Clinic 28).

- **Separate studying from learning.** Cramming gets you through, but weak techniques don't facilitate the thinking needed to retain — compensation happens somewhere (years of catch-up in the profession). "My dad crammed through med school and is a fine doctor": he likely spent far more time learning on the job. On-the-job learning works better because relevance and experience put the brain in an optimal state; the course techniques replicate (even exceed) that relevance during study.
- **Define a good outcome.** Competent from day one, minimal catch-up, no crazy hours, life intact — not merely "did they pass". Many professionals live work-study-work-study with no balance, spending all free time on training exams; some can't manage it and take time off. Justin completed a full-time master's part-time while running a business, spending ~1/8–1/10 the time others did. Opportunity cost matters more than the pass.
- **Residual knowledge.** Run spaced interleaved retrieval until the exam, or as long as you want the knowledge. With high-quality initial encoding, residual knowledge at clinical years is still high and clinical practice naturally interleaves it thereafter. For other degrees (e.g. education), plan how to keep interleaving professionally relevant material. See [revision](revision.md).
- **Interleaving as capital.** Don't just retrieve to maintain memory — make the OUTPUT valuable: write reports/commentaries to publication standard, get published in editorials/magazines/media. A few extra hours turns revision into employability and professional credibility. University is career training: "professional life 0.5", not "high school 2.0".
- **Front-load in batches** where your schedule allows: study a whole topic/semester up front, then maintain it through natural use and interleaved retrieval. Justin's master's: 1–2 weeks at the start of each semester to finish ALL studying, batching by paper (3–4 days each) — afterwards only assignments remain. Batch size must respect technique capacity and overload limits. Details in [time-management](time-management.md) (Clinic 28).
- The most efficient way to cram is to use the course techniques (Clinic 28); see [revision](revision.md).

## Detail-Heavy Fields: How Before What
For detail-heavy fields (medicine and equivalents), establish **how the information will be used and how it connects** — the "how to think" scaffold — before the details. Relational thinking plus chunking makes most "details" automatically memorable; only the residue needs rote learning (Clinic 15).

- Information serves either schema-building ("how to think about this topic") or problem-solving/application. Without the "how", information is isolated dots — Google has all the "what" but makes a poor doctor.
- **Context of use forces relational seeing:** anatomy matters because surgery and pain localisation depend on it; drug doses matter because different conditions need different doses. Meaning + structure → memorability.
- **There is no inherent "detail vs concept" label.** Until you have run the relating-and-chunking process you don't know what actually needs memorising; many small details become unforgettable because forgetting them breaks the topic's logic. Afterwards the rote volume drops "by a truckload" — put the remainder on flashcards and maintain with spacing/interleaving/retrieval ([memorisation](memorisation.md)).
- **It compounds.** Relationship search on a blank slate is slow and one-by-one at first, but existing chunks let you eliminate whole branches ("not related to this chunk, so not to its children") — it gets faster and faster.
- **Skipping it is "shooting yourself in the foot three times":** more forgetting, more tedious repetitive study, no future speed — plus a forced, overloaded consolidation later (e.g. in front of a patient) when others assume you already think this way. Starting late in a high-volume career makes the relearn burden almost impossible.
- **Pace is a real constraint.** Medicine's culture is extreme-paced: multiple people assign learning daily without seeing your plate, and a day's expectations can equal months of curriculum — an impossible pace for ANY technique. PhD/professional pace is usually adjustable around milestones.

Steps: (1) before consuming, ask what the information is FOR and in what context you will use it; (2) learn it relationally (each piece → use-case and other pieces); (3) chunk the relationships; (4) flashcard whatever remains unabsorbed; (5) maintain with spacing, interleaving, retrieval.

Common mistakes: memorising lists (history questions, pathologies, microbiology, anatomy) without the use-context; abandoning the process because it is slow initially; accepting the expectation that definitions must be rote-learned (expectations are built on what people know, and rote is not the only way).

Tip: non-medical learners should substitute their domain's equivalent of "examining a patient" as the use-context anchor (Clinic 15).

## Relevance-First, Goal-Driven Self-Study
Ineffective self-study (rushing, skipping, no accountability) is usually not a resource-structure problem but a **process** problem: learning isn't directed. Flip the formula — identify relevant information FIRST, then learn only that (Clinic 35).

- **Ineffective:** learn → figure out relevance. **Effective:** identify relevance → learn only that. Analogy: random turns hoping to reach the mall vs picking the path first, then making the turns that get you there.
- Skipping happens because you don't see relevance and try to find it while learning. When you control what/why you learn, skipping makes no sense — each piece directly fills a felt gap.
- **More powerful outside a formal curriculum:** you control resources, endpoints and objectives. As an autodidact you don't need an external curriculum or learning objectives — you know why you're learning; decide what to learn, scout resources, extract the relevant learnings (Clinics 22, 23).
- **Professions test you for free.** Professional learning is tied to real life, so it is tested immediately and practically; students are tested weeks/months later and need extra retrieval techniques (interleaving table, [revision](revision.md)) (Clinic 35).
- **Unstructured resources** (1000-page textbooks, Google, courses with no curriculum/order/objectives) overwhelm because relevance is missing — overwhelm and lack of direction are symptoms; lack of relevance is the disease. Start from prior knowledge, skim for curiosity or a link to what you know, hypothesise keywords, build a disposable mini mind map, repeat passes, then scope other resources against a specific gap. Relevance compounds and a wrong hypothetical map corrected later beats building from nothing (hypercorrection). Full cycle in [learning-science](learning-science.md); pre-study mechanism in [aiming](aiming.md) (Clinic 35).
- **No material at all?** Blind mapping (build a map from a keyword list using only logic and prior knowledge) is especially useful for unstructured lifelong/professional learning — see [note-taking](note-taking.md) (Clinic 40).
- **Skills: set a one-week retrieval goal.** Start from a real-world goal, work backwards to the simplest authentic practice opportunity achievable within the first week, then chain goals. Full procedure in [learning-science](learning-science.md) (Clinic 32).
- **Endpoints.** Without a curriculum, rely on tested confidence plus real-life testing; use objectives where they exist (often only ~40% of available information is relevant, especially postgrad), reach ~80–85% and let interleaved retrieval target the rest rather than fine-tooth combing to 100% first. Example: Justin's AI/ML endpoint is holding a productive conversation with specialists to extract value — not perfect recall of a 1,000-page textbook. See [revision](revision.md) (Clinic 35).

Common mistakes: blaming unstructured resources for rushing/skipping when the process is undirected; consuming information then trying to justify its relevance; assuming you need a formal curriculum to self-study; chasing 100% coverage before testing.

Tip: apply relevance-first strictly and the accountability/rushing/skipping issues resolve at the root cause; pick testing methods from the interleaving table that fit your real-world context (Clinic 35).

## Procedural Skills & Technical Interviews
Procedural fluency (actually doing the thing) is not delivered by conceptual encoding alone: build the concepts with the core techniques, then add heavy interleaved practice. The course is not optimised for purely procedural outcomes — expect extra hustle (Clinics 22, 23).

- **Technical interviews** depend on the employer (FAANG differs). You need strong conceptual domain knowledge (main concepts, web of understanding) plus heavy interleaved practice for actually using algorithms — hundreds of questions for FAANG-style loops (Clinic 22).
- **Coding progression:** smaller, controlled, isolated interleaving soon after learning (~1 day), then project-based practice once fluent (~1 week). A big project immediately after initial learning produces too many errors to be efficient (Clinic 23). For project-based work use the interleaving table's procedural/declarative page (Clinic 27).
- **Wrap practice with thinking on both sides:** planning/priming before → execution → reflection/consolidation against your mental structure afterwards. This adds declarative depth to a procedural activity and exposes mistake trends and their root causes (Clinic 39).
- **Raise the order by comparison.** Clinical auscultation is ~8/10 procedural and typically practised at order 1–2/5; asking "how is this sound similar/different to another patient's, given the anatomy, attributes and comorbidities?" makes it declarative-consolidated, higher-order diagnostic reasoning (Clinic 39). Programming: compare another way to achieve the same function (costs/benefits), or how structure changes across frameworks, client priorities or tight deadlines (Clinic 39).
- Even heavily procedural skills have essential declarative content: for public speaking the skill is executing the speech, and the declarative knowledge is *how to be an effective speaker* — not the words (Clinic 39).

Common mistakes: expecting the course alone to prepare procedural interviews; jumping into a large project before fluency; practising execution with no planning or reflection (mistakes repeat without a diagnosed cause). See [learning-science](learning-science.md) for designing your own interleaving technique and [exam-technique](exam-technique.md) for error feedback.

## Professional Learning Systems — Choosing by Environment
A learning system is a multi-component entity composed of several techniques. The right system depends on your professional environment, and all of them presuppose competency in the core cognitive processes.

- **Efficiency comes from chunking and tolerating relatively high cognitive load.** If a system feels very difficult, your core cognitive processes are probably underdeveloped — return to the core course, build them, then plug back in ("tweak it and lubricate the joints"). Early struggle is a prerequisite gap, not a failure.
- **Match the system to the environment:**
  - *Heavily experiential* — daily, directly relevant learning experiences (clinical medicine: the patients themselves carry what you need to learn) → clinical system.
  - *Slower, more removed academic learning* — exam study relates to the field but not to daily tasks (financial advisory, accountancy) → microlearning system layered on conventional study.
  - *Very tight schedules* (competitive athletes training 30–40 h/week, multiple jobs) → microlearning-dominant. Spend scarce focused blocks PREPARING micro-learning resources and opportunities for the week (e.g. 3–4 of 6 Saturday hours) plus 2–3 hours of deep big-picture encoding, then exploit ~1 h/day of pockets (Clinic 34).
- **Personalise at the principle level** once core techniques are unconsciously competent: drive up interleaving, microlearning or relational emphasis to fit subject, season and life constraints; multipass is a ready-made adjustment for sprint/burst study. Don't copy a canonical schedule verbatim — change the principle weighting, keep the fundamentals. See [course-guide](course-guide.md) and [revision](revision.md).

## Microlearning System
A system built on multiple discrete short intervals (as short as 5 minutes) instead of extended sessions, cycling **Prime → Map → Explore → Dive → Consolidate**, potentially several cycles per day. It is the most common professional system and very flexible.

**Positioning.** Not better than extended sessions — a way to make do, and it can be layered on top of any existing system (deep weekend study + weekday microlearning). Use it to build logic, conceptual understanding and mastery; offload facts you don't need memorised (see next section).

**Why it works.** The time pressure of discrete bursts sharpens concentration and may temporarily boost encoding/working memory — an effect not seen in extended sessions. Time constraints force very simple chunks, and deeper learning is already framed inside a chunk, so the organisational structure is inherent and mastery comes faster. Pocket-matching and the deadline/urgency mechanism are covered in [time-management](time-management.md) and [memorisation](memorisation.md).

**Two governing principles.**
1. **Always cycle between stages.** Prime only ~1–2 hours' worth of extended-study material at a time, so exploration stays small (a few key concepts, not a whole textbook).
2. **Schedule retrieval.** Initial encoding is fragmented, so plan one big consolidated retrieval session (e.g. 4–5 interleaved hours on a weekend) or several smaller ones to avoid retrieval failure. Skip this only if daily work already retrieves constantly (language learners applying the language immediately; small business owners using what they learn ~20 times a day). If a week passes without real use, add a session.

**Steps.**
1. **Prime** — identify exactly what you need to learn (curriculum-bound or on-the-job). Use a list of questions (Justin's favourite), keywords, objectives/outcomes or KPIs.
2. **Map** — identify all available resources, what information each holds, and how you will navigate them. Preparation only; no studying yet.
3. **Explore** — extract the 3–4 main ideas and their relationships; build the logic layer quickly in non-linear, relational notes (iPad, laptop touchpad, paper, reMarkable, scrap paper — even a napkin). Representing relationships in linear text becomes thousands of words once moderately complex, so non-linear notes are strongly preferred ([note-taking](note-taking.md)). Loop this stage across several 5-minute sittings until the main ideas and connections are clear.
4. **Dive** — pick any one relevant chunk/point (the starting point doesn't matter) and learn it more deeply, adding to the same non-linear notes. Repeat until fleshed out.
5. **Consolidate after EVERY dive** — zoom out to the whole framed logic, connect the new detail to other areas, create more relationships. Sessions hours apart disrupt the narrative and encoding, so re-consolidation is essential. Then either re-prime (if outcomes/resources changed) or jump back into explore/dive. Keep looping big picture ↔ dive.
6. Once everything primed is covered, **prime the next topic**.
7. **Rare memorisation variation** — during dive/consolidate, add must-memorise facts to flashcards and run them per your algorithm. This can replace a separate PKM, since the flashcards hold the information ([memorisation](memorisation.md)).

**Example day:** prime 10 min on the train → map 3 min waiting for a lift → explore 20 min at lunch → first dive 4 hours later while waiting for the water to boil.

**Natural retrieval pro tip:** teach what you learned to a colleague/junior or present it in a meeting — retrieval practice during work hours that also helps the team. Ask your manager for a recurring slot (e.g. an hour every couple of weeks). Repackage into a NEW structure rather than regurgitating your notes; the repackaging standard is in [revision](revision.md) (Clinics 38, 39).

Common mistakes: diving into every item sequentially without consolidating between dives; priming too much material for the available micro-intervals; skipping retrieval sessions when daily work does NOT naturally retrieve; trying to run resource-heavy deep study in a 5-minute gap; sacrificing sleep to fit in study.

## Offloading Facts: PKM / Second Brain
A second brain stores knowledge outside your head so your first brain doesn't carry it. Professionals need far less immediately retrievable fact volume, so offload aggressively and spend cognitive effort on logic and mastery.

- Any searchable store works: Notion, Obsidian, Roam Research, a Google Doc or a spreadsheet. Justin's stack: **Roam Research** (core), **EndNote or Zotero** (references), **Connected Papers** (literature mapping) (Clinic 14).
- Pen-and-paper second brains are insanely difficult; software is the practical medium (Clinic 14).
- **Notion is not recommended for studying or scheduling** — notes are locked linear, and scheduling in it combines the rigidity of time management with the upkeep of task management. Its student marketing is a dependency funnel, not learning science (Clinic 14). Note-taking app guidance: [note-taking](note-taking.md).
- If must-memorise facts already live in flashcards, you may not need a separate PKM at all.
- Elaborate app setup feels productive but isn't: productivity is getting stuff done, not writing about getting stuff done (Clinic 14).

## Clinical Learning System
An advanced, niche system purpose-built for clinicians (doctors, residents studying training exams, clinical medical students). It maximises learning from practice by letting **patients dictate what you study**, mapping each patient's journey with its variations, and exploiting the naturally high interleaved retrieval of clinical work. It is a strategy for WHAT and WHEN to learn; the actual studying uses core-course techniques ([bear-hunter-system](bear-hunter-system.md) BHS/Hipshot, [revision](revision.md) multipass).

**Three unique aspects of clinical learning.**
1. **Patients are a learning resource** — every interaction teaches (patient-facing specialties; less so radiology/pathology). Your examination and history skills must be good enough to actually elicit the signs, or the resource is wasted.
2. **The volume is massive**, so you can be relatively undiscriminating — almost anything related to your patients will be needed eventually. This is where you exercise order control ([learning-science](learning-science.md)).
3. **Naturally high interleaved retrieval** — common conditions and their variations recur 20–100 times in practice. Studying high-recurrence topics gets spaced interleaved retrieval for free, and each interaction yields encoding + retrieval + re-encoding value.

**The perspective switch: order control is patient-dictated.** Requires high core competency (chunking, relational thinking, non-linear note-taking, priming — at least Ascent 3, ideally Basecamp; see [course-guide](course-guide.md)). Treat it as something to work towards and implement components of progressively; "studying" inside it means encoding sensory information at maximum efficiency with techniques you are already fluent in, mixing and matching principles rather than following one fixed technique (Clinic 24). The goal is NOT to cover every branch: cover what is most relevant, important and common; less common points get covered peripherally over time. **Common things are common** — high-yield recurring flows prime you for tomorrow's cases instead of rare "party trick" diseases you will never see again.

**Mini-to-one learning.** Each patient mapped with 3–4 extra variations emulates 3–4× the patient exposure, building consultant/attending-level wide pattern recognition far faster than years of experience alone. Branches are transferable: learning the inflammatory process for one post-op complication applies to every patient with inflammation.

**Steps.**
1. See a patient; list the pertinent learning points (problems, diagnoses, pathologies, examination findings). Early on, "everything about this patient" is fine. If you are shadowing rather than treating, discuss next steps, differentials and management with the supervising doctor to complete the picture.
2. Pick the highest-yield point (most common, most life-threatening, most examinable). The exact choice doesn't matter — just pick something relevant; it builds from there.
3. That evening, **brain-dump a map of the patient journey**: e.g. history → investigations → management → follow-up. Depth matches your level (medical student: etiology, epidemiology, presenting complaints, history elements, pertinent closing questions, investigations, differentials; trainee: deeper). Write whatever you know; blanks are fine.
4. **Branch every variation**: presenting symptom A vs B; CT positive vs negative; blood test +/−; post-op infection typical/atypical, resistant/non-resistant; impaired-healing factors. Mapping the obvious variations can take a couple of hours — that is good learning and sets up interleaving.
5. **Learn ONE branch per day** (the weakest or most relevant), staying with the learning point ~1–2 days. Half a branch in a day is fine. Branching naturally prompts review of "boring" basics (cytokine pathways, antibiotic profiles, pharmacology, coagulation cascade) wrapped in clinical context.
6. **Pick a new patient (~twice a week)** whose condition overlaps at least one existing branch; build the same map; find where the journeys intersect (similar recovery complication, anaesthetic risk, etiology, comorbidity); learn the main branch plus a couple more over the following days.
7. **Repeat.** After 3–4 weeks, heavily intersecting areas become strong knowledge; after 1–2 months, common conditions and high-yield variations are covered, mapping is much faster, and navigating permutations becomes fluent.
8. **Near exams, sweep the curriculum** to catch the tiny points the "big open net" missed (the system covers ~80–90% of them) and consolidate.

**Prime for tomorrow.** If you know tomorrow's surgery, think about the patient type and flow, learn a little about the procedure, then expand the flow and variations later that day. Seeing a mapped variation the next day is review/re-encoding, not new learning. Walking into clinical experience unprimed wastes 50–70% of its learning value — exactly like attending a lecture without pre-study ([aiming](aiming.md)).

**Timeline expectations.** The first weeks are extremely overwhelming (massive webs, many gaps) — start anywhere and let it carry you; each new patient adds proportionally less uncertainty as patterns repeat. Students typically cover student-level content within ~2 weeks: behind for 2 weeks, at peers' pace by weeks 3–5, then ahead. Without a system, the same material is relearned yearly (Justin reviewed the brachial plexus every year for seven years).

**Examples.** Surgery: "if we cut here, what if slightly left — nerve? artery? why blunt-dissect here?" — build the same variation web to stay engaged in theatre. General surgery placement: diverticulitis, appendicitis, cholecystitis are high-yield deep dives because you will see them again tomorrow. Medication dosages become memorable via permutations (what if overdose/underdose?), deepening pharmacology/pharmacokinetics; similar mechanisms reveal similar side-effect profiles.

**Exam preparation.** Encode first, then use question banks as interleaving — smashing through thousands of USMLE questions before building a network is the common trap and gives poor results. If you get most questions wrong, the deficit is conceptual: learn theory first. See [revision](revision.md) (Clinic 23).

Common mistakes: chasing every branch (inefficient, overwhelming — let relevance guide depth); studying rare diseases because they feel important, then never seeing them again; arriving unprimed; letting consultants' scattered "you should study this" suggestions overwhelm you — the system forces one learning point/patient flow at a time.

## Clinical × Microlearning Hybrid
An advanced combination for on-call/acute weeks or time-poor clinicians (e.g. a urology trainee who was also a competitive cyclist): run the clinical system inside hospital downtime instead of forcing exhausted evening study.

- **Prime** → the patient plus a very basic map.
- **Map** → identify which areas of that flow to work on.
- **Explore/Dive** → single branch-points in 5-minute bursts: one management option; a variation of that option; how a comorbidity changes it; a different severity alternative; "what if this test came back negative — how does my reasoning change?"
- **Consolidate** as usual between bursts; rest at home, or use home time only to prime/map for the next day.
- Do the bursts while relatively energetic (lifts, on-call gaps, post-round coffee, an uninteresting round presentation). The microlearning pressure helps encoding, so this beats forced late-night study.

Caveats: quality is lower than with dedicated time; it is tiring and not sustainable long-term; it requires mastery of every principle plus good COBES to fine-tune ([learning-science](learning-science.md), [habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md)).

## Patient Flow Mapping (vs Diagnostic Algorithms)
Build a **patient flow** — a knowledge schema plus the patient's journey and its variations — not a **diagnostic algorithm**, which is a precision decision tool.

- **Algorithm:** focused on reaching a diagnosis; very specific binary branches and thresholds. Good for decision-making, limited for knowledge structuring, and it drags you into lower-order memorisation of minute details.
- **Flow:** follows the journey/chronology — what is happening now, variations of what could have happened, what happens next (management, complications, post-op), and the underlying pathophysiology. Students often see only the middle or end of a journey but still need the whole thing. This staples "science-y" theory (cell lineages, genetics, physiology) to a real case, making abstract material relevant.
- **Pull back on precision detail** (e.g. "relief <2×/year vs >2×/year", exact fracture-classification thresholds): keep vague signposts ("if it's here it means this") and put genuinely needed specifics on flashcards. Keep splits that change what you DO (e.g. x-ray/ultrasound vs splint).
- **Push branching EARLIER.** Flesh out branches that currently die (e.g. "sensory only") until relevance fades, then let them fade; revisit and refine half-done branches later. Previously fully-fleshed branches get revised from new perspectives.
- **Importance-based early chunks.** Early chunks must be broad categories reflecting your thinking, not binary decisions: "symptom pattern" or "pain distribution" (motor only / sensory only / both / future variants) instead of "motor affected yes/no"; "dermatome/myotome" or "medial/ventral/lateral" instead of "median distribution". Decisions become branches off the chunk. Broad early chunks leave room to build and re-chunk later.
- **Diagnostic-algorithm thinking is detectable:** strong linearity and very specific wording in EARLY chunks (downstream chunks may legitimately be specific). It usually means insufficient consideration of variations.
- **Convergence is the payoff.** After the first 5–10 flows, much less new content appears. Shared commonalities become anchor points for first-principles thinking: you can construct a semi-flow on the spot for an unfamiliar presentation, and you can skip tests that won't change the journey. Later learning the formal algorithm has more relevance points because you hypothesised your own.
- **Illness scripts and type 1/type 2 thinking.** Consultants rely on wide, instinctive pattern recognition (type 1) — an illness script stores the pattern, natural history, decisions and management options. Juniors/students use effortful hypothesis generation (type 2). Never rely on type 1 alone (errors, misses, false judgments), but building pattern recognition and scripts is the fastest route to baseline competency.
- **The clarity goal:** "I was memorising do-this-this-this; now I see I am just testing whether X happens, and these are the steps." Achieved only through pattern recognition across multiple patient journeys.

Steps: (1) draft the flow as a journey with hypotheses and what-ifs (one student used blue text for hypotheses) — more organised and enjoyable than a linear list of signs; (2) re-chunk binary early decisions into broader, thought-reflecting categories; (3) branch early and often, carrying each branch until relevance fades; (4) chunk carefully so the spatial layout doesn't collapse into crossing flows ([note-taking](note-taking.md), [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md)).

Worked example (orthopedics hand pain): "hand pain → motor affected yes/no → median distribution" was re-chunked to "symptom pattern" and broader distribution categories, opening branches for trauma, burns, motor-neurone vs neuromuscular-junction conditions, and prompting questions like "would I even need an x-ray if only sensory is affected?"

Common mistakes: linear lists of history/exam signs; letting precision-oriented diagnostic detail dominate at the expense of journey and variations; binary, over-specific early chunks that can never branch again.

## Chunking Clinical Content
Clinical content is chunked like any other content — the general method lives in [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md). The clinical deltas:

- **No single right model for every condition.** Different conditions suit different models (severity for one, shared symptomology for another). **Shared symptomology is highly recommended**, especially with many diseases, because it mirrors practice: you see symptoms and signs, then diagnose. Find similarities/differences between related conditions and how they affect your reasoning (Clinic 22).
- **Break the frame first.** Framing bias is the biggest blocker: interpreting a case inside the label you were handed ("here's someone with appendicitis" before the symptoms) prevents independent chunking. Take the raw keywords — symptoms, investigation findings, management options — ask why each is important and how it fits, find similarities/differences and trends, then chunk on those. Patterns shift learning from memorising to understanding (e.g. inflammatory + not viral + possibly infectious + pain → assess inflammation, culture, then antibiotics/steroids/analgesia) (Clinic 22). Medical students are the most framing-bias-prone group because content is taught in a fixed way (Clinic 28).
- **Meaning before lists.** Patients present isolated facts (sore throat, sick since Thursday, chest pain, high BP), not diagnoses — so practise creating meaning FROM isolated facts, not regurgitating label→list. Reason mechanistically (cardiac pain receptors sit in the lining, not the muscle, so a heart attack can be painless); interrogate the detail (what TYPE of chest pain, WHERE, what makes it better/worse); then chunk benignly (pain-related vs breathing-related symptoms, 3+3, instead of six isolated items). Rote is the LAST step. Meaning-first is what makes flashcards accurate and therefore sustainable (Clinic 16).
- **Never encode into the institution's template.** An attending's diagnosis schema (e.g. syncope) is a retrieval format, not a knowledge structure: encode the knowledge YOUR optimal way, then spend ~15 minutes using their template as retrieval practice. Fluid knowledge retrieves in many ways; a grouped list is not a chunk map. Their bottom-level sub-chunking often doesn't match real clinical retrieval — signs/symptoms-based chunking is more strategic. The choice is "learn sub-par, forget, relearn yearly to please the attending" vs "learn properly + 15 min of template retrieval" (Clinic 18). See [learning-science](learning-science.md) for order control.
- **Algorithms and drug groups:** learn treatment principles conceptually first (algorithmic first-encoding takes ~10× longer), generate and compare candidate groupings, and layer structures where useful. Full procedure in [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md) (Clinics 28, 29).
- The core techniques were originally developed for clinical medicine; difficulty usually means the raw independent-chunking skill needs work, not that medicine is different (Clinic 22).

## Clinical Attachments & Progress Tests
Extract maximum value from mandatory attachments: pre-study a surface baseline so every clinical day becomes advanced spaced interleaved retrieval, master the current attachment deeply, and optimise encoding for cognition rather than for the test.

- **Conflicting demands:** today's patient vs the supervisor's suggestions (sometimes well-directed, sometimes rare "cool" diseases that matter for senior trainees, not you) vs the school curriculum/portal vs formal tests. Without a system, students waste mandatory attendance time — hiding in the library instead of engaging with patients, waiting for the day to end.
- **Every clinical day should provide relevant value that feeds into itself.** Load the clinical day with learning to offload outside-study pressure. Relevance is a function of prior knowledge and the connections you can form (higher-order thinking trained in the core course).
- **Pre-study the rotation** for a surface-level baseline of declarative knowledge; placements then become spaced interleaved retrieval, often multiple times per day — exactly like pre-studying before a lecture so you are not lost and barely keeping up ([aiming](aiming.md)).
- **Progress tests are cohort-based:** graded on trends and normal distributions/percentiles across the cohort, not single results. You are not expected to answer questions on attachments you haven't done. Scores commonly jump after general medicine/ED attachments because so many questions cluster there.
- **Never learn for the test.** Initial encoding is always cognitively optimised; only retrieval practice may balance cognitive and exam optimisation ([exam-technique](exam-technique.md)).

Steps: (1) pre-study a surface baseline of the rotation's common conditions; (2) run the clinical system on the patients you actually see and master the current attachment deeply; (3) don't chase extra attachments (ED, critical care) for exam coverage — you will have those placements later; (4) let question banks and teach-create-test be exam-aware while encoding stays cognitively optimal.

Common mistakes: splitting time across the current placement plus unrelated attachments to hedge for progress tests (dilutes exposure and interleaving, and forgetting the current attachment adds future work — it will be tested again in later progress tests); learning rare diseases because a supervisor is excited about them; sacrificing encoding quality to study "for the test".

Tip: the system makes even uninteresting attachments engaging as a learning process — you may not like the field, but the process is cognitively engaging, so you stop waiting for the day to end.

## Remembering Names
Encoding-based techniques for remembering names on the spot. Forgetting a name ~15 seconds after hearing it means it was never encoded out of working memory: short-term memory spans seconds to a couple of minutes, and encoding quality determines retention and decay rate. A name is just sensory data — give it "a place to belong" (relationships, structure) and the brain keeps it.

**Steps.**
1. **Hypothesise** before/while hearing the name: what type of name do they look like? ("Looks like a James" — they say "Kevin"). Generation plus hyper-correction both boost encoding, right or wrong.
2. **Compare and contrast** with someone you know of the same name (another Gabriel): how is this person similar/different? Builds relationships with prior knowledge in seconds, without awkward staring.
3. **Use the name multiple times** immediately and later ("Hi Gabriel, nice to meet you… thanks for that, Gabriel") — a little spaced retrieval.
4. **Alliterate with an observed trait:** "Golf Gabriel", "Tidy Tim", "Organized Oliver". Forces attentive observation of their character, doubles as a conversation starter ("do you play golf?"), and the cognitive effort increases encoding. Useful when meeting a group and you want confidence that you have everyone.
5. **If you forget, ask again immediately** — don't let it drag into awkwardness. To rescue someone who forgot YOUR name, pretend you forgot theirs ("it was Gabriel, right?") and re-introduce yourself.
6. **Foreign names:** ask how to spell and pronounce it properly, and whether it means anything. Shows respect for their heritage and creates extra associations/memory cues.

Common mistakes: low-level tricks like mentally "writing the name on their face" — time-consuming, fails when meeting many people at once, and inefficient; relying on a single memorisation trick instead of the underlying theory. Understand WHY it works so you can fix it yourself — "be the mechanic for your own brain".

Example: Justin's networking result — meet 20 people in 5 minutes, remember 16–17 names. People think more highly of you and feel taken seriously.

## Digesting Research Literature & Journal Articles
Articles are not presented for direct study, so even creating a basic frame from them can take over an hour. The fix is to frame across a COLLECTION, then fill in with your normal techniques (Clinic 22).

**Steps.**
1. Gather as many relevant articles as you think you need — Justin often has ~20 open at once.
2. Frame out the overlapping key frames/themes across them (expect >1 hour for raw articles).
3. Go through the articles and fill everything in using your normal techniques.
4. **For pure learning:** build a mind map from that frame. **For writing/literature reviews:** write the synthesised review directly as you read — you will use the passage anyway, so don't mind-map it first.

- **Never build understanding from one article.** Use at least 3–4 simultaneously, often 20–30 (Clinic 27). Starting inside one paper's constraints is framing bias.
- **Low domain knowledge?** Build a broad, slightly superficial baseline FIRST (systematic reviews, commentaries, books, overview articles), then progress through the layers. If a detailed version exists, a more basic version exists somewhere. A paper is just another information source: the same pre-study → layers technique applies unchanged — struggling usually means an earlier technique step is weak, not that "research reading" is special. See [aiming](aiming.md) (Clinic 29).
- Research produces an ever-growing web, not necessarily one ever-growing mind map — split maps as useful. At Basecamp your note-taking should flex structure without breaking technique ([note-taking](note-taking.md)).
- Screening/finding relevant articles is a separate scholarly skill.
- When sources conflict (common at PhD/professional level), judge information by testing how it fits your network rather than adjudicating "which source is true" — see [learning-science](learning-science.md) (Clinic 40).
- AI can help gather keywords and concise definitions, but must not outsource the thinking — see [aiming](aiming.md) (Clinics 36, 38, 40).

Common mistakes: studying a single article directly with no frame; underestimating how long framing from raw articles takes; mind-mapping a literature review you were going to write anyway.

## Everyday Professional Use: Meetings, Books & Isolated Material
Once the skills exist, you use them everywhere — most people simply don't have them yet (Clinic 27).

- **Meetings:** Justin mind-maps meetings with lawyers and investors (Clinic 27), and team business-strategy meetings (Clinic 20). Mind maps are the core encoding tool for any domain-knowledge content, even small pieces; skip only for truly tiny content ([note-taking](note-taking.md), [bear-hunter-system](bear-hunter-system.md)).
- **Non-fiction books are just an information source** — use the course techniques to learn them fast. Fiction read for enjoyment is not a learning-efficiency problem: just read, don't annotate ([note-taking](note-taking.md)) (Clinic 15).
- **Isolated material is hard to learn** because it has no relevance. Even a single article contains multiple concepts, so you can form a logic layer — but deliberately learn slightly OUT OF SCOPE to build authentic relationships and chunking opportunities; the extra volume is outweighed by the efficiency ([learning-science](learning-science.md)).
- Once mastered, the technique becomes default thinking and you need less explicit scaffolding — the same expertise effect that makes consultants learn fast (Clinic 27).
