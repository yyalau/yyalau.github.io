# Chunking, GRINDE Maps & Skin
> Chunking groups facts into larger units by shared patterns and importance; GRINDE is the six-point quality checklist for the resulting mind map; Skin is the consolidation pass that re-chunks a post-Shoot map into a GRINDE map, lifting learning to Evaluate with minimal rote memorisation.

## Overview
This note is the authoritative reference for how knowledge gets **grouped** (chunking, importance-based chunking, intuitive chunking), how map quality is **judged** (the GRINDE checklist and its audit), and how the post-Shoot map is **consolidated** (Skin — map cleanup plus the subject-specific variants for English, Maths and Languages). It also covers the chunking failure modes coaches diagnose (framing bias, pre-chunking, spider-webbing, information hierarchies), how to chunk awkward content (equations, linear processes, vocabulary, clinical lists), the worked chunkmapping demonstrations, and subject-specific chunking strategy for medicine and anatomy.

Position in the system: [aiming](aiming.md) generates the questions, [bear-hunter-system](bear-hunter-system.md) runs Aim → Shoot → Skin, and Shoot builds the first mind map ([note-taking](note-taking.md)). First-pass maps are chaotic because weighing and prioritising every relationship in one go is very hard — Skin exists to finish that job, moving learning from Apply/Analyse (Bloom 3–4) up to Evaluate (5). The map should end up holding ~90–95% of the topic; the ~5–10% of arbitrary details left over goes to flashcards and memorisation imagery ([memorisation](memorisation.md)). Re-skinning from pure recall days later doubles as high-value revision ([revision](revision.md)). The underlying memory science (Cave Theory, layers of learning, Bloom's levels, reverse causality, Kolb's cycle) lives in [learning-science](learning-science.md).

## Chunking
Grouping individual facts into larger units ("chunks") by finding patterns — similarities, common trends and differences — so the brain stores and retrieves fewer, better-connected units instead of many isolated ones. Chunking is the "G" in GRINDE and the core mechanism that makes mind maps efficient.

Why it works:
- Unchunked knowledge is isolated dots: 12 facts cost ~12 units of mental energy, are weakly connected, and the brain may wipe the whole set. Chunked into 3 chunks + 6 loose facts, you hold ~6 items (~7–8 energy units): a chunk costs slightly more than one unit but far less than its members stored separately.
- **Zip-file model**: a chunk compresses its members; accessing the chunk "extracts" all of them, so a 4-fact chunk is easier to hold than 4 separate facts.
- **Russian-doll / room-and-drawer model**: organisation inside organisation. A drawer with a stationery organiser beats a messy drawer; nesting chunks multiplies the effect (a zip inside a zip inside a zip). Knowing the room and the drawer is not enough — you still need the organiser tray (pencils left, stapler middle) = sub-chunking (Clinic 19).
- **Warehouse / Amazon-janitor model** (Clinic 11): information must be shelved meaningfully AS IT ARRIVES. Unorganised packages are cleared out by the "janitor" (forgetting) after ~20–30 minutes because retaining them is energy-inefficient; retrieving isolated data is slow AND tiring (the worker digging through piles — why pure flashcard drilling feels exhausting). **Organised ≠ meaningfully organised**: shelving by weight when packages ship by destination is still a mess at retrieval. Organise by how the information will need to be RETRIEVED. Read → understand → "throw it behind you" → memorise → next produces a disorganised pile you remember learning but cannot recall a week later.
- A mind map is a representation of how you are organising information in your head; a messy map means messy internal organisation — a chunking problem showing up on paper, not a drawing-technique problem. Past papers force retrieval-driven reorganisation (you move packages as questions demand), which is why they help — but waiting for them wastes all the intervening time; organise correctly from the start (Clinic 11).
- More relationships → higher retention and efficiency (this checks Cave Theory's pattern-forming box — [learning-science](learning-science.md)). The science is not fully settled; increased relationships is the likely mechanism, though chunking may be a separate psychological phenomenon. Chunking is not the same as analogous learning (using prior knowledge to understand new information); analogous learning may enable it, but the relationship is unclear (Clinic 29).
- Expert vs beginner: an expert explains a topic to a five-year-old as "really just this and this, and inside that, this"; a beginner recites a flat list of disconnected facts.
- Chunking does not delete information — it moves it deeper/later in the branching, so the map has fewer crossing arrows and reads more cleanly. More chunking → fewer chaotic arrows → clearer relationships → higher Bloom level and a large retention gain.
- The technique is identical across law, economics, medicine, maths and everything else — you do not need subject expertise to spot chunks. Relationships are discovered, not invented; all knowledge inherently has them.

Structural rules:
- **Trigger rule: roughly 4+ branches off any single node = a chunking opportunity.** Circle every such node and group its branches. Ascent 1+ learners should spot a 2–4 violation within ~0.5 seconds of seeing a map (Clinic 17).
- **2–4 rule / rule of four**: aim for 2, at most 4 items under any chunk or sub-chunk; even at four, try to reduce. Working memory struggles beyond ~2–3 items (the old "12–13 items" claim is outdated). Justin has almost never found >4 items that cannot be sub-chunked (Chunkmapping Demo). A node splitting six ways is under-chunked, not "detailed". Any topic, however huge, distils to 2–4 main points and their relationships — reduce detail, never cut the topic (Clinic 18).
- **Depth and quality are separate checks**: the 2–4 rule counts branches; the intuitive test (below) judges whether the division leverages prior knowledge. Run both when reviewing a map (Clinic 17).
- Sub-chunk by shared importance: six flat growth factors (temperature, pH, moisture, nutrients, waste removal, antibiotics/antiseptics) become three organised sub-chunks — enzyme control (temperature + pH), moisture/nutrients, and so on (Chunkmapping Demo).
- Nest chunks inside chunks; occasionally overlapping chunks are allowed when two genuine patterns exist (rare, but it adds a dimension to the relationships).
- Groups need not be official taxonomy — they are whatever makes sense in your mind. Multiple valid chunkings of the same material exist; what makes sense to you may differ from someone else. **Doing the organising process is what matters.**
- Scope must be big enough to chunk: a single lecture leaves too little material to compare (narrow scope syndrome); bigger topics are counterintuitively easier. Default to a whole topic/chapter ([aiming](aiming.md), [course-guide](course-guide.md)).

Purpose-driven chunking (Clinics 14, 23):
- Chunks and relationships are recursively intertwined: many similar relationships between concepts form a chunk; relationships between chunks link chunks; sub-concept relationships form sub-chunks.
- **The best chunking depends on your purpose.** Pants, shoes, jacket, socks, t-shirt → "clothing" (function) for daily life, but "non-fragile items" for a shipping company — both valid. A pen stylus + whiteboard marker → "writing utensils" (both write) OR "small & light" (fit a pencil case when moving house). There is no single canonical chunking; nuance and experience decide what fits how YOU will use the information. Comparing the options IS the learning.
- Early stage: just build the habit of identifying reasonable chunks; refine purpose-fit later. Importance-based chunking is taught near the end of Technique Training / Ascent 1 — don't confuse yourself by applying it before basic chunking is competent (Clinic 14).

Foundational grouping (Fundamentals level):
1. Group keywords by what feels like it belongs together — a hypothesis, revisable.
2. Label each group with simple, intuitive words (problems, outcomes, skills, planning) that leverage prior knowledge. A label may reuse a member's term if intuitive ("self-management", "spacing"); avoid brand-new jargon.
3. Map relationships; reorganise, break, redistribute and create groups as understanding grows (e.g. split "research and improvement" into research + practice).
4. Apply the 2–4 rule: if a group has 6 items, subgroup (self-management → emotions [motivation, discomfort, procrastination] vs actions [time/task management, focus, habits]).
5. Add detail underneath the frame simply.

Key principles and tips:
- Grouping activates higher-order thinking (comparing, similarities/differences, how groups affect each other). **The cognitive load of figuring out the chunks IS the learning** — do not avoid that discomfort (desirable difficulty, [learning-science](learning-science.md)).
- Think relationships and groupings through in your head BEFORE writing; retrospective arrows weaving around text blocks are the wrong order of higher-order thinking. Good groups make relationships clean; poor groups force messy cross-map arrows. A messy, hard-to-follow map is a grouping/spacing problem, NOT a visuals problem.
- Reorganising is normal and expected; sometimes break the structure or restart for a better grouping. Early chunking is not doomed to be wrong — the exploration built into the process yields chunks that are slightly off, not massively, even with little prior knowledge.
- Keep raising the challenge as skill grows: when chunking 6→3 points becomes easy, push 3→even fewer with minimal effort. If it feels easy you are not getting maximum learning; cognitive load should never really drop (full system preview). Once you see how to form relationships you "can't unsee it" — but calibrating "good" to your beginner standard is false confidence.
- Finding relationships is where learning BEGINS, not ends: many individual arrows without grouping/prioritising will not stick. You must cut, evaluate, compare and group the raw material ([learning-science](learning-science.md), Clinic 30).

Common mistakes: leaving 5+ branches on one node (a "hedgehog" map of spikes — more memorable than linear notes but far below standard); long arrows dodging across the page; **using an acronym/mnemonic to "chunk" 6 items — that is memorisation, not chunking, and mnemonics must never carry the main chunks of a topic** (Clinic 17); assuming you must know the subject before chunking it; deleting detail instead of nesting it; stopping at the first level of chunking; copying as-taught groups (passive, no cognitive load); polishing colours/visuals while a 2–4 violation remains.

## Importance-Based Chunking
Building chunk structures from answers to "why is this important?" so that groups and their organisation reflect evaluated importance rather than the order or headings in which material was presented. **A chunk is a group of things important for the same reason.** This is the course's default chunking method at every stage, including pre-study (Clinic 29).

The chain (order matters): **questions → relationships → chunks → non-linear notes.** Broad questions reveal relationships; relationships reveal how ideas group; chunks are then represented. You cannot improve chunk quality directly without improving the questions and relationships upstream. The exact sequence: keywords (unchunked) → broad importance questions → ALL possible answers/relationships → compare & contrast similarities/differences → candidate chunks from shared importance → compare alternative chunk structures → active decision → express non-linearly. Every step in order; "logical" ≠ "most logical", "works" ≠ "most efficient" (Clinics 25–27).

How shared importance produces chunks:
- "Why is this important?" must yield MULTIPLE reasons (A, B, C, D, E…). Another concept yields (S, T, U, V, W, C…); the overlap (C) is a shared reason for importance → a chunk. With one answer per concept there is nothing to compare. Phone example: important for communication, but also navigation, payments, entertainment, work, safety — multiple reasons enable comparison (Clinic 29).
- Answer the broad question broadly; narrow rephrasings ("why is the pericardium important *to the heart*?") are lower-order inquiry that tricks you into thinking you chunked by importance (Clinic 29). Importance checklisting and question quality are covered in [aiming](aiming.md).
- The same "boring" open question reused across concepts is correct — it yields different relationships each time. Drop redundant "what is X" questions when "why is X important" cannot be answered without it anyway.
- Grouping criteria: shared function, similar characteristics, similar reasons for importance, similar processes — whatever the relationships reveal.
- Ask the importance question of headings/groups too, not only individual keywords (why are the types/advantages/disadvantages important?).
- The broad importance question creates the wide relationship pool for major chunks; specific "how does X relate to Y" questions close in, clarify pertinent relationships and rule out invalid ones. Ask both.
- **Importance is broader than cause-and-effect and broader than "so what"** (Clinic 16): some things matter as prerequisites of understanding, not because of downstream effects. Cause-effect-only chunking risks single-node chains; consequentialist "so what" narrows thinking and hurts later subtle conceptual work. Importance can be application-based (maths/physics/chemistry) or conceptual (relationship to another concept) — both valid; the goal is a network of relationships and groups (Clinic 24).
- **Two lenses of importance — use both** (Clinics 19–21): (a) in the context of the topic / how it relates to the other main ideas (conventional importance-based chunking); (b) against your existing knowledge network (intuitive chunking — closer to frameworks you already understand). Correct answers connect to frameworks you already know (survival, protection, function, resourcefulness), so they are logical without memorisation.

Hierarchy rules (Chunkmapping Demo):
- The answer to "why is it important?" determines an item's level: an **application of a concept sits BELOW that concept** ("culturing microbes" is an application of "growth of microbes" → sub-chunk); **two equally important ideas sit at the SAME level** ("role of microorganisms" = "growth of microbes").
- Arbitrary listing that gives an application equal or greater weight than its parent concept is not importance-based chunking.

Standard of comparison:
- Generate multiple candidate groupings and **compare whole chunk structures against each other**, not just individual chunks. Most student failures come from insufficient comparison, not wrong technique.
- Hold the standard very high: if a chunk doesn't truly reflect the importance you reasoned about, break it and re-chunk. This happens most of the time — the pre-chunk step exists because it deepens thinking, not because chunks survive. Never move on until the structure is as simple as it can possibly be expressed by any human; chunk creation needs only superficial information IF you are razor-focused on why-it's-important/how-it's-retrieved (Clinic 11).
- Chunk-quality test: for each item in a group ask "why is this important and how does it relate to the others?" — if the answers differ, there is a missed sub-level of chunking.
- Everything is a hypothesis; avoid the sunk-cost fallacy (don't keep a structure just because you already drew it).
- Similarity alone is not a chunking criterion: grouping viruses by "can cause rash" is useless if all can and absence means nothing. The grouping must carry discriminative relevance (Clinic 29).
- Errors are transferable across subjects: the same student's biology and probability maps showed identical under-chunking — fix the process, not just one topic's notes.
- WPW reteaching mirrors this chunking exactly (justify each chunk's importance before naming it) — the two are the same skill ([revision](revision.md)).

Accuracy benchmarks (Clinics 25–27): early chunks ~50–60% accurate is fine; with skill they reach ~90–95%; consistently <50% signals a technique problem. Constantly re-forming chunks significantly at every step is itself inconsistent/inaccurate chunking. Chunk quality is judged by the cognitive process used, not by whether the result looks tidy.

Map simplification (Hipshot accounting demo):
- Chunking by importance can remove the need for a mnemonic — properly chunked content is straightforward to remember. Chunking "faithful representation" by importance made the student's mnemonic unnecessary; deep understanding also lets you generate accurate definitions on the spot instead of rote-learning them (two correct phrasings of "reporting period principle").
- **Flip repeating hierarchies** to the cleaner grouping: a balance sheet repeats current/non-current under both assets and liabilities → reorganise as current vs non-current, each containing assets/liabilities.
- **Omit labels obvious from context**: a balance sheet can only contain assets/liabilities, so just write "current / non-current". Examples derivable from the concept (economic benefit within 12 months) are not memorised as lists.
- Write repeated identical content once, not every time: the benchmark set (past = trend, future = budget, current = industry average) recurs across every measure — write it once.
- Simplification time is always well spent ("time spent simplifying information is always time well spent"): zoom back repeatedly to find tighter fits, and re-consolidate the map after each new chunk.
- Order: identify the most important (Layer 1) concepts and chunk around them → invert repeating sub-structures → delete redundant/obvious words and duplicated content → re-consolidate.
- Arbitrary detail with no conceptual hook (e.g. the GST tax-invoice requirements list) is routed to memorisation techniques instead of the map ([memorisation](memorisation.md)).

Common mistakes: importance checklisting (one "because X" answer, then move on); settling for "good enough" structures; treating chunks as fixed once written; committing to initial keyword categories before asking the questions; answering importance inside a pre-decided chunk; writing full-sentence importance notes (hold the 3–4 candidate reasons mentally or as a few keywords — they usually narrow to 1–2 within ~10 minutes; writing them out limits thinking and invites laziness) (Clinic 11).

Practical tips: if you can't find shared reasons for importance, submit work for expert review WITH a rigorous COBES — the struggle is usually checklisting or shallow reflection (Clinic 28). If finding shared reasons feels easy early on, cognitive load is probably too low — early stages should feel more overwhelming than easy. Schools teach the "what" but assess the "why" and "how" — chunk for the assessment, not the textbook layout (Clinic 11). With practice, importance-based chunking becomes a habitual default frame for consuming any information — meetings, books, courses, learning on the job; by the end of a page the map already exists in your head and you just write it down.

## Logical vs Intuitive Chunk Structures
A quality standard for chunks: a structure must be both **logical** and **intuitive** — so obvious, given your prior/domain knowledge, that it needs no memorisation. Intuitiveness is a property of the whole chunk structure, not of isolated chunks.

- Logical ≠ intuitive. Textbook chunks are almost always logical; the decisive test is whether the structure feels obvious (cause→effect, before→after, up→down, problem→solution). Illogical example: chunking everything containing the letter G.
- A chunk alone is not intuitive: "before" means nothing until paired with "after". Even a silly rule becomes intuitive if the contrasting chunks make it meaningful. Always evaluate a chunk in relation to the other chunks in the structure.
- **Good divisions reuse familiar frameworks**: before/after, cause/effect, form/function, how/why, what/why, input→process→output, World/National, Primary/Secondary, Sustainable/Unsustainable (Clinics 19–21). "Intuitive/logical/makes sense" = the new information fits patterns of thinking you already bring (existing knowledge and life experience, e.g. cause-and-effect learned as a baby); chunking intuitively calibrates new material to how your brain ALREADY knows how to think, which is why less memorisation is needed (Clinic 15).
- Domain knowledge makes structures intuitive (medicine: history → investigation → management/treatment). What is obvious to you may not be to others — judge against your own prior knowledge; even technically heavy topics can usually be made intuitive this way. The check is personal alignment, not an objective metric — subjectivity doesn't matter (Clinic 15).
- Non-intuitive structures add memorisation load (you must remember the structure itself); forgetting the structure cancels chunking's benefit. Intuitive structures align with prior-knowledge networks and need no extra effort.
- Use obvious chunks only as hypothetical springboards for higher-order thinking — even discarded hypotheses were worthwhile because comparing them engaged evaluative thinking. The only way to lose is committing to them instead of doing proper importance-based questioning.
- Avoid **lonely chunks** (a chunk with nothing in it) and **single-node chains** (A→B with no content): merge nodes to reduce unnecessary chunks and data points.

Decision flowchart:
1. Is the chunk logical? No → don't chunk it.
2. Is it intuitive and obvious — could someone with little domain knowledge predict it; would you need effort to memorise it? No → don't chunk it. **If in doubt, don't chunk it.**
3. Run importance-based inquiry ("why is it important / how is it related").
4. Does the chunk align with the reasons for importance? Yes → keep. No → break it and re-chunk.

Examples: "individual vs system" chosen over "individual vs group" because the contrast word must feel like the natural opposite; "system" later dropped when nothing sat under it (lonely chunk). Practice → cognitive retraining: practice splits into method vs duration, and the outcome of practice is cognitive retraining. Non-intuitive splits to avoid: "Chemical Reaction → Reactivity vs Valency"; "Economic Development → Activities vs Increasing Participation" (Clinic 19).

Common mistakes: committing to textbook headings/chapter names/terminology as chunks (too technical, not intuitive → forgotten; relationships spider-web and waterfall everywhere); judging a chunk in isolation; keeping a structure that doesn't reflect importance just because it is already drawn.

## Intuitive Chunking (Advanced Refinement)
The Ascent III refinement: choose chunk headings and sub-chunks so they feel logical from prior knowledge rather than requiring memorisation. The test: **does this chunk structure rely on memorising terminology, or on things that already make sense?** Equivalently: would this division from big chunk to sub-chunks have occurred to me without having studied the topic (Clinic 15)?

Full chain: inquiry → discover relationships → form chunks → make chunks importance-based → evaluate alternative chunk structures → leverage prior knowledge.

Key principles:
- "Intuitive" means leveraging prior knowledge. A structure that only makes sense once you have memorised the terms is not intuitive.
- **Reverse chunking**: put the reasoning/importance at the chunk level and the terminology at the leaves, so you memorise the label rather than depend on the label to structure the map.
- **Keyword substitution / bracketing** (Clinic 15): use the word that feels most intuitive as the heading; put the required technical terminology in brackets underneath — understanding first, terminology preserved. Example: a pen stylus divided into structure vs function is intuitive; "silicon-containing components / capacitance tiers / plastic variations" is memorisation fodder unless you already think that way.
- Keep sibling chunks at the same level of intuitiveness: "loss of function" pairs naturally with "change in anatomy" or "types of damage", not with "glomerulus".
- Intuitiveness is domain- and experience-relative: acute vs chronic is intuitive to a medical student; dialysis vs transplant is not intuitive to a beginner, but becomes so after weeks of exposure. Judge against durable prior knowledge, not terms you just learned.
- **Chunk until you can't chunk anymore** (Clinics 25–27): the logic layer is the biggest, most intuitive, all-encompassing chunk covering the whole topic; being in layer 3 (important details) does NOT mean you stop chunking. Resorting to terminology = entering memorisation; hold it off as long as possible. Go wide-and-shallow across multiple layers (logic → concepts → important details) rather than narrow-and-deep — same cognitive load, better relationships. Krebs cycle: know its point/importance/big-picture fit first; you don't need every step or ATP count yet (Clinic 26).
- **Single-node chain** (a node whose only child is one further node) is a red flag: ask whether a different chunking removes it. Collapse into one chunk with heading plus subtitle ("Failure — ESRD/ESRF") rather than Failure → ESRD. Not always fixable, always worth considering.
- Chunk shape encodes emphasis, and emphasis drives memorability. Choose the shape that stresses what matters for this topic; there is no universally right answer, and the act of deciding is itself Evaluate-level learning.
- Even if you keep the original chunking, the thought process of connecting it to prior knowledge raises encoding and produces higher-order learning. It increases cognitive load — which is desirable at this stage.
- Requires very high-quality, well-tuned COBES and sometimes feedback from more expert practitioners. Aim for consistency across subjects and difficulty levels; inconsistent results mean the skill is not yet tuned.
- Not medical-only: the same reasoning applies to business, marketing, economics, accounting, arts and education.

Technique:
1. Scan the map for single-node chains and terminology-only chunk headings.
2. For each chunk ask "why is this important?" and re-title it with the importance-based reason.
3. Ask "is there a more intuitive division that uses what I already know?"
4. Insert an intermediate chunk where a jump is unintuitive (Failure → Treatment options → Dialysis / Transplant).
5. Consider restructuring by indication — when and why each option applies (severity, stage, timeline, progression) — instead of by name.
6. Compare two or more candidate structures; pick the one whose emphasis best matches the topic's importance.
7. Push terminology to the leaves (bracket the official term under the intuitive heading); keep logic at the chunk level.

Worked example — kidney/renal map (Shoot-stage, not yet skinned):
- Function loss → Acute / Chronic → Failure (ESRD) emphasises that both types converge on failure; the alternative (Function loss → Failure, with acute/chronic branching off the side) emphasises process flow instead. Pick per topic.
- Failure → Dialysis / Transplant is unintuitive for a beginner; both are treatment options for end-stage failure. Dialysis matters because it lets the body keep performing kidney function while awaiting a transplant (or after a potentially recoverable acute injury); transplant matters because it replaces the kidney and fixes the problem.
- Dialysis → Indications → Peritoneal / Haemodialysis is a single-node chain ("indications" = when a treatment is appropriate). Better as Indications vs Types, or restructure the top half by when each treatment is indicated.
- Glomerulus → Nephrotic / Nephritic: better re-chunked as poor filtration → protein vs blood, then memorise which is called nephrotic and which nephritic.

Skill progression (Clinics 35, 36, 38): intuitive chunking is the END of a progression, not a starting point — (1) good non-linear note-taking → (2) basic groups and relationships → (3) actively control groups/relationships via curiosity and inquiry → (4) prioritise groups/relationships by importance and connectivity → (5) fine-tune with intuitive chunking. Each level must reach unconscious competence before the next. Attempting the top skill first is overwhelming and slow (piano analogy: starting with a famously difficult piece could take 15 years vs learning scales first). Natural, quick grouping emerges from working up the competence levels via COBES, marginal gains and enough retraining pressure; backbone relevance keeps being refined through Ascent 3. The difficulty along the way is desirable difficulty, not failure.

Common mistakes: terminology-based chunks that force memorisation and add retrieval failure points; dividing a concept into one intuitive chunk and one arbitrary term; leaving single-node chains unexamined; putting all memorisable detail in the leaves while the chunks themselves are arbitrary labels; dropping into terminology too early instead of continuing intuitive chunking; jumping straight to intuitive chunking without the prerequisite habits.

## Pre-Chunking & Framing Bias
**Framing bias** is letting the frame in which information is presented (textbook headings, lecture order, taught groupings) dictate your conclusions and chunks. **Pre-chunking** is creating chunks before/without the full inquiry process — lower-order chunking and the most common cause of messy, unstable mind maps. Clinic 28 is essentially one extended class on this ("the framing bias live clinic").

Key principles:
- Linear resources are someone else's transformation of non-linear knowledge; their headings and hierarchies are usually NOT the optimal encoding structure for you. Textbook headings are usually not good chunks.
- The correct process has a strict order: develop inquiry → use inquiry to find relationships → evaluate multiple relationships against each other → arrive at groups → prioritise which group is most important → find relationships between groups to build the big-picture frame. Deviating from this order causes huge problems.
- Chunks are REVEALED through inquiry and comparing relationships; you never have to "think up" an alternative grouping theoretically. "I can't think of another way to chunk it" is itself a symptom of framing bias — the alternative appears when you find shared reasons for importance across concepts.
- Pre-chunking is not always bad — obvious chunks can help you think more deeply — but never commit to them; they are hypotheses to validate through inquiry. You can create your own framing bias by chunking a word list too deeply before asking the importance questions.
- **Pre-chunking ≠ chunking during pre-study.** Pre-study is simply studying before the main learning event; you can (and should) use the correct importance-based process within it (Clinic 29). Basic organisation at the Aim step is not pre-chunking either — it is surface-level grouping achieved by the correct method, never "because it was given that way" or "feels right". Collecting many keywords and basic organisation into ~3 headings is OK as long as you don't pre-chunk further before answering questions (Clinic 24).
- Confirmation-bias variant (Clinic 30): choosing comfortable, textbook-like chunk structures and then convincing yourself you followed the technique by giving logical reasons that support the behaviour. It feels correct in the moment and is only caught in deep reflection.
- Medical students are the most susceptible group ("nine out of ten times"), because they are taught information in a fixed way and absorb study-guru myths about "the way medicine must be studied".
- Needing to reorganise the map is normal early on and reduces as skill improves; constant major re-framing suggests the initial frame was built with an incorrect technique (sub-optimal chunks that later learning exposes). With correct broad pre-study + Aim, the frame should be ~80–85% accurate ([aiming](aiming.md)).
- These problems affect every stage from Fundamentals to Ascent 3/Basecamp, especially in people who rushed the course (Clinic 28). They are common enough to have named terms (the creator keeps a list of ~80 common mistakes for coach training) and are hard to self-detect even with good COBES — feedback speeds it up (Clinic 23).

Countermeasures / technique:
1. **Consciously break the original frame** and apply the techniques fresh, uninfluenced (Clinic 22). Notice how the material was framed (e.g. "here's arthritis: signs/symptoms/x-ray/management"), then take the raw keywords/symptoms/investigations/management and ask why each is important, how it fits, and find similarities/differences.
2. Collect keywords without using chapter/sub-chapter headings as the grouping; make your own headings.
3. If order control is a struggle, deliberately write the keyword list OUT of the book's order (or straight down the line), so the given sequence cannot anchor you (Clinics 27, 30).
4. Run the Aim questions from that unchunked list; hypothesise chunk structures, compare them, and arrive at 3–4 main chunks independently. Hold multiple candidates; be willing to discard all of them.
5. Follow every step of the taught process exactly; use self-awareness to notice when you deviate — if you don't know where you deviate, you can never stop deviating.
6. Use consistent COBES to detect your own pre-chunking: observe the cognitive response ("I chunked first, then asked questions"), abstract the tendency, experiment with following the steps more closely.

Common mistakes: chunking by textbook headings/bold words because they "seem important"; committing to initial categories before asking questions, then refusing to break them; asking "why is this important" but answering inside a pre-decided chunk; letting an upfront diagnosis/label bias your interpretation; believing linear subjects (law, legal processes, step-by-step procedures) cannot be restructured — they can; hundreds/thousands of law students do it. "Find the way" is the objective answer; difficulty is not evidence of impossibility (Clinic 30).

Practical tips: the fix is always "follow the techniques exactly as taught + build the metacognition (via COBES) to catch deviations" (Clinic 28). Catching yourself 0% → 70% of the time IS progress even while the same mistake still occurs — progress includes detection, not just solutions (Clinic 30). Struggling to reorder step-by-step content is a sign you're trying to do it right; do it anyway (Clinic 30).

Examples: a gene-regulation mind map that must be reorganised every session — the frame was likely pre-chunked from the article structure instead of derived from compared relationships (Clinic 28). An Ascent 2 student keeps choosing textbook-similar chunks and rationalises them; fix = unchunked keyword list written out of order, then Aim-driven hypothesising (Clinic 30). **Appendicitis framing** (Clinic 22): if a case is introduced as "appendicitis" before the symptoms (tummy pain worse with movement, fever, high WCC/inflammatory markers), your interpretation is framed by that diagnosis; breaking the frame lets you reason from the symptoms independently.

## Reverse Chunking: Fixing Spider-Webbing
When one keyword keeps reappearing across many chunks and you draw long lateral arrows to connect them (**spider-webbing**), the chunk structure is wrong: the repeatedly shared concept is itself a reason of shared importance and should become the chunk (**reverse chunking / flipping the chunk**). **Anti-spider-webbing** — knowing the relationships exist but hiding them to keep the map tidy — is the same underlying failure.

- Sock analogy: a pair of socks split across drawers can be connected with string (spider-webbing) or the string can be omitted (anti-spider-webbing) — both lose the socks. The fix is to put them in the same drawer: co-locate related things in a chunk that expresses the relationship structurally.
- Both patterns mean the chunk structure was not evaluated enough BEFORE committing to paper. Before writing any word down, consider how it relates to everything else on the map; big looping arrows added after layout, and arrows pointing at other arrows, mean relationships were not pre-thought — such maps cannot be remembered.
- Some lateral connections are good; many long lateral connections between detail areas signal items that should have been in the same chunk from the start. Two chunks joined only by many leaf-level connections is a weak spot: frame a more direct chunk-to-chunk relationship, or sub-chunk to simplify — "where there is complexity, seek simplicity" (Clinics 19–21).
- Hasty chunking ("here's a group, put it down; that's a heading, it must be important; that word is bold") is lower-order thinking; higher-order evaluation solves spider-webbing, anti-spider-webbing and waterfalling at once. (The full failure-pattern catalogue — waterfalling, wheel-and-spokes, details-stripped — is in [note-taking](note-taking.md).)

Fix procedure:
1. Notice a keyword recurring under multiple chunks (or messy crossing/looping arrows).
2. Ask: is this concept itself a shared reason why all these things are important?
3. Flip the chunk: make the shared reason the parent (e.g. "cell strength") and reorganise the old parents as its components (e.g. structural vs physiological contributors).
4. Re-evaluate remaining relationships; some repetition is inevitable in any structure — choose the arrangement that is simpler overall.

Example: cell biology — "cell strength" appearing under structure, junctions, etc. → flip to chunks "things that achieve cell strength" vs "things that achieve cell transport", with structural (cell wall…) vs physiological components underneath (Clinic 28).

Practical tips: the mess is not caused by discovering relationships; it is caused by starting inside the textbook's boxes — independent chunking prevents the issue from arising (Clinic 28). If a map starts messy and later versions are cleaner, the logic layer should have been built on the clean base; persistent mess points to insufficient inquiry depth (Clinic 29).

## Information Hierarchies & Reverse Causality
A common beginner mind-map failure: a **family-tree-shaped map** that simply re-groups the textbook's headings, subheadings, terminology, definitions and examples. It is grouped but not reflective, so it fails the GRINDE "R".

- Looks organised (passes G) but shows no order control — the structure is the source's linear order, not the learner's own logic. The "why is it important?" question is usually missing entirely.
- When the question IS asked, it is often answered with **reverse causality** — justifying a cause by its effect ("X is important because it creates Y") instead of what gave rise to X. Correct logic runs the other way: Y exists because of X. Reverse causality feels logical but leaves knowledge isolated and memorised: to remember X you must already have memorised Y. Full treatment and the DNA→mRNA→protein example in [learning-science](learning-science.md).
- **Closed-loop test** (Clinic 24): "would I need to MEMORISE this answer to remember the chunks?" If yes, it is likely reverse causality / non-intuitive. A correct answer builds a network — A is important because it is the product of C, which allowed D, without which E couldn't occur, and A produces B — relating to prior knowledge and other parts of the network, not just what A directly leads to.
- Because the relationships are not logical, good chunking only makes memorisation easier — it does not produce understanding, and the facts still sit in isolation. It does not reflect the layers of learning, because details were never deferred in favour of logic.
- A true sequence of events is not the same as a reason for importance.
- Fix: exercise order control ([learning-science](learning-science.md)), build logic first, then re-chunk by importance. Pre-study matters because you need a broad base of anchoring points to connect things; starting with nothing makes networking hard (Clinic 24).

Common mistakes: copying heading order into a map and calling it a mind map; assuming grouping alone equals quality; believing you have asked the importance question when you have only justified causes by their effects; building a whole map of downstream justifications and calling it logical; listing equations/rules under a topic ("because it's part of integration") — you'd only know that by memorising.

## Equation & Formula Chunking
Do not go straight from a chunk to a formula, or group formulas together merely because they are formulas. Lead with a verbal/abstract expression of the formula's relevance and importance, then attach the formula as a sub-note — **keyword-anchor it, just like an image** (Clinic 26).

- Even a simple formula (e.g. F = ma) is not immediately intuitive; the verbal concept is what lets you form logical chunks.
- Grouping two formulas only because both are formulas is arbitrary and conceptually empty — equivalent to chunking "things with the letter O in them". If formulas share a function/feature/conceptual basis, that shared basis should be the chunk name, not "formulas".
- Especially important in physics and maths, where formulas are dense.

Technique: first chunk using words; ask "what is this formula trying to do, why do I care, how does it fit?" then place the equation next to the concept it serves. Learning purely via numbers/equations is one of the most ineffective routes to expertise — ask any maths/physics PhD or professor (Clinic 26). For Maths consolidation and problem-creation, see Skin for Maths.

Common mistakes: drawing a chunk labelled "formulas/equations" with equations hanging off it (pure memorisation); making equation chunking a habit — it becomes detrimental to logical, intuitive chunking.

## Chunking Linear Processes & Sequences
Any information can be chunked — including linear multi-step processes and "content-heavy" subjects — because information has no inherent structure or meaning; you compare, find similarities/differences, and group until it is as simple as possible (Clinics 22, 23).

- **A single-node chain usually means the notes capture a process/sequence, not true chunking.** Logical ≠ chunked: the two overlap but are not identical (importance-based chunking sits in the intersection). Sequences are logical but memory-taxing — each link has only one tether and follows its own forgetting curve, so you must retrieve every link ≈ rote memorisation (Clinic 17).
- **50–60% of the time you do NOT need to memorise order**: if you know each item individually, the order becomes obvious. Being taught in an order doesn't mean it must be learned in that order (Clinic 17). ~70% of single-node chains mean a different, simpler chunking exists; a fully refined technique would never produce one (Clinic 19).
- **When a fixed sequence genuinely must be remembered** (immunology cascades, evolution, some engineering processes), chunk the steps into stages by shared purpose — steps 1–3 achieve X, 4–6 achieve Y — with branching sub-chunks instead of a flat chain (like the course's high-yield phase vs growth phase). For a 13-step process, ask what the first 3–4 steps achieve, compare steps, simplify/group (Clinics 17, 22, 23).
- Tables are also avoided where possible (same isolation problem) (Clinic 17).
- **"Content-heavy" is subjective** — it depends on your processing capacity. The technique changes your perception of heaviness, not vice versa. Chunk taxation, law or 100 slides exactly like everything else: find similarities, group, use inquiry. If even the "basic" concepts are too advanced, chunk them into a simpler framework first — you control how simple (Clinic 22).

Technique:
1. Scan notes for single-node chains (recognisable even in an unfamiliar language).
2. Ask "why is each item important?" to find shared reasons and re-group into real chunks.
3. If order truly matters, group steps into purpose-based stages with branching sub-chunks.
4. Otherwise, drop order memorisation and use a more intuitive chunking frame.

Examples: cars chunked by type (sedan/van/SUV) vs fuel (gas/electric) — intuitive, not a flat list (Clinics 23, 24). Single-node chains also flag reverse-chunking opportunities (repeated structure hiding in a chain) and are common in chronological subjects (history, evolutionary biology) when you follow the taught order instead of doing inquiry — the fix is identical: proper inquiry avoids the chain (Clinics 17, 19).

Common mistakes: confusing a logical flow of events with good chunking; memorising sequences in order when understanding the items makes order obvious; leaving processes as flat 1→2→3→4→5→6 chains; treating linear processes or "heavy" content as unchunkable or needing a different technique.

## The GRINDE Checklist
GRINDE is the instructor's six-point checklist for judging whether a post-Skin mind map is high quality; a map that passes is a "GRINDE map". It specialises the general effective-note-taking principles (VPRefRE — visual, processed, relational, freehand, reflective; [note-taking](note-taking.md)) for mind maps specifically. The post-Shoot map usually will NOT pass this checklist; Skin is what makes it pass.

- **G — Grouped**: lots of chunking. (Named G rather than C because GRINDE was taught before chunking, "group" is a more accessible word, and it sounds better.)
- **R — Reflective**: reflects a good learning process — relationships, layers of learning and order control are all visible. Logic first, then concepts, then important details added onto the concepts and leading back to the logic. Requires order control, because you are normally handed the details first.
- **I — Interconnected**: links run horizontally as well as vertically, showing how ideas interact and influence each other. Raises analysis/evaluation and adds connections. Only interconnect what is genuinely meaningful — a real cause-and-effect relationship, not two words that happen to sit near each other.
- **N — Non-verbal**: more doodles and abstract imagery, less reliance on words. Words take up space and clutter the page; imagery uses a different part of the brain that is normally unused and directly increases retention.
- **D — Directional**: the information has a flow and a purpose rather than just branching outward. Only achievable at Evaluate (L5), because you must decide what matters more than what. Arrows supply the direction, and directionality tests whether the logic is real cause-and-effect.
- **E — Emphasised**: main concepts, relationships and arrows are visually clearer — bigger font, thicker/bolder/stylised/coloured lines. Forces you to critique and weigh which links matter most, which also pins you at L5.

Common mistakes: information hierarchies that are grouped but not reflective; reverse causality masquerading as an answer to "why is it important?"; interconnecting everything for the sake of it (random arrows); relying on words where an image would be smaller and more memorable; treating all connections as equally important (no emphasis, no direction).

Practical tips: use the checklist as an audit — score each letter, then fix the weakest (usually N and E). A digital infinite canvas makes re-skinning cheap, so you can iterate to a higher score.

## GRINDE Map Audit (Worked Example)
A demonstration of auditing a post-Skin cardiovascular mind map against each GRINDE letter to decide "have I skinned it enough?"

- **Grouped**: mostly good, but one area still has 5+ branches and should be split into ~3 groups — the instructor admits getting lazy there.
- **Reflective**: yes. A clear backbone is visible; minor points have less backbone; the order shows core logic learned before details.
- **Skew** is a useful artifact of good order control: the early/core part looks neater and later additions look rougher, because the map starts at the core and builds outward. Not a hard-and-fast rule, but a good sign that main connections were prioritised first.
- **Interconnected**: yes — many meaningful cross-links showing how the information connects.
- **Non-verbal**: only a couple of images — the weakest criterion. Several worded nodes could be expressed as pictures instead.
- **Directional**: yes — clear flow; the information has a purpose for existing.
- **Emphasised**: yes, but main links should be bolder, thicker or coloured to pop more.
- **Verdict: passes at ~90%; another ~10% of skinning is available.** Skin is iterative.

Common mistakes: declaring a map "done" straight after Shoot; leaving one dense region ungrouped while the rest is well chunked; under-using imagery and under-emphasising main links (the two most common shortfalls); assuming a map that already looks clean needs no audit.

Practical tips: re-skinning is cheap on a digital infinite canvas or tablet (erase and redraw a region) versus redoing an entire paper map — a key reason to go digital. Audit letter by letter and fix the weakest, usually N and E.

## Skin: The Map-Cleanup Workflow
Skin is the third BHS step: consolidate the post-Shoot mind map into a cleaner, more relational, higher-mastery structure by re-chunking and re-evaluating relationships (Evaluate, Bloom 5). Its role, timing and scheduling live in [bear-hunter-system](bear-hunter-system.md); this section is the concrete editing procedure demonstrated on Biology and Chemistry.

Principles:
- Arrows represent relationships, and relationships are the key — clearer arrows mean prioritised relationships.
- Same information, different arrangement: nothing is lost, only re-nested and re-positioned.
- A "hedgehog" map (spikes off every point) is more memorable than linear notes but still far below what a mind map should be.
- Poor spatial planning on the first pass is the usual cause of intersecting lines; the second pass fixes it while preserving the planned backbone.
- Skinning often reveals facts that are now so obvious you will never forget them — remove them from the map.
- Content split: the map holds ~90–95% of the topic; the remaining ~5–10% of arbitrary details goes to digital flashcards ([memorisation](memorisation.md)).

Procedure:
1. Scan for dense nodes: circle every point with 4–5+ branches (the chunking targets).
2. Find the pattern among the branches and group them (e.g. 5 reproduction types → 3 groups).
3. Re-arrange spatially so relationships are short and readable; move nodes to shorten long "dodging" arrows.
4. Eliminate crossing/intersecting arrows — they are much harder to remember and signal relationships not being prioritised.
5. Replace words with images and doodles wherever possible.
6. Prune facts that have become obvious through studying.
7. Keep the remaining arbitrary details on digital flashcards (Anki/Quizlet).
8. Compare before/after: same information, cleaner flow, clearer direction. Audit against the GRINDE checklist.

Doing Skin from pure recall days later is a strong revision activity that also raises mastery — better than a practice question and far better than rereading/rewriting notes ([revision](revision.md)).

Worked examples:
- **Biology 1 — asexual reproduction**: 5 branches at several nodes → the 5 types grouped into 3 (splitting: fission/fragmentation; travelling: spores/budding). Nuclear transfer moved next to failure rate so the arrow is short. Advantages/disadvantages collapsed to 3 each with examples. Cloning chunked two ways at once — plants vs animals AND lab vs non-lab techniques.
- **Biology 2 — blood system**: messy "control" region re-arranged; coronary heart disease and control better chunked; removed "capillaries are the smallest vessel" (now obvious). Kept capillary detail: one cell thick, pores, exchange of O₂/CO₂, pressure drops as surface area and branch count rise. Added an exercising man and a fight-or-flight diagram. Mnemonic: a sun drawn inside the "D" of diastole → "sunny diastole" = relaxation. Only 2–3 flashcards needed (e.g. valve events in the cardiac cycle).
- **Chemistry — intermolecular forces**: first map already clean, left alone. Second map messy → hydrogen bonds moved out to its own point to stop line overlap; rearranged polar / hydrogen bonds / solute-solvent interactions while preserving the planned structure → solute-solvent interactions backbone. A dumbbell drawn for the "H" of hydrogen bonds encodes "strong" without writing the word; an ice drawing encodes water as a coolant. ~5–10% (definitions, equations, formulas, constants) left to memorise.

Common mistakes: long connections threading between other nodes ("impossible to remember"); lines intersecting; writing out facts you will never forget; not planning use of space before drawing; auditing only the messy map (or only the clean one).

Practical tips: freeze the before/after frames and try chunking the circled areas yourself before comparing with the instructor's version. Add small memorable marks and doodles to make points pop off the page. If one of two maps is already clean, leave it alone and spend the effort on the messy one.

## Skin for English: Text Response
For English text response, Skin is not mainly map cleanup — it is consolidating themes and techniques by using them from many angles through practice essays and analysis of other people's essays. The map is a means to essay fluency, not an end in itself.

- Shoot built themes → causes → examples/techniques. Skin consolidates by finding patterns and types of examples/techniques that all connect to a single viewpoint you can associate with more easily.
- Goal: hit the material from different angles; be comfortable with the themes, with rationalising WHY the themes are important, and with linking themes to techniques in many different combinations.
- Runs on Kolb's experiential learning cycle: experience → reflection → abstraction → experimentation ([learning-science](learning-science.md)). Being "locked into" the cycle means every essay produces learning rather than a plateau.
- It does NOT save time, and it relies on having done the Shoot/writing step correctly.
- More effective than old practice-essay habits because the context has changed: you are doing exercises that connect techniques to themes, not blindly trying to improve prose.

Technique:
1. Confirm the Shoot map links themes → causes → examples/techniques.
2. Write practice essays across varied question types, deliberately recombining themes and techniques in ways you would not normally think of.
3. After each, reflect and abstract: summarise the different types of approaches you could take.
4. Read and analyse other example essays — famous school texts have hundreds or thousands available — to see how others communicated the same ideas. This is revision in essay form: it builds connections, rearranges them in different orders, and shows approaches you had not thought of.
5. Re-experiment with new combinations and repeat the cycle.

Common mistakes: practising essays blindly to improve prose instead of exercising theme↔technique connections; expecting Skin to save time; skipping a correct Shoot/writing step and expecting Skin to go smoothly.

## Skin for English: Unfamiliar & Persuasive Text
For unfamiliar and persuasive texts, Skin distils a long list of observed themes into a few umbrella themes and locks the theme→technique direction, so any essay question can be answered systematically.

- Everything learned from Aim onwards has a purpose: techniques are linked directly to themes, and themes to the author's agenda.
- Skin = consolidate. Find patterns between theme words and collapse them into a single umbrella idea you can anchor to: better/good/happy/positive change → "positive change"; honest/grateful/local/relevant/collaborative → "customer first / customer appreciation"; repeated normalising statements → "normalising". Distilling many themes into ~3 makes the material easy to use under exam conditions.
- **Direction matters: develop the eye to dissect themes → techniques, NOT techniques → themes.**
- Systematic essay skeleton: state the agenda/purpose → introduce the umbrella themes → for each theme, rationalise it using the identified techniques.
- Curveball questions (e.g. "analyse the persuasive literary techniques used and prove the purpose") are handled by the same BHS sequence, not by new knowledge.
- Where the text gives insufficient context to know why a theme matters, flag it (a "curvy line") rather than inventing an answer.

Technique:
1. List everything you notice: visual elements (smiling person, pencil, sunlight, birds, clouds, waves) and word choices (tiptoe, fathom, echo, million, sigh, wander, meander).
2. Convert each observation into a candidate theme (positivity/calm/comfort/freedom; vast unknown depth, possibly frightening; confusion/being lost).
3. Group the candidate themes into ~3 umbrella themes.
4. Identify the techniques carrying each theme: top-to-bottom spectrum, waves, emotive diction, colour, smiling face, birds, repetition, rhetorical questions, emphatic statements, juxtaposition with statistics, value-driven words ("recent theft" vs "easier, safer").
5. Ask "why is this important?" of each theme; if the text lacks the information, flag it instead of guessing.
6. Write the essay from the skeleton — agenda → themes → techniques → rationalisation — and make it flow.

Examples: a local store's cashless-change advertisement — the agenda is to portray the change as positive, customer-first and normal; techniques include positive handshake photos, repetition in headlines, rhetorical questions, emphatic customer-service statements, normality claims, and juxtaposition of positive change with statistics and value words. An image with a smiling student, pencil, sunlight, birds and soft clouds above dark waves below → juxtaposition of positivity, freedom and calm confidence against unknown, chaotic, potentially frightening depths; words like "plummet", "fathom" and "echo" reinforce scale and uncertainty.

Common mistakes: going techniques → themes instead of themes → techniques; keeping a long flat list of themes that is hard to use in an essay; inventing authorial intent when the text does not support it. Practise the linking skill (technique → effect → theme) separately from the content; the analysis itself is highly accurate, systematic and repeatable. Essay execution downstream in [exam-technique](exam-technique.md).

## Skin for Maths
For Maths, Skin consolidates relationships and finds patterns by practising problems AND creating your own problems, after Aim/Shoot have established why each equation exists. (Chunk formulas by meaning, not by "being a formula" — see Equation & Formula Chunking.)

- Aim/Shoot flip the practice ratio from "do the same problem again and again" (1:1) to roughly **10:1** — each problem now teaches more, because the purpose of every equation is already known before you meet it.
- Maths has a relatively limited curriculum but is theory-heavy; Skin's purpose is still to find patterns and merge relationships. Like English, hit it from multiple angles so any question type is comfortable.
- Practice problems become more effective at Skin because the brain was set up differently: you **predict ("I think it is like this"), test, and correct** — instead of randomly memorising a step-by-step for each problem type. With strong understanding of why equations exist you naturally know when to apply them and which combination to use; a failed attempt reveals a missing element rather than a random gap.
- High school maths is tested at Apply/Analyse/Evaluate, not Create — you do not need apex mastery, just refined skill. Locked into the Kolb experiential cycle ([learning-science](learning-science.md)), every wrong problem yields learning and improvement over time.
- **Two independent weaknesses, two different fixes**: procedural weakness = you cannot do the problems → practise; conceptual weakness = you cannot create problems → go back to the mind map.

Technique:
1. Finish Aim/Shoot so the purpose and interconnection of every equation is clear.
2. Practise a wide variety of problems, predicting the method before executing and correcting your model when wrong.
3. Look for patterns across problem types — which are the same in which ways.
4. **Create your own problems**: as challenging as possible, always applied to a situation or real-life context. Aim at the hardest exam-level questions (the ones that would end up in the news).
5. If you cannot create such problems, return to the mind map, re-check the keywords/concepts and the general purpose of everything, then try again.
6. Optionally pair with a friend or study group and challenge each other with created questions.

Problem creation is a diagnostic: difficulty creating hard problems is a strong signal of flawed conceptual understanding of why equations exist and how to use them. Because you understand the network, you can chain equation 1 → equation 2 via a known pathway and build scholarship-level questions.

Examples: trigonometry applied to a person on a cliff dropping an object, with an observer at the base — solve for the angle given the distances. Calculus applied to a plane's acceleration between two cities: a function represents it, the derivative finds the rate, and the anti-derivative recovers the result — so you can create problems that traverse that pathway in either direction.

Common mistakes: creating trivial variants (the same triangle with different numbers) — the process is identical, so there is no new learning; doing millions of practice problems without understanding why equations exist; continuing to practise when the real gap is conceptual (going back to understand is more time-efficient); being unable to combine equations — stuck at Apply/Remember/Understand.

## Languages: Use-Case Chunking & Skin
Languages are genuinely harder to encode (words look arbitrary, relationships seem absent, forgetting rate is high), so they need more memorisation than most subjects — but far less than pure-rote approaches assume, because words, grammar and sentences can be importance-chunked by the situations in which they are used (Clinic 10). Skin then finds deeper connections via etymology and shared roots. **Skin is optional unless you want real fluency**: the Aim/Shoot cycle (built on the comprehensible-input hypothesis, [aiming](aiming.md)) is the priority; if short on time, do Aim/Shoot strongly instead.

Use-case chunking (encoding, Clinic 10):
- The common approach (~99% of learners) is pure memorisation of exam outputs; the system instead chunks by use case, giving memory cues and structure.
- Chunk words/expressions that serve the same scenario: "that's okay / that's alright / that's all good" form one chunk; "how are you?" chunks into ways of asking × response types (positive / negative / in-depth).
- Learn phrases, idioms and fixed combinations as GROUPS first (e.g. currency language always occurs in certain settings), then break them into individual words later — group-first is easier than word-first.
- Known-but-unused words are unchunked words: you may know "serendipitous" but never use it until you chunk it with coincidental/fateful synonyms. To upgrade professional English, re-chunk new words with their meaning-neighbours, then interleave usage across settings.
- Some items are straight rote (e.g. gender/pronouns) — support them with visualisation (French "la bibliothèque" feminine → imagine a feminine librarian) ([memorisation](memorisation.md)).
- Languages are naturally recursive: earlier material reappears in later contexts, so interleaving gets easier and layers build on themselves. Expect more repetition than in science subjects — that's legitimate, not a technique failure. Language chunk maps can be wordy; a word document is acceptable, though drawing apps allow embedded visualisations and clearer flow.

Skin via etymology and roots:
- Skin's purpose is still connections, patterns, chunks and organisation; it speeds up reading and writing most.
- Mind maps work but become huge because there are so many connections; small trees of characters and words are more manageable. Try it and see whether it suits you.
- **Etymology** = the study of the origins of words and how languages developed. Words branch from roots, so learning one root teaches many words. Greek *phos* ("light") → photo- → photograph, photography, photometry. Recognising roots is subtler in unfamiliar languages, and even in English: "soccer" comes from "Association" plus British "-er" slang, with the "a" dropped.
- Target: instead of memorising 100 words, memorise ~42 roots and treat the rest as combinations of them.
- Non-alphabetic scripts still have patterns: Chinese characters are often compounds or share radicals/shapes — the "king" element inside the "borders" element gives country / Middle Kingdom; a box shape suggests a border; a bird-like character aids recall. Patterns may be legitimately related or partly arbitrary/mnemonic — both are useful.
- Combine with active flashcard management to retain many characters and words ([memorisation](memorisation.md)).

Technique:
1. Keep the Aim/Shoot cycle running on comprehensible input; chunk vocabulary by use case as you go.
2. Every few weeks, or about once a month, browse the whole flashcard list for 30–60 minutes.
3. Look for similarities you had not noticed: shared roots, shared radicals, overlapping character shapes, similar-sounding words.
4. Build small trees/networks of the connections you find; these become your mind map.
5. Re-express arbitrary items with a memorable image or association.
6. If the network becomes overwhelming, drop back to other ways of recording the information.

Common mistakes: memorising isolated vocabulary lists with no use-case structure; assuming languages are exempt from the system because they "seem" relationship-free; building one giant language mind map that becomes unmanageable; rote-memorising every word separately instead of finding roots; assuming patterns always exist — in a new language you often cannot find this level of organisation, and you may not have time. Half an hour to an hour of browsing usually yields several new patterns, or at least makes some words more memorable.

## Chunkmapping Demonstrations
**Chunk mapping** (technically: inquiry-led relational priority chunk mapping) is the instructor's note-taking method — a variation of mind mapping that is more consistently effective and more transferable across subjects (including maths) than typical mind mapping, concept mapping or process mapping. Two worked demonstrations teach it.

Chunk map vs conventional mind map (Clinic 23):
- They look similar but the cognitive process differs. A **conventional mind map** maps information non-linearly with basic grouping (by overlap or how it's taught); it has a hard cap on efficiency and retention relies on memorising where things sit on the page. A **chunk map** makes explicit chunks + a clear backbone + organisational structure the dominant focus, built via inquiry.
- Correct flow: raw info → questions → relationships → reorder/repackage → compare/contrast/evaluate/synthesise → memorable chunks → non-linear format. Wrong flow: info (maybe pre-chunked) → mind map → relationships added → vague questions popping in (isolated processes, not a chain).
- A specific bad style (taught by a certain "mind map expert" business): branches with things written on lines, meaningless intersections, not explicit about schemas (e.g. 12 things coming off one point) — it messes up the knowledge schema. Start on a clean slate with these techniques; mixing other methods muddies the picture.
- Test: ask "where are the main chunks and how do they relate?" If you can't see them, it's a mind map, not a chunk map.

Core discipline — skeleton first:
- Build the main-chunk skeleton (the ~3–5 biggest chunks across the ENTIRE topic and how they relate) BEFORE the leaves/details. Focus on the main trees, not the weeds. Of ~10 hours on a topic, spending the first hour only on the backbone is reasonable — it is one of the most important and most difficult steps; once the frame exists every later detail "has a box to belong in" (Clinics 19–21).
- **"Isolation is evil"**: never look at a keyword/concept in isolation as step one; find the relationship first. If what you're reading doesn't create a relationship, skip it and return later. By the time relationships and chunks are built, ~half the information is already obvious and barely needs "learning" (Clinics 19–21).
- House-frame analogy: arranging furniture on an empty plot is hopeless; with the foundation and wooden frame (rooms marked) it's easy — pre-study builds that frame so lecture info has a place. Linear learning is like building a house wall-by-wall (paint, hang picture, then next wall) — inefficient and structurally wrong, which is why past papers feel like "learning it for the first time".
- Wrong fundamental chunking produces a "spider-web between the leaves" pattern: relationships everywhere, chaotic, hard to follow, poor retention, very time/energy consuming — "polishing a turd." No amount of later polish (or pretty visuals) fixes bad chunking. A messy/"cracked-glass" map that feels like a spatial problem is usually a structural problem: no clear backbone was created first.
- Once the frame is right, the rest is easy and problems are easy to fix; if the chunking is fundamentally wrong, a good-looking map is basically impossible. A good map has a clear flow/outline/structure and can hold a lot of detail while staying consistent.
- Study topic-wide, not lecture-by-lecture, to avoid **islands**: separate well-chunked lectures joined by a single arrow = two cities with a highway, not one city with suburbs. Details that don't fit signal missing organisational structure ("you have socks but no sock drawer") — create the drawer via broad importance questions, don't force the detail (Clinics 22–24).
- People who struggle to create structure are usually skipping the main-chunk skeleton stage (Chunkmapping Demo).

Procedure: (1) get the keyword/concept list and chunk it by importance; (2) draw only the main chunks and their relationships first (the skeleton/shape); (3) expand one layer at a time — sub-chunks, then details, keeping the logic clean; (4) add visual imagery last (marker-pen icon recipe in [note-taking](note-taking.md)).

**Special Clinic: Chunkmapping Demonstration** — importance-based chunking applied live to a microorganisms topic:
- "Growth of microbes" (factors + culturing/application) and "role of microorganisms" as equal top chunks; "infectious diseases/pathogens" with "transmission" and "types" (examples, structure/function) nested under pathogens.
- Growth factors sub-chunked by shared importance: enzyme control (temperature, pH), moisture/nutrients, waste removal, antibiotics/antiseptics — six flat items become three organised sub-chunks (rule of four).
- If you can't see why two items belong together, do a quick search to confirm the shared reason before grouping. Increasing the level of organisation makes the map easier to follow and improves encoding.
- Multiple valid chunkings exist (by environmental impact, by positive/negative effects, by breakdown effects, by human application); pick the organisation that makes most sense to you.

**Ascent I: Demonstration of core techniques 2** — a think-aloud demonstration of importance-based chunking on the iCanStudy curriculum concept list, showing the micro-decisions and the persistence standard behind building an optimal backbone map:
- Watch for the **standard**, not just the technique: how far the instructor pursues logical/intuitive, importance-aligned structures. Most student failures come from insufficient comparison of chunk structures, not wrong technique.
- Park uncertain ideas ("leave it on the side") and skip keywords freely; return later — lightbulb moments often arrive after more nodes exist (e.g. the research-practice gap deferred until the rest of the map made sense of it). A keyword that doesn't fit any chunk: don't rebuild the whole structure for 1–2 odd keywords; note them as details to resolve later (Clinic 24).
- Anticipate later concepts: think about the whole list first and leave map space for what is coming; building linearly start-to-finish blows the map out of proportion and forces re-chunking. Knowledge has no start or end — it is omnidirectional. Start anywhere (a keyword you know better, or the top of the list); grow organically; fine-tooth-comb sweep later for anything missed. ~100 keywords should collapse into ~5 major chunks, not 100 chunks; once you see relationships, jump around the list rather than going in order (Clinic 24).
- Drop redundant nodes when the relationship is already implicit (spacing effect removed because it is implicit in spaced retrieval); add visuals only where memory needs the boost (e.g. an image for tug-of-war practice). Aesthetic cleanup (spacing, organisation) makes thinking easier and gives a tired brain a micro-break.
- Prep: get 2–3 hours of your own practice plus at least one COBES cycle on your difficulties before watching, so the nuances land; re-watch later with more experience.
- Per unknown keyword: locate it fast (search the summary doc/textbook rather than re-watching a lecture); ~30 seconds to 2 minutes for a general gist only, then move on. Merge, break and re-chunk repeatedly until the structure cleanly reflects importance; defer awkward regions until more understanding exists.
- Time budget: a topic worth 4–5 lectures can justify 45–60 minutes of pre-study Aim; silent work is faster than think-aloud. For pre-study only, a briefer pass over the most important keywords with a couple of structures is enough. If basic chunking is solid, the later GRINDE-map refinement is rarely the bottleneck.
- Example decisions: spaced retrieval = maintenance of knowledge structures; interleaving = technique applied during retrieval sessions vs spaced retrieval = scheduling of those sessions; microlearning = macro-level strategy; time-to-mastery vs time-to-improvements = knowledge vs skill; practice → cognitive retraining, with method vs duration underneath; knowledge-vs-skill drawn as a long windy road with a mastery flag and an improvement bicep.
- Drawing hack: sketch the outline of the image first, then trace over it with a black pen for cleaner lines; add colour as desired.

## Chunking Quality Diagnostics (Ascent 2 Checkpoint)
Concrete tests for whether mind-map chunking is truly importance-based (logical/intuitive) rather than copied from textbook headings or memorised process chains. Run these before Ascent 3; the full checkpoint context is in [course-guide](course-guide.md).

Tests:
1. **Relabel test**: rename chunks in simpler language (GMOs / bacterial transformation / fragments of DNA → "examples vs mechanism" or "what vs how"). Correct importance-based chunks relabel straightforwardly; if they can't, the chunking is suspect.
2. **Re-derive test**: do the sub-chunks feel intuitive enough that you could figure them out again if you forgot — or would you have to memorise them? Memorise-only chunks mean the Aim step didn't go deep enough on "why is it important".
3. **Sibling-label consistency**: "uniform vs describing" or "orbits vs maintaining" are mismatched splits (expect uniform/non-uniform); inconsistent parallel labels reveal non-importance-based chunking.
4. **Count branches**: one node splitting into six directions breaks the 2–4 rule → not enough chunking.
5. **Single-node chains** (this→this→this): indicates process/information-based chunking rather than importance-based; overall flow and directionality suffer (see Chunking Linear Processes & Sequences).
6. **Importance justification**: reverse causality fallacy = "precipitation is important because it's one of the things that happens to a salt" — importance grounded in a memorised fact, so forgetting the fact leaves nothing to hold on to ([learning-science](learning-science.md)).

Key principles:
- Chunk everything as much and as simply as possible, at every level including the concepts layer ("chunking to the edges"); constantly ask "could this arrangement work better?" and simplify the picture.
- Don't think inside the box of the textbook's existing chunks/headings; be willing to move items between chunks (independent chunking + order control). Box-thinking errors are under the radar and hard to self-detect via COBES — they often need expert feedback.
- Work with the biggest chunks FIRST, build the frame, then hang everything off it. Expanding one chunk fully before starting the next is an efficiency drain — chunks appearing "out of nowhere" or from unlabeled arrow intersections signal this.
- Grid-like, ultra-organised maps that all look the same lose the unique-shape memory cue; vary spatial arrangement so each map's shape serves as an additional memory cue.
- Arrows should reflect a flow of importance, not just cause-and-effect (cause-effect framing was acceptable in Technique Training but is not conceptually valid chunking now).
- Technique must be consistent across topics and survive added detail — watch for deterioration (crossing lines, logic layer getting lost as information grows). Ability must not depend on how well the topic was taught. Add more detail to a clean map to stress-test how you handle higher volume on one page (Clinic 19).
- Biggest limitation for most students: not spending enough time getting the logic layer super clean — thinking only about individual relationships instead of intuitive, importance-based grouping.

Examples: physics map — gravity branching six ways (2–4 rule violation); vectors→force→motion chains with unclear arrow meaning; likely cause = superficial Aim step / reverse causality. Economics — "allocation of resources" + "max number of resources" should chunk together under "resources"; "inequality" may be an example of "when it goes wrong" (which could split short-term vs long-term). Disease topic — "classifying diseases in population vs infection": infection may be a type of disease in the population, or the label should reflect the physiology; relabel for accuracy.

## Chunking for Medicine & Anatomy
Medical content (treatment algorithms, disease clusters, drug lists) and memorisation-heavy anatomy must be chunked independently around conceptual understanding and prior knowledge — never memorised in the taught algorithmic order first. There is a dedicated "chunking for medicine" Spot the Issue challenge; see also Clinic 25 for maths-style procedural subjects (Clinic 28).

Meaning before memorisation — lists & clinical facts (Clinic 16):
- Lists (e.g. disease symptoms) must first be made meaningful — why each item matters, how it will be used in reality, how items relate — then chunked, and only then rote-learned. Memorising a list attached to a label is non-intuitive framing that guarantees flashcard failures.
- **Real-world use is the reverse of the list**: patients present isolated facts (sore throat, sick since Thursday, chest pain, high BP), not diagnoses — you must practise creating meaning FROM isolated facts, not regurgitating lists.
- Reason mechanistically: heart pain receptors live in the lining, not the muscle → a heart attack can be painless if the lining isn't affected; irritating blood can cause pain. Nuances ("could have chest pain, might not") become logical. Interrogate the detail: what TYPE of chest pain (crushing vs stabbing), WHERE (left/central/right), what makes it better/worse — each difference is meaningful (e.g. dry eye: viral → watery, bacterial → sore, allergic).
- First, second and third steps are always: create more meaning → make it logical/intuitive → relate it to other things / why important / how used. Rote (flashcards) is the LAST step. Then chunk benignly: pain-related vs breathing-related symptoms (3+3) instead of six isolated items.
- Medicine still requires substantial memorisation — but meaning-first makes flashcards accurate. Flashcards are unsustainable only because you get them wrong; getting every card right is sustainable (unless you have tens of thousands). Getting the same card wrong six times vs right every time is the difference meaning-making buys (Clinic 16).

Algorithms, diseases and drug groups (Clinics 22, 28, 29):
- Treatment algorithms are hard to understand unless you know WHY the algorithm is the way it is. Learn treatment principles conceptually first; then the algorithm becomes logical ("address these symptoms first, then investigate because they're ready, then this treatment").
- **Never sacrifice first-encoding quality to fit an algorithm or assessment format**: learning it algorithmically takes ~10x longer. Encode efficiently, then practise retrieving/manipulating it in algorithmic (or any assessed) form later.
- **Chunk by shared symptomology to mirror how you'll see patients** (symptoms/signs → diagnosis) (Clinic 22). Patterns reduce memorisation: inflammatory + not viral + possibly infectious + pain → assess inflammation (blood test), infection (blood culture), give antibiotics/steroids/pain relief — intuitive, logical, less rote.
- Chunk by what is intuitive relative to your EXISTING prior knowledge, not what "feels intuitive after studying it today" — the goal is minimising rote memorisation. Sometimes pathology-based chunks linked to presentation beat presentation-only chunks; it depends on the disease cluster.
- Similarity alone is not a criterion (see Importance-Based Chunking). Better discriminative chunks for a virus cluster: type of exposure (e.g. overseas travel in the history), severity/overall impact, or findings unique to only a few viruses — not "can cause rash" or AST/ALT elevation shared by all (Clinic 29).
- For drug groups with several good groupings (effects, contraindications, administration): define your criteria for "good" (most logical? least memorisation? best for retrieval?), consider how the information will be used (contraindications may be the critical part; or route — thigh injection vs oral), and you can layer structures (primary chunk by urgency of use, secondary by contraindications). Some repetition is inevitable either way; choose the simpler structure. EMT example: one map for effects, one for contraindications, one for administration → evaluate and possibly combine rather than keeping all three separately (Clinic 28).

Procedure: (1) build conceptual understanding of the condition(s) and general treatment principles first; (2) generate multiple candidate groupings (pathology, presentation, exposure, severity, unique findings, urgency, contraindications…); (3) compare candidates — which leverages prior knowledge best, needs least memorisation, and suits how you must retrieve it; (4) encode with the winning structure, then practise retrieval in the assessed form.

Anatomy — strategic chunking (deep dive; the full Aim procedure and integration frameworks are in [aiming](aiming.md)):
- Isolated information is not retained; anything that can't be integrated must be memorised — minimise that volume. Multiple integration routes usually exist; pick the most strategic for your learning needs. There is no inherently "correct" chunking for anatomy.
- For anatomy, importance-based chunking only works ~50% of the time; when it fails, use any relationship you can (embryological derivation, clinical relevance, common injury mechanisms) to add memory cues instead of rote memorisation (Clinic 20).
- Chunk patterns repeat heavily across regions; new terms usually just name new parts ("this knob vs that knob"), so cognitive load per keyword stays low despite high keyword counts. Once you know how to think about anatomy, the same approach works for upper/lower limb, thorax, cranial vault, etc.
- **A chunk structure that doesn't make the density easier to remember is not a good one.** In dense regions (forearm/hand), simple structures (anterior/posterior, medial/lateral, flexion/extension) fail because the hard details (origins/insertions, nerve branchings, myotomes/dermatomes, vascular supply) end up in the leaves — and you'd never forget anterior vs posterior anyway. Choose structures that capture the difficult information: e.g. chunk by characteristic clinical presentations (wrist laceration, swollen elbow, crushed hand, dislocated finger) — a few presentations, not hundreds.
- **Landmark strategy**: deeply integrate the most forgettable/complex parts first → they become strong, memorable landmarks; surrounding parts become easier to link and infer between landmarks (e.g. a vessel's course inferred between two known clinically important points); remaining isolated gaps use memorisation techniques. Full integration of everything is not time-feasible pre-clinically — pockets of integration are the goal.
- Combine frameworks per region (related structures, embryology, presentation, examination, management); swap frameworks when one stops yielding. Brachial plexus → memorisation technique + pathology focus ("what if this trunk is severed — what would I expect, how would I examine?").
- Anatomical variation is real (nerve pathways differ between people) — memorise implications/consequences rather than exact textbook paths.
- Clinical patient-flow mapping (vs diagnostic algorithms) and importance-based early chunks ("symptom pattern" / "pain distribution" instead of binary "motor affected yes/no") are covered in [professional-learning](professional-learning.md).

Common mistakes: memorising "1. give this drug, 2. give that drug, 3. refer…" as the starting point; flashcarding "symptoms of X" lists as the first step; chunking diseases purely by clinical presentation because it felt intuitive that day; grouping by a similarity that carries no discriminative relevance; asking "am I ready for Ascent 2?" with algorithm-focused, passive maps showing no independent importance-based chunking — that work is mid-Technique-Training level (Clinic 28).

## Repeating Chunk Patterns & the Pattern Database
Patterns of importance legitimately repeat across topics and subjects (e.g. "real-world application" in maths; "signs/symptoms → diagnosis → management" in medicine). Reusing a chunk pattern is fine IF you independently arrive at it each time; experience builds a personal "database" of recurring patterns that makes chunking faster (Clinic 29).

- Knowledge is structured so importance patterns recur; reusing "application" as a chunk is acceptable, but you must still actively check whether a simpler/better grouping exists in this sitting — don't default to the comfortable pattern (common medical-student trap). "Just because it CAN fit these chunks doesn't mean it SHOULD."
- You can be more specific (which application?) to keep processing deep.
- **The pattern library compounds** (Clinic 15): the more you chunk, the more reusable thinking patterns you accumulate; eventually new topics map onto an existing pattern and you "just fill in the details". A domain like biology has only ~7–8 framework patterns covering virtually any topic. Distinguish patterns of THINKING (frameworks for content) from patterns of LEARNING (the habits you use to learn). Start building patterns early — late starters face growing content with no pattern foundation, so it gets harder, not easier.
- Beginners have zero stored patterns (hence chunking is hard); experienced learners have ~50 recurring patterns worked through hundreds of times and combine 3–4 of them for new material — faster relevance-finding with less load. Every subject has repeating knowledge-schema patterns bound by its relationships; at a certain level no new patterns emerge, which is why experts reuse patterns learned years ago and learn faster (Clinics 23, 29).
- Justin's hypothesis (explicitly flagged as his theory, research-sparse): cognitive load matters because it produces good chunk structure AND relevance; if relevance already exists, the load may not be strictly necessary — BUT the only way to build the pattern database is to go through the independent-chunking struggle first.
- **Fadeout effect**: a perfect teacher who makes everything easy removes your need to engage independently; later, when concepts are too complex for even the best teacher to simplify (or the teacher changes), you lack the skill to create structure yourself.

Common mistakes: letting a familiar pattern (diagnosis/management) auto-apply without evaluation; worrying that repeated chunks mean reduced processing while actually re-deriving them independently (that's fine). If you catch yourself pre-chunking into a familiar frame, stop, unchunk, and re-run inquiry — the frame may be contaminating your importance answers (Clinic 29).
