# Sleep, Rest & Recovery
> Sleep, rest and recovery are the physiological foundation of the whole learning system: sleep consolidates what you study and sustains energy, emotion and focus; timed rest protects energy across the day; and deliberate recovery rescues low-energy days — so every hour studied actually converts into usable, retrievable knowledge.

## Overview
This topic is the **body-and-energy layer** underneath every technique. [learning-science](learning-science.md) depends on sleep-dependent consolidation to make studied information retrievable; [focus-attention](focus-attention.md) depends on the energy and emotional stability sleep provides; [exam-technique](exam-technique.md) depends on being awake and alert enough to execute. The course treats sleep as a **non-negotiable prerequisite**, not a lifestyle optimisation: "sleep is for the smart", and compromising it compromises learning, mood, health and exam performance at once.

It covers four things: (1) the **science of sleep** — consolidation, functions, stages, debt and how to find your number; (2) **sleep hygiene and protection** — the practical rules that keep sleep quality high and consistent; (3) **rest and recovery** — break timing, rest quality, fatigue diagnosis and low-energy-day rules; and (4) **sleep and diet** — meal timing that protects exam-day alertness. The full work/rest cycling technique (OFF-rest) lives in [time-management](time-management.md); focus-training/active-relaxation technique lives in [focus-attention](focus-attention.md); this note links to them rather than repeating them.

## Sleep-Dependent Memory Consolidation
The single most important reason sleep is non-negotiable for students. Newly learned information sits in a **short-term storage area** of the brain and must be **consolidated into higher-level cortices during deep sleep (stages 3–4)** before it becomes freely accessible and usable.

**Key principles**
- Without consolidation, much of what you study stays **trapped in inaccessible brain regions** — you spend hours studying and cannot use it in practice or exams. Study time without sleep largely goes "into the bin".
- Poor sleep can make **40–50% of studied material inaccessible during an exam** — the worst combination: knowing you studied it but being unable to recall it (Clinic 10).
- Sleep is what makes studied information retrievable for solving questions, discussing and applying it — knowledge becomes "more your own".
- You need not just sleep but **deep sleep** for consolidation.
- All-night cramming immediately before a test locks information in short-term storage; you cannot access it during the exam.

**Pre-exam rule**
- **Never stay up the night immediately before a test.** You may stay up the night *before that*, but get a solid, long block of sleep right before the test so everything consolidates.
- Be conservative: aim for **9–10 hours on each of the two nights before** an exam; if you must pull all-nighters, do them the **week prior**, never the final two nights — "don't argue with this, get your sleep" (Clinic 10). Extra sleep improves performance.
- Sleep deprivation can drop IQ by 10–20 points and impair executive processing — you would be both unable to use information *and* less able to solve problems.

**Practical tips**
- Stopping a session when tired is fine: consolidation strengthens the networks overnight, so the next session is easier with higher retention (Clinic 40).
- "Sleep is for the smart", not the weak — protecting sleep protects the return on every hour studied.

## Functions of Sleep & Effects of Deprivation
Beyond consolidation, sleep maintains hormonal/emotional balance, the stress response and teenage brain development. Chronic deprivation quietly raises baseline stress, destabilises emotions and damages health and cognition.

**Key principles**
- The **circadian (day–night) rhythm** governs hormonal balance (especially cortisol), brain development, learning and creativity.
- Prolonged stress triggers the **resistance reaction** — a long-term fight-or-flight state where the body summons energy: weaker immune system, slower healing, muscle breakdown. Insufficient sleep pushes the body toward this state.
- Sleep loss raises baseline stress **very slowly**, so you do not notice it day-to-day — compare yourself to 2–3 years ago. Hormones strongly influence emotions, so you become emotionally fragile, short-tempered and less patient (hurting relationships).
- **Secondary brain development** in the teenage years builds cognitive maturity, critical thinking, imagination and IQ; deprivation impairs it.
- The lesson cites (unverified by Justin) studies suggesting a sleep-deprived day can drop IQ by 10–20 points, and claims 1–2 hours less sleep per day is comparable to obesity in health damage.
- Sleep quantity/quality underpins the energy needed to enter flow — low-energy repetitive tasks (e.g. routine web dev) can be done sleep-deprived, but high-load active studying cannot (Clinic 19). See [focus-attention](focus-attention.md).

**Common mistakes**
- Believing "sleep is for the weak" and trading sleep for study time — it makes you dumber *and* wastes the studying (no consolidation).
- Not noticing slowly accumulating stress and emotional instability because there is no day-to-day contrast.

## Sleep Stages & Architecture
Sleep proceeds through stages **1** (drowsy transition), **2** (light sleep), **3** (deep sleep begins) and **4** (labelled REM in the lesson — an active brain state with vivid dreams). The benefits of sleep come from **deep sleep**, which requires prolonged, uninterrupted blocks.

**Key principles**
- Stages 1–2 are checkpoints/transitions; you must spend time at each level to reach stages 3–4 where consolidation and restoration happen.
- **REM periods lengthen across the night**: early cycles may be only 5–10 minutes, later ones 30+ minutes — another reason total sleep must be prolonged and uninterrupted.
- **Fragmented sleep defeats the architecture**: waking every ~2 hours resets you to stage 1, so 8 total hours may contain almost no deep sleep and you still wake tired. Remove night distractions (phone on do-not-disturb).
- **Natural waking rule**: if you wake naturally ~10 minutes before your alarm, do **not** go back to sleep — you will drop into stage 3 and be woken from deep sleep, feeling groggier. Only return to sleep if you can get at least ~1 hour (enough for a full deep-sleep cycle; e.g. waking at 6 am with a 7:30 alarm, go back to sleep).
- **Polyphasic sleep myths** (e.g. three ~1.5-hour sleeps per day, ~4 hours total, loosely recalled as a "Goldman sleep cycle") theoretically force direct entry into deep sleep, but studies of split/short-burst patterns show they do not work — the brain is adapted to **one prolonged, single, uninterrupted session**. There is no way to hack less sleep and make more time.

**Common mistakes**
- Cramming extra short naps ("just 30 more minutes") after naturally waking — it makes you feel worse, not more rested.
- Believing total hours matter more than continuous deep-sleep blocks.
- Chasing polyphasic schedules to "save" time.

## Sleep Debt & Circadian Rhythm
Sleep debt accrues over time like any debt and can only be repaid with **consistently sufficient sleep over a long period**. A disrupted circadian rhythm produces the double-edged state of nighttime **insomnia** plus daytime **hypersomnolence** (excessive sleepiness).

**Key principles**
- Justin's cautionary story: 2–3 hours of sleep per night in first-year university led to hypersomnolence (falling asleep while talking, driving, in any dark room), excessive napping that wrecked his day–night rhythm, and insomnia at night — too much sleep by day, too little by night.
- A few good nights do not clear years of debt; recovery requires consistently adequate sleep for a long time (it took Justin ~4–5 years).
- The circadian rhythm is central to healthy hormonal balance, brain development, learning and creativity.

**Practical tips**
- Figure out your sleep number early and stick to it — Justin's 4–5 years of guessing and accruing debt made his whole journey harder.
- Aim to hit your required sleep band on **~29 of 30 days**; occasional short nights happen, but consistency is what repays debt.

## Determining Your Sleep Need
A calibration protocol to find the **minimum sleep duration at which you wake rested**, so you can block it into your schedule. Most people need **8–9 hours** (8 is the lower end); few need under 8 and very, very few under 7.

**Key principles**
- Sleep need is a bell curve: most people sit at 8–9 hours; some highly productive people need 9–9.5. Justin's golden zone is 8–8.5 hours; below 7.5 he is tired, below 7 it is "not a good time".
- You must know your number to plan around it; guessing for years accrues debt. Many students have never slept more than 6.5 hours before the course — the course starts by prioritising ~8 hours (Clinic 25).

**Technique / steps**
1. Sleep with **no alarm, undisturbed, for 7–10 days** (it takes ~3–4 days to break your old wake-up rhythm). Note hours slept and whether you feel rested on waking.
2. Start at **9 hours for a full week**. If still tired, bump to 9.5 for a week. If still tired at 9.5, suspect sleep **quality** (e.g. anxiety) rather than duration.
3. Once rested, drop by **30-minute chunks, one week per step**, until you reach the point where any less makes you wake tired — that is your number.
4. Block that number into your schedule and hit it ~29 of 30 days.

**Common mistakes**
- Testing each duration for only a night or two — the body needs ~a week to forget the previous rhythm.
- Assuming you are a short-sleeper; most people need more than they think.

## Sleep Hygiene & Guidelines
Practical rules that keep sleep quality high and consistent. The dedicated hygiene lesson (3.2) — which 3.1 says includes a wind-down routine for anxiety-driven sleep-quality problems — has no usable transcript in the course files; the rules below are reconstructed from the sleep lesson, the focus must-do and clinics.

**Wind-down & environment**
- Build a consistent pre-sleep routine: light exercise, reading, meditation, **turn the phone off ~30 minutes before bed**, and **do not use the phone in bed** (Clinic 13).
- **Offload tension during the day** (breaks, relaxation, focus training) so you are not saving it all for bedtime, where it is the first time you try to relax and suddenly realise how much you have been holding (Clinic 13). See [mindset-emotion](mindset-emotion.md) and [focus-attention](focus-attention.md).
- Keep a **prolonged, single, uninterrupted** sleep block; phone on do-not-disturb; remove anything that wakes you (3.1).
- **Separate the sleep/play zone from the study/work zone** (a curtain or divider in a studio apartment) so the bed is not conditioned as a work or distraction space (Clinic 29). See [habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md).

**Sound & hacks**
- **White noise / rain sounds** are a legitimate, calming sleep aid that blocks distractions; Justin plays rain sounds as part of his wind-down (Clinic 13, Clinic 26).
- **Binaural beats** (alpha/beta/gamma waves) have some evidence for inducing sleep but research is mixed; they require uncomfortable sleep-adapted earphones. General sleep hygiene and course consistency make a **bigger, more reliable impact** than these marginal hacks (Clinic 26).

**Napping**
- Daytime napping is **usually best to avoid**, especially if you have night-sleep issues — it disrupts the circadian rhythm (Clinic 13, Clinic 25).
- If genuinely exhausted, a **brief timed nap of ~20–25 minutes** is ideal (roughly one sleep cycle, so you are not abruptly woken from deep sleep). When drowsy, a short nap beats coffee (Clinic 21).
- A timed nap can also anchor a pre-exam ritual: one student always showered then took a 20-minute nap before every exam — and crushed them (ritualization, Maintaining Focus). See [exam-technique](exam-technique.md).
- But if you *constantly* need naps, the real fix is to **optimise your night sleep**, not nap more. Justin napped for years thinking it was normal; once he fixed his sleep he napped only 2–3 times a year, after poor nights (Clinic 13).

**Consistency**
- Hit your sleep band ~29 of 30 days; know your number; follow the natural-waking rule; do not chase polyphasic hacks (3.1).

**Common mistakes:** phone in bed; saving all relaxation for bedtime; chasing binaural/polyphasic hacks instead of consistent hygiene; afternoon naps to compensate for poor night sleep.

## Protecting Sleep: The Non-Negotiable Platform
Sleep is a **non-negotiable prerequisite** for focus, learning and exam execution — protect it at all costs, even when stressed. Sleep compromise is almost never worth it: it affects everything (Clinic 20).

**Key principles**
- Sleep is the **first of the four exam fundamentals** — sleep, sufficient encoding, sufficient spaced interleaved retrieval, and calm confidence; ~99.999% of exam problems trace to these four (Clinic 10). See [exam-technique](exam-technique.md).
- Sleep drives energy, mood/emotional stability and focus — the whole platform for productive study. Going from **5 h to 8 h can buy back ~2 h of daily efficiency**; a solid platform beats a fragile, sleep-deprived, emotionally unstable one.
- **Cramming's downward spiral**: more hours → lower efficiency → less sleep → lower retention → higher error rate. Sleep-dependent consolidation also fails, so you cannot use what you learned. In effective cramming, **protecting optimal sleep is guideline 3**, not an optional extra — see [revision](revision.md).
- **Transient silly mistakes are often just sleep deprivation**; the fix is more sleep and rest, not more technique (4.4). See [exam-technique](exam-technique.md).
- Being nearly asleep during an exam **caps your result**, because you spend the time fighting to stay awake (4.5).
- **Rest is productive** when mental health needs it; never compromise mental/emotional health — it affects focus, concentration, raw learning ability and sleep. See [time-management](time-management.md) (priority myths) and [mindset-emotion](mindset-emotion.md).

**Sustainable routines**
- Design sleep/wake routines to be **sustainable and flexible**: if you cannot do it on your worst day, do not build it. Consistency is the #1 influencer of whether a behaviour sticks (Crippling Anxiety Clinic).
- To shift a wake time, **acclimatise gradually** (e.g. 10 am → 8 am for a week → 6 am → 5 am), adding study only once waking is stable. Do not jump straight to an extreme (Crippling Anxiety Clinic). See [habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md) and [time-management](time-management.md).
- Reject **toxic productivity**: extreme schedules give a temporary boost that equalises, and are usually socially non-viable. Consistent, average-looking routines sustained for years beat extreme ones; balance rest and social life (Crippling Anxiety Clinic). See [mindset-emotion](mindset-emotion.md).

**Common mistakes:** hustle culture (4-hour nights, early rises, "maximum every day"); cutting sleep to study more during stress — the studied information then cannot be consolidated or used.

## Sleep Scheduling & Early-Rising Myths
There is **no universally optimal sleep/wake clock** — productivity is driven by when your mind is primed to view time as focused and distraction-free, not by the hour on the clock (Clinic 25).

**Key principles**
- **Early-rising gains are short-term.** Waking at 4–5 am feels productive, but the gains diminish to baseline (sometimes within a week). Switching to a nocturnal schedule produces the *same* temporary gains again — proving the benefit is novelty/priming, not the clock hour.
- **Fluctuating cycles hurt sleep quality.** Constantly shifting early/late undermines the regularity and routineness of sleep, which is bad for quality.
- Justin's rule (not viable for fixed-hour people): **go to sleep when tired, wake when rested**, and plan around fixed wake times only when required. Figure out what works, stick with it until it stops working, then change it.
- You cannot "dial in" sleep timing to produce sustainable, large performance gains — the magnitude of effect people hope for does not last (Clinic 25).

This mirrors the [time-management](time-management.md) priority myth that **schedule-switching productivity equalises**, and the [mindset-emotion](mindset-emotion.md) warning against chasing impressive-but-unsustainable routines.

## Rest Timing: Frequent Short Breaks
The beginner rest pattern: take breaks at **higher frequency and lower duration** — work ~20–30 minutes while still relatively fresh, then rest 5–10 minutes — rather than grinding 1–2 hours until depleted and needing a huge recovery.

**Key principles**
- The brain regains focus much more easily when it still has **reserve**; full depletion takes disproportionately long to recover from (running analogy: 100 m + 5 min rest repeatedly covers more distance than 30–40 min exhausted + 4 h recovery).
- Goal: **steadily high energy across the whole day** until natural tiredness at night, then good sleep and repeat — not high morning energy, dead by 3 pm, "walking dead" for the rest of term.
- Short frequent breaks are repaid through higher efficiency.

**Technique / steps**
1. Work ~20–30 minutes.
2. Take a **5–10 minute break while you still feel relatively fresh**; set a timer (5, 10 or at most ~15 minutes).
3. Repeat; if breaks are frequent enough you rarely need >15 minutes.

**Common mistakes:** waiting until completely drained, then taking 30-minute to multi-hour (or multi-day) breaks; not using a timer — a "10-minute" break becomes a 10-hour break.

The **full OFF-rest technique** (rest = work ÷ 3, rest when focus goes *dull* not *tired*, productive break vs active relaxation, eye rest and greenery) is taught in [time-management](time-management.md); this basic pattern is later replaced by it. You can adopt OFF-rest directly — no Pomodoro prerequisite (Clinic 19).

**Intentional rest vs procrastination (Clinic 19)**
- Rest must be an **intentional decision**: deciding to take the day off (phone off, present, hike/beach/show) is rest; intending to work but watching 4 hours of YouTube is procrastination — it makes you more tired and worsens mental health.
- **Schedule leisure** into the calendar (e.g. 2 hours of gaming at day's end) so daytime urges can be deferred guilt-free. On a low-energy day, intentionally take it off and hit harder the next day. Reframe: "when should you *not* take breaks?" See [time-management](time-management.md) (protecting time).
- **Rest is rest**: do not run productivity techniques while relaxing (Clinic 15).

**Micro-breaks & strain (Clinic 14)**
- Sore eyes or a headache from long focus mean you **must** rest — hydration and good sleep reduce vulnerability.
- Even **30 seconds with eyes closed during an exam** is a legitimate reset that costs almost nothing and restores focus; while studying, use strategic rests rather than pushing through strain.

**Active recovery: relaxation & meditation**
- You cannot turn the brain off; the best alternative is to turn it onto something **very low-energy** (holding a pen, watching the breath) so it rests. **Active relaxation** (focus training, ~5 min) recharges far faster than **passive relaxation** (scrolling), which is slow and only fine without a time limit. ~15 minutes of focus training can feel as restful as ~2 hours of passive scrolling. See [focus-attention](focus-attention.md) for the technique.
- **Meditation as recovery between exams**: 20–30 minutes of meditation in a break can give restoration comparable to restful deep sleep (skilled meditators reach a similar low-frequency brainwave state, shown in EEG), without a full REM cycle. Even 3–4 minutes noticeably raises energy, focus and calm. It is an **adjunct, not a replacement** for sleep (4.6). Example: Ajahn Brahm meditated through a lunch break during ~9 hours of exams while friends suffered post-meal somnolence. See [exam-technique](exam-technique.md).

## Study Fatigue: Tired vs Drowsy
Feeling tired after a high-load study session is **normal and a sign you are doing it right**; drowsy is different (Clinic 28).

**Key principles**
- **Tired ≠ drowsy**: tired = energy spent (good sign — even Justin gets tired studying properly; if you are never tired you are probably not doing it right). Drowsy = **sleep-deprived** (bad — fix sleep).
- **Drowsiness while studying when *not* sleep-deprived** means the brain has switched off — the learning process is not engaging enough. See [learning-science](learning-science.md) (cognitive load) and [focus-attention](focus-attention.md).
- With time, the load stops bothering you and becomes intrinsically motivating — information feels "spongy", taken in easily (Clinic 28).
- **Second-topic quality drop** (even after a break) is a common, "good" problem. Fixes: longer breaks; optimising *what* you do during rest (OFF-rest); exercise, a meal, or chilling between blocks; Justin leaves a couple of hours between blocks (morning/evening split). **Batching alternative**: study one paper/topic for 3–4 days straight, then switch — staying in one mode avoids switching costs.
- Do not go straight from a good session into a different domain — it "feels bad"; insert real rest (Clinic 28).
- **Gaming breaks**: only if you can genuinely stop after a couple of hours. If starting games means the day is gone, do not. Justin does not game until all important work is done — and sometimes rest is more important than another work block (Clinic 28).
- **Menstrual cycle and load tolerance** (Clinic 28): no published research; from first principles it is plausible (neurohormonal shifts affect executive function and mood), but substantial limitation is probably rare — many top performers are female and no pattern emerged across thousands of students.
- Use **COBES cycles** to find the marginal improvements for your specific fatigue pattern (Clinic 28). See [learning-science](learning-science.md).

**When sleepy, choose the study type that keeps you awake (Clinic 11)**
- Work backwards: instead of "how do I study to stay alert?", ask **"what activity guarantees I will not fall asleep?"**, then make it a little more study-related.
- Example: walk outside while casually mind-mapping from memory in a notebook and looking things up on your phone; add light exercise; return to desk work when alertness recovers. Reserve this mode for the slump window, not as your main study method.
- If normal studying makes you fall asleep at the desk within 15 minutes, a study type that is only ~30% as effective but keeps you **awake** is the better option.

**Practical tip:** feeling tired/demotivated by 7–8 pm despite 8–9 hours of sleep and daily exercise is often **accumulated muscular/mental tension** catching up as energy drops — not necessarily a sleep problem. Offload tension several times a day (scan and relax shoulders/neck/head) so it never stacks until bedtime (Clinic 13). See [mindset-emotion](mindset-emotion.md).

## Low-Energy Days: The Energy-First Recovery Rule
On genuinely low-energy days, choosing the "most productive" task at ~20% efficiency yields little and can worsen a negative feedback loop. Instead choose the activity that **maximises energy + engagement**, which restores efficiency and is a net win even if it costs study time (Clinic 34).

**Key principles**
- Even good self-management is only ~70–90% effective; some days you will feel cruddy — accept it and be kind to yourself (you are a regular human, not a perfect productivity specimen).
- Pushing productivity at low efficiency forces more time to compensate and deepens fatigue (tomorrow at 10%) — a downward spiral toward burnout.
- Prioritising energy + enjoyment breaks the loop: a short rejuvenation lifts you to ~80% efficiency (net win on less time), or a longer recovery (even 1–2 days) still wins because it stops the multi-week decline.
- For minor "lost edge" (not true low energy), use **BEDS-M, Higher Ground strategies and environmental optimisation** instead. See [habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md).

**Technique / steps**
1. Gauge whether you have merely lost the edge (optimise environment, BEDS-M, Higher Ground) or are truly low-energy.
2. If truly low-energy, ask: **"What activity would leave me most energised and engaged afterward?"** (walk, gym, run, socialising).
3. Do it for a short or long period as needed, with enough self-management that it does not become week-long procrastination; let efficiency recover.
4. Return to focused work at restored efficiency; accept the time trade as a medium/long-term win.

**Practical tips**
- **Going for a walk outside** is the easiest, disproportionately effective reset: light aerobic movement, looking far away (pupillary relaxation → parasympathetic response), and lateral gaze shifts (may deactivate the amygdala, reducing fear/sympathetic response). Justin caveats the neuroscience but values the effect (Clinic 34).
- Extreme illustration: if you will fall asleep at the library desk within 20 minutes and waste the day, an hour at the gym/socialising that buys 1–1.5 hours of good focus is an obvious win vs near-zero productivity (Clinic 34).
- Justin's own off day: woke at 11 am, lunch, mobile games on the couch, wrapped in blankets like a burrito for hours — no self-criticism (Clinic 34).
- **All-nighters: usually NO**; a rare last resort only when genuinely unavoidable (e.g. never a routine weekend trade to "get ahead"). They badly hit efficiency and take a long time to recover from (Clinic 34).
- Huberman Lab is a decent resource for neurophysiological/cognitive effects (Clinic 34).

**Common mistakes:** choosing the conventionally "most productive" task at 20% efficiency and feeding the fatigue loop; beating yourself up for an unproductive day instead of resting; expecting recovery tactics to work 100% of the time.

## Sleep & Diet
Optimise sleep **and** food so exam-day performance is not lost for trivial reasons (4.5).

**Postprandial somnolence (post-meal sleepiness) & the afternoon slump**
- After a meal you feel tired roughly **1–2 hours later** ("postprandial somnolence": post = after, prandial = eating, somnolence = sleepiness). The afternoon dip is largely physiological and unavoidable: cortisol (alertness drive) peaks shortly after waking and bottoms out around midday, on top of the post-meal effect (Clinic 11).
- The course's simplified model: blood is **shunted from the brain** (and skeletal muscle) to the digestive system as part of **rest-and-digest**; bigger meal → bigger shunt → more sleepiness.
- **Refinement (Clinic 11):** cerebral blood pressure/perfusion is actually well-maintained, so the shunt model is incomplete — the sleepiness is more likely a **complex neurochemical/hormonal change** after eating. Skipping lunch makes you less sleepy; a big lunch makes you sleepier.
- The only override is **fight-or-flight** (i.e. exam anxiety), which is worse because anxiety destroys calm recall.
- Digestive effort ranking: **protein (meats) > fats > carbohydrates > processed carbohydrates / pure sugar**. But *any* meal produces the effect.

**Pre-exam meal timing**
- **No big meal in the ~2 hours before anything that matters.** For a 4 pm test, avoid big meals from about 2 pm onward.
- **Snacks are fine**: half a protein or muesli bar, or an apple, is not a big enough meal to cause a meaningful effect. If very hungry, have a small snack rather than a full meal.
- Accepted trade-off: **rather be hungry and energised than full, sluggish and groggy** (4.6).

**Brain foods & stimulants**
- "Brain foods" are **modest, not magic**: a normal healthy balanced diet with healthy fats (e.g. nuts), not over- or under-eating, supports hormonal balance. Do not get pedantic — five almonds a day is not what is holding you back.
- **Nootropics/stimulants are marginal** (Clinic 21): relatively safe ones (caffeine, L-theanine) are fine but avoid experimental compounds. **Caffeine worsens anxiety and hyper-distractibility**, so it does not help focus there; and if you use the techniques correctly your cognitive load is already high enough that you do not need them. When drowsy, prefer a short nap over coffee. See [focus-attention](focus-attention.md).

**Common mistakes:** eating a large lunch right before an afternoon exam, then fighting sleepiness; trying to override the effect with anxiety.
