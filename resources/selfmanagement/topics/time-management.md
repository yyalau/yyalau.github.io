# Time Management, Scheduling & Prioritisation
> Choosing the highest-value tasks, protecting the time for them, and executing without waste — managing your attention and priorities rather than the clock itself.

## Overview
This is the efficiency layer that makes the rest of the system practicable: without it, succeeding in the course is "a long and tiring struggle" ([course-guide](course-guide.md), Rapid Start). It covers task vs time management, prioritisation (top-two, consequence-of-failure, Priority 0+1, Eisenhower), scheduling mechanics (mistakes, guidelines, underscheduling, Google Calendar, study/spacing scheduling), protecting time and sustainability, environmental optimisation, rest and attention (OFF-rest, decisional delays, attention management), and applications (microlearning, batch studying, intentional living, exam planning).

Two framings run through everything:
- **"Time management" is a misnomer.** Time flows regardless; you actually manage your *attention* and your *priorities*. An activity done without focus produces nothing, exactly as "studying" without learning does.
- **Efficiency is a means to a holistic life, not an end.** The Bear Hunter System's efficiency buys the hours that make deep revision and a full life affordable ([bear-hunter-system](bear-hunter-system.md), [revision](revision.md)).

The skill progresses: beginner scheduling (Technique Training) → Priority 0+1 (Base Camp) → decisional-delay awareness → attention management (Camp II). Each stage presupposes the last; do not rush to attention management before you can detect decisional delays. Time/task management is interlinked with focus, procrastination and motivation — a beautiful schedule cannot fix wrong priorities or slow encoding ([course-guide](course-guide.md), self-management preview).

## Rapid Time Management: Task vs Time Management
**Task management** = deciding which tasks to do and balancing them (prioritisation). **Time management** = putting them on a schedule and executing daily/weekly. A perfect schedule with bad prioritisation still wastes time on the wrong things.

**Urgency trapping** is filling all your time with urgent tasks while neglecting non-urgent but more important ones that set up your future.
- The brain is bad at medium/long-term prioritisation: wired for short-term survival, it equates urgency with importance, so gut-feel prioritisation is usually wrong.
- Important-but-not-urgent work (study skills, addressing weaknesses before an exam, new career skills) feels postponable until it is too late — students asking for exam help one week out cannot be saved.
- **Schedule by importance, not urgency.** Then even an incomplete day delivered the highest-impact work. Scheduling by urgency means important non-urgent work is perpetually deferred ("swimming to keep your head above water").
- "Luck is when opportunity meets preparation": the same opportunity wastes on the unprepared and transforms the prepared.

**Common mistakes:** letting homework crowd out weakness-identification before a test (homework keeps everyone happy but rarely changes the grade); postponing skill development for months (15 min/day for a month would have freed large amounts of time); scheduling important non-urgent tasks late in the day.

**Tip:** place important-but-not-urgent tasks near the start of your functional day (before class/work, or right after getting home and a break) — never at the end, where urgent tasks always displace them. See the Eisenhower matrix and consequence-based prioritisation below, and positional decisions ([decision-making](decision-making.md)).

## Prioritisation: The Top Two Things
Actively choosing the tasks that give the most value toward your current most-important goal, and building the schedule around them. Good scheduling without good prioritisation just fills time efficiently with meaningless tasks.
- The same six hours can produce nothing or move you decisively; the goal changes week to week (an English essay, family time, self-care before burnout, catching up maths).
- If you don't prioritise, urgency prioritises for you: neglect self-care until a crisis forces you out of school; neglect maths until cramming can't save the grade. **Balance is achieved across weeks/months, not by balancing everything every day.**
- Busy ≠ productive.

**Technique (starter system):**
1. Night before (or start of week), ask: *"If I could only get TWO things done tomorrow (or this week) and still be satisfied, what would they be?"*
2. Write the 2 (max 3) priorities.
3. Schedule those items into the calendar FIRST.
4. Fill remaining time with lower-priority tasks, buffers and rest.
5. Re-evaluate at the end of each day/week.

**Cadence:** weekly priorities in relaxed periods → daily when busy or life changes fast. Do it the night before (weekly: Sunday night; daily: each night). Because you re-prioritise frequently, deferring an "also important" item to tomorrow is fine — over time everything gets through. Even Justin rarely clocks more than three consistently.

**Tips:** the planning ritual itself is relaxing/therapeutic (a feeling of control amid chaos); consistent top-priority completion preserves free time. This is the beginner precursor to Priority 0+1.

## Prioritising by Consequence of Failure
Importance is determined by the **consequence of NOT doing a task**, not by how important it feels. Because consequences change daily, priorities must be re-judged daily against your fixed time budget. (Clinics 10, 14, 15)
- Feeling-based importance is the common trap — "everything feels important" usually means urgency is masquerading as importance. Ask instead: *"what actually happens if I don't do this?"* Write the consequence down; the list usually shrinks dramatically (Clinic 15).
- Genuinely stacked high-consequence categories are rare (e.g. running two businesses plus caregiving). A regular student's homework is not truly high-stakes — worst case is a slap on the wrist, not a weighted grade (Clinic 15).
- **Schedule the high-impact items FIRST** (e.g. 1.5 h including 20–30 min of study-skills work), then cram everything less important into the remaining time — never the reverse ("homework takes 2 h, so only 1 h left for what matters" is backwards) (Clinic 10).
- For low-stakes homework, find the **minimum possible time** to get it done and do it in that minimum; don't let it consume prime time. Truly weighted assignments make protecting priority time even more critical (Clinic 10).
- Family time can be deeply important in general yet lower-consequence *today* than another task — and higher tomorrow. This is task-level prioritisation, not a statement of values (Clinic 10).
- Consistently doing the one most important thing daily for a year transforms your position; losing low-importance items costs little (Clinic 14).

**Common mistakes:** rating importance by gut/urgency; over-inflating a test's value because teachers say it is valuable — check the actual consequence (does it affect university entrance?) before sacrificing experimentation time (Clinic 11); overloading days with trivial obligations at the expense of high-impact work; equating a full calendar with productivity.

**Tips:** investing 20–30 minutes every 2–3 days in the course buys back study time permanently — not having time for the thing that creates time is ultimate self-sabotage (Clinic 10). If you are ahead and teachers allow it, do predictable homework during class; some schools grant library self-study hall passes with periodic check-ins. A downloadable course letter can request using the new techniques — use discretion with old-school teachers (Clinic 10). Justin's task list fell from triple digits to the 30s–50s while output stayed high; pruning is a skill outcome, not laziness (Clinic 14).

## Priority Myths (Speed & Hustle)
Two productivity myths that cap output: (1) working faster makes you more productive; (2) hustling harder makes you more productive. Both fail because quality and direction matter more than speed or effort.
- **Speed ≠ output.** You already work at ~80–90% of your max high-quality speed, so little headroom exists. Speeding up raises error rate, and errors cost far more to fix than to do right (detect → recap/rebuild context → relearn → risk cascading misunderstanding, 1+1=3 → 1+1+2=5).
- Rule of thumb (Justin's experience, not formal research): one error costs ≥4× the time of doing it right; +10% errors ≈ +40% time. Break-even needs ~40% more speed — unachievable without wrecking quality.
- **Exception:** a better *technique* can be ~400% faster — that is changing the process, not rushing the same one. Rushing even BHS produces worse results than a calm, confident, brisk pace.
- **Hustle ≠ productivity.** "Productive" = anything that moves you toward YOUR goals (which differ per person and change over time); a picnic/Netflix can be productive for a relaxed-life goal, and rest is productive when mental health needs it. Mental/emotional health is paramount — never compromise it (it affects focus, concentration, learning ability and sleep).
- Fly-against-the-window: hustling in the wrong direction is effort without progress. Over-hustling causes tunnel vision and reduces reflection time; since energy/mood/goals constantly change, priorities need constant calibration.

**Tip:** put effort into the RIGHT priorities at the right pace with the right process. See accuracy-before-speed ([course-guide](course-guide.md)) and rushing ([habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md)).

## Priority 0+1 System
Justin's personal system: each day identify your single number-one priority (the task that best advances your goals), schedule and protect it first, then fit lower priorities around it; monthly, re-align with **Priority 0** (your values and long-term goals).
- **Priority 1** = the most important thing to get done today; schedule it first and protect that time (block fixed commitments like school, then place the P1 study block; add PEER if you procrastinate — [habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md)).
- Typically 1–3 priorities/day (Justin usually 2); everything else (exercise, family, friends, games, meditation) fits around them.
- If disruption hits, rearrange or DROP lower priorities so P1 always gets done; if P1's block is unavoidably consumed, clear everything else to make room for it.
- Doing only 1–2 high-importance things daily keeps you on top over time: busy weeks fall slightly behind, free weeks catch up → steady, predictable progress.
- **Priority 0** = values and long-term goals; review ~monthly, because if these are wrong every downstream priority/decision is wrong. The check-in keeps life intentional, improves motivation, and permits changing your mind (a hustle life → a relaxed life flips what counts as important — self-care may become the focus quadrant, study secondary).

**Technique:**
1. (Monthly) Priority 0: review values/long-term goals; confirm direction; adjust if changed.
2. (Daily, end of day) Capture tasks throughout the day in a simple list (Google Notes, Asana, Trello, Amazing Marvin, or a notebook — no need to organise in the moment).
3. Prioritise the list via the Eisenhower matrix; pick P1 (and P2/P3).
4. Build tomorrow's schedule: fixed commitments first → P1 (protected) → P2/P3 → everything else.
5. During the day, protect P1; rearrange/drop the rest as needed.

**Common mistakes:** getting so consumed by tasks you forget why (skip Priority 0); overscheduling — better to underschedule.

**Tips:** flexible, personal and student-friendly ("never a situation where it has not worked"); worksheets are provided (learn the theory first). Justin's tools: Amazing Marvin for tasks + Google Calendar for scheduling (Crippling Anxiety Clinic).

## Eisenhower Matrix & Task Batching
A prioritisation tool splitting tasks by urgency × importance into four quadrants, used daily to decide what to focus on, schedule, batch/delay, or delete. **Importance = significant consequence if you do NOT do it.**
- **Urgent + Important = FOCUS** (do now; e.g. study for an imminent test, apply to university before the deadline).
- **Urgent + Not important = DELEGATE** traditionally; for students with no one to delegate to, **BATCH and DELAY** to the last moment, then crank through them in one flow block. Delay because doing them now steals time from important work.
- **Not urgent + Not important = DELETE** freely and rigorously; if it truly matters it will find its way back onto the list. Double-check it isn't secretly important/urgent.
- **Important + Not urgent = SCHEDULE** (the "golden zone"); put calendar time in first. These make the biggest life difference (e.g. improving study efficiency — payoff maybe 6 months out, but the consequence of neglect is life-changing). Unscheduled important tasks become urgent → stress and constant fire-fighting.
- Aim to spend most time in Schedule, then Focus; it is impossible to live entirely in Schedule.
- **GTD 2-minute rule** (distinct from PEER's reward 2-minute rule): if an urgent-not-important task takes <2 minutes, do it immediately while reviewing the list — managing it costs more than doing it; batch anything longer. Getting Things Done is the source of the task-list + 2-minute ideas, but is not recommended wholesale for students — just these borrowed bits.

**Technique:** keep a running task list → end of day sort each task into the four quadrants → Focus: protect in schedule; Schedule: book calendar time first; Delegate: do <2-min tasks now, batch+delay the rest; Delete: remove → feed the result into the Priority 0+1 schedule.

**Clinic clarifications (Clinic 21):** an empty "A" (urgent+important) corner is fine (e.g. semester hasn't started) — don't manufacture urgent tasks to fill it; habits need not be listed (they are automatic by design); you don't have to force-move items from B to A.

**Caution:** the matrix alone is not enough — even students who have done whole prioritisation workshops still fall into urgency trapping.

## Task Management Over Scheduling
Prioritised task management beats explicit scheduling: **prioritisation without scheduling is far better than scheduling without prioritisation.** Scheduling is overhyped — "the spaced repetition of time management"; its ceiling is set by your task prioritisation/management. (Clinics 14, 16, 18)
- Justin runs ~90% task management / 10% time management, except mad-crunch weeks. Busy professionals juggling many commitments: time management plays a real role, but ~60% of the time task management is still the bigger limiter. Students (study + part-time job + social): almost always task management or base encoding speed (Clinics 16, 18).
- Most student "time problems" are task-management or slow-encoding problems, not scheduling problems. Perfect scheduling can still execute the wrong things in the wrong order/quality; good prioritisation + semi-structured moderate scheduling gets everything important done.
- **Priority to-do lists instead of a fixed calendar are fine** — better when family/life makes schedules volatile. A near-empty calendar can coexist with high output and free time (Clinic 14).
- Time spent scheduling depends on fluency: Justin ~2 minutes/day for prioritisation + scheduling combined; beginners need longer. Don't budget a fixed "scheduling time" — spend what fluency requires; it shrinks with skill (Clinic 16).
- Neurotic "what if I miss X" worries: experiment 2–3 times with COPEs — the what-ifs usually prove irrelevant ([learning-science](learning-science.md)).

**Tools:** the tool matters less than the principles. Justin uses **Amazing Marvin** (tasks, Eisenhower baked in) + **Google Calendar** (Priority 0+1 / attention management). A diary can bundle tracking, reflection, prioritising and next-day planning in one step (Clinic 13). **Notion is not recommended for studying or scheduling** — "worst of both worlds": the rigidity of time management plus the complexity/upkeep of task management; elaborate app setup feels productive but isn't ("productivity is getting stuff done, not writing about getting stuff done") (Clinic 14). Avoid overrated setup-heavy systems that cost more time than they free ([course-guide](course-guide.md)).

**Tip:** struggling with time management? Counter-intuitively think LESS about scheduling, MORE about task management; test it (Clinic 18).

## Scheduling Mistakes
Three mistakes make most schedules fail within a week or two:
- **Not scheduling at all** = winging it ("today maybe this, revise when I feel up to it") — no structure, no control over time.
- **Scheduling too vaguely** ("1–3 pm: biology") gives no idea what to actually do in the moment → decision delays at every transition → distraction and procrastination during the delay → the whole day derails.
- **Overpacking the day** leaves no room for error: one missed 3 pm slot and it's tempting to abandon the whole day ("schedule's screwed, may as well give up"). Brittle, not flexible.

Vague + overpacked together is the perfect failure recipe. **A consistent schedule you actually complete every day beats an ambitious one you fully achieve only on your best day and half-fail 9 days out of 10.**

**Tip:** beginners should break blocks down until they know exactly what they're doing at each moment, and leave buffers between tasks — these basics are the foundation for advanced time management.

## Scheduling Guidelines & Tips
The practical mechanics of a sustainable schedule — the basics Justin still uses daily after ~10 years.
- **Tool:** diary (calming pen-and-paper feel) vs app (portable, shuffle/reorganise the day on the fly). Best tool = the one you'll use consistently day in, day out. Schedule social/fun time deliberately — busy people who don't schedule it end up with none.
- **Specificity kills delays:** not "biology 2 pm" but "2:00 go to library; 2:10 books open, seated; 2:15 set study goals for the next hour; 2:15–3:00 focused study using [technique]". The setup time exists anyway; a clear next action removes the "what do I do now?" gap where procrastination breeds. Pair each block with a written intention ([focus-attention](focus-attention.md)).
- **Study goals per hour, not per 3-hour block:** hour 1 = A, B, C; hour 2 = D, E; hour 3 = revise A–E. If you struggle to focus, set 30-minute goals. Beginners should over-specify; the need fades with experience. Set goals from the session's *function* (pre-study / encoding / revision), not superficial completion ("finish the mind map") — [course-guide](course-guide.md), [aiming](aiming.md).
- **Overestimate task duration by 25%** (1 h → 1 h 15; 2 h → 2 h 30).
- **Underfill the day by ~25%:** ≈15-minute buffer between every task + 30–60 minutes completely unscheduled at day's end. If that doesn't fit, remove items and prioritise harder. Leftover free time can absorb an extra task with nothing lost (top priorities are already done).
- **Calibrate with experience:** Justin now overestimates by ~10%, needs only 10–15 min buffers, and schedules relaxation explicitly instead of underfilling. Start conservative.
- **Schedule the planning itself:** use the end-of-day 30–60 free minutes as dedicated prioritisation/planning time so the system never collapses for lack of it.

Following this checklist yields a ~90% good schedule and is the prerequisite for the advanced system.

**Technique:** prioritise (night before / start of week) → put top 2–3 items in first → break every block into specific actions and hourly (or 30-min) study goals → inflate each estimate 25%, insert 15-min buffers, leave 30–60 min free at day's end → use that free block to plan the next day → repeat.

## Underscheduling & Followable Schedules
A schedule you can't follow was scheduled wrong — a schedule is not a program that executes itself. Build loose, achievable schedules that account for real-life friction, and tighten only as execution skill grows. (Clinic 14)
- **Always underschedule.** Justin's calendar is mostly empty; fully packed days happen 1–3 times a year, even though high-priority work fills his days.
- **Impossible-itinerary analogy:** a 30-minute layover that ignores baggage and terminal transfer is an impossible plan, not a passenger failure. Schedules likewise ignore travel, refocusing time, distraction losses, breaks and task overrun — none of which ever vanish in real life.
- Loss of motivation to schedule is usually a **consequence** of repeatedly failing an unfollowable schedule (willpower exhaustion), not the root problem — fix followability and motivation returns.
- Less on the schedule forces better prioritisation, gives basic daily structure, and becomes motivating because you actually complete it; add more as skill increases.
- **Realism standard:** if being late to any scheduled item cost you your job/university place, you'd schedule very differently.

**Technique:** make the schedule achievable at your CURRENT skill level → budget for transitions, refocus, breaks, distractions and overrun → follow it (completion builds motivation and prioritisation skill) → gradually add items as execution improves.

**Common mistakes:** packing the day tight, failing, then blaming yourself or "motivation"; trying to fix scheduling demotivation with motivation techniques instead of fixing the schedule.

**Tip:** schedule free time/leisure as a rule of thumb — it adds structure and protects that time. If you can't stick to a schedule, it is a scheduling/prioritisation technique problem, not a character flaw — give it a go, collect failures, get feedback (Clinic 14).

## Google Calendar Setup (Rapid Workflow)
A starter workflow for putting the principles into a digital calendar (Google Calendar is used by the mentors; any calendar works if the principles are applied).
1. Enter **non-negotiable events first**: classes, training, family commitments — plus travel time blocks. This reveals how much (or little) time you actually have.
2. Schedule **important-but-not-urgent tasks next** (e.g. a 30-minute iCanStudy block), before worrying about urgent tasks. Be realistic — skip a slot you know you'll be too tired for.
3. Use **recurring events** for regular blocks like travel so setup isn't arduous.
4. Fit **urgent tasks** into remaining slots; when time is short, consciously prioritise the most important AND urgent task — you cannot do everything.
5. **Overestimate duration** (1 hour of homework → block 1.5 hours).
6. Give every block a **specific, measurable intention**: "write two paragraphs of ~250 words each" rather than "work on English essay" — you'll know whether you hit it, which curbs procrastination.
7. Use **week view** for the bigger picture.

**Common mistakes:** blocking time without an intention (two scheduled hours can still be wasted); underestimating durations and overcommitting; trying to schedule everything ("we're not robots"). The tool matters less than the principles: importance-first, realistic estimation, explicit intentions.

## Study Scheduling (Spacing Into the Diary)
A simple expanding-gap spaced-repetition schedule locked into a diary with fixed weekly/monthly blocks and accountability. This is the **WHEN**; the spacing science and revision techniques (the WHAT) live in [revision](revision.md) and [learning-science](learning-science.md).
1. First revision within **1–2 days** of learning.
2. Second revision **~7–10 days** after the first.
3. Third revision **~3–4 weeks** after the second.
4. In a diary/app, block a **half-day at each week's end** for weekly revision, and a **day/half-day/couple days at each month's end** for monthly revision; make them repeating.
5. Schedule a consistent **pre-study** slot too.

**Key principles:**
- Not exact science — approximate timing is fine; the principle matters more than precision.
- The MOST important part is **scheduling it into your day and keeping accountable**, not the exact intervals.
- Flaws: excessive for some subjects, insufficient for others → advanced techniques later (Justin used this for his first 2–3 years of university).
- Force of habit: students abandon advanced scheduling when school starts; accountability (diary, friends) overcomes this.
- Pre-study: 1 h saves ~10 h later; never de-prioritise it ([aiming](aiming.md)).
- Spacing follows encoding quality and time of day: better encoding → wider gaps; late-day exposure → scaffold immediately, consolidate next morning, pre-study predictable material. On a busy schedule, retrieval value depends overwhelmingly on initial encoding quality. Details in [revision](revision.md) and [learning-science](learning-science.md) (Clinic 28).

**Common mistakes:** knowing the tips but not using them; relying on exact intervals instead of accountability; dropping pre-study when busy. See the full spacing ladder (pre-study → class → same-day → midweek → weekly → monthly → pre-test) and the fixed weekly WPW teaching day in [revision](revision.md).

## Protecting Time
Treat scheduled time like a baby you must keep safe: take concrete actions that protect the block from foreseeable risks, rather than hoping intention and willpower form an "ethereal barrier". **A plan only counts when executed** — a beautiful calendar screenshot means nothing.
- **Baby analogy:** before a 40-minute grocery run you'd hire a babysitter, use a cot/alarm, tell a neighbor — or not go. You'd never leave the baby mid-room and hope. Time is equally precious yet rarely protected.
- **High-risk slots cannot be protected:** don't schedule important, self-volition tasks where you have never demonstrated execution (e.g. early mornings if you're not a morning person). That stakes the plan on first creating an entire new habit ("I won't smoke on days I ride my bike — except I don't ride a bike"). Build the waking-up habit first, consistently; only then load important tasks into that time (gradual acclimatisation: [habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md)).
- Straight after work/school/uni (especially after a long commute) is **low-energy, not low-time** — a risky slot for important work. Put meaningless tasks (e.g. email) in risky slots.
- Give the most important things the **safest, least-disturbable slots** (e.g. immediately after dinner).
- If a usable slot carries a known risk, add **safety rails**: a 5-minute pre-block energizer for post-work fatigue (short walk, 30 press-ups, favorite smoothie — easy, low-willpower); phone do-not-disturb (possibly automated); telling others you need focus time; a dedicated space conditioned as a focus area.
- Document the specific protective actions. "I'll try my best at that time" is an intention and means nothing without real-world actions.

**Technique:**
1. For each scheduled important block, ask: is this slot high-risk for me (energy, disturbance, track record)?
2. Fundamentally high-risk → move the important task to a safe slot; leave only low-stakes tasks there.
3. Workable-but-risky → list the specific risks that could derail it.
4. For each risk, implement a concrete protection (energizer, DND automation, notifying people, focus space).
5. Write these actions into the plan and execute; judge the plan by lived execution, not by how it looks.

**Common mistakes:** scheduling important tasks where you've never executed anything important; expecting the schedule itself to create a brand-new habit; sending calendar screenshots for approval instead of reporting execution; protecting time with intentions only.

**Cross-refs:** reserve willpower for system-building, not daily motivation ([habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md)); pre-program scripted actions for limiting habits and use social accountability for wake-time goals ([habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md)); schedule energy-intensive tasks only at high-energy times (V-ABC, [habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md)).

## Sustainable Scheduling & Life Balance
The **#1 time-management guideline is SUSTAINABLE** — you must be able to do it every day for the rest of your life. Judge a routine by whether you can sustain it consistently for years, not by how impressive it looks. (Crippling Anxiety Clinic)
- If you can't do it on your worst day (holiday / bad day / tired / sick), don't build it on your best day either. **Consistency is the #1 influencer** of whether Justin adopts any behaviour.
- **Schedule-switching productivity equalises:** the first month of a new early/late schedule feels productive, but the day still has the same hours and sleep, so the benefit fades. Extreme early waking (e.g. 2am) can be highly focused but is socially non-viable. There is no sustainably dialled-in magic schedule — find what works, stick with it until it stops, then change (Clinic 25; early-rising myths: [sleep-rest](sleep-rest.md)).
- **Toxic productivity:** reject being productive for its own sake. Productivity should serve a holistic life; razor-edge productivity is a **means to do all of life, not the end**. Fill freed-up time with life experiences, not just more work. Justin's real routine is surprisingly average — but sustained consistently for 6–7 years (emotional drivers: [mindset-emotion](mindset-emotion.md)).

Routine-building mechanics (ball-rolling actions, gradual wake-time acclimatisation, want vs need vs body) live in [habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md); rest and sleep protection in [sleep-rest](sleep-rest.md).

**Common mistakes:** rigid, ambitious routines you can't sustain on bad days; chasing extreme schedules for a temporary boost; centering all of life on the career path. See the hustle myth (Priority Myths) and balance across weeks (Prioritising).

## Environmental Optimisation & Friction
Behaviour is a product of environment and habits, not of the behaviour itself; changing behaviour by trying harder at the moment of behaviour is inconsistent and unsustainable. **Cookie jar effect:** jar on the counter → you eat cookies daily on willpower; jar gone → you may not think of cookies for months. Make bad behaviours hard to access and good behaviours easy; **remove triggers instead of resisting them.**
- Modern stimuli are engineered (billion-dollar attention-hacking). Being easily distracted means you're a normal human in an environment far beyond the dopamine hits the cave brain adapted to — effort must go into the environment, not into resisting at the behaviour point.
- Highly disciplined people are equally distractible neurologically; they engineer environments where distractions cannot enter, while the distracted rely on willpower (works briefly, never long-term).
- **Use laziness as a weapon:** make the bad behaviour so effortful to access you can't be bothered. Invest ALL willpower into the environment, not as a "little helping hand" alongside resisting.
- **Friction ladder for the phone:** on desk → do-not-disturb (often not enough) → buried under clothes in the wardrobe / kitchen safe (timer-activated lock) / safe with someone else's password. DND + removing a desktop icon alone signals you're still relying on willpower (Clinic 18).
- Keep a **trigger sheet** beside you (same tool as the BEDS-M distractions cheat sheet): log every trigger, then engineer it out. Identify triggers (news/politics, waking, intrusive thoughts) and run experiments (e.g. restrict news access) in the next COPEs cycle (Clinic 25; [learning-science](learning-science.md)).
- **Optimise other people's environments too:** their intrusions are their habits, not malice. A "Studying — please do not disturb" door sign breaks parents' walk-in habit; a coloured post-it system signals focus time in shared offices; online, use built-in DND. For family interruptions, stage it: explicit communication → visible cue → white-noise earphones → lock/relocate ([focus-attention](focus-attention.md), Clinic 13).

**Clinic upgrades (Clinic 29):**
- A new living space = blank associations → build habits correctly from day one, IF you know your limiting habits (via COBES).
- **Silo/isolate:** keep distractions out of the work space; a clear boundary between sleep/play and study/work (curtain or divider in a studio apartment).
- **Friction barriers:** Justin's gaming PC is unplugged with the monitor on his MacBook; playing requires getting up and swapping the HDMI (~30 seconds but high effort vs alt-tab) → games once a week/fortnight/month. If 30 seconds isn't enough, add more.
- **"Maximum possible goal"** (opposite of a minimum viable goal): build the biggest possible barrier to procrastinating on things you shouldn't do.
- **Access is preserved, not banned:** on a planned day off he sets the PC up and plays freely — "it's not controlling me anymore; I am in control of it."
- **Lighting presets** condition behaviour (chilling / deep work / recording); **work tools always ready** (tripod, iPad, reMarkable on the desk → zero setup friction).

**Technique:**
1. List every distraction/trigger (use the trigger log to catch hidden ones).
2. Remove or add friction: phone out of the room; website/app blockers with accountability (a friend notified + $100 if you change settings); uninstall games; deactivate social accounts; mute Discord channels; remove desktop icons.
3. **Router-level blocking:** log into router admin → enable parental controls → block sites and search-string keywords per device → set a long unmemorable admin password, write it on paper, give it to someone else so you can't undo it.
4. **Defang recommendation algorithms:** YouTube incognito/logged-out (generic homepage); or search the video on Google first and use direct links so you never land on the homepage, comments or sidebar.
5. Plant cues for good behaviours; set up others' environments; condition modes; keep work tools instantly ready.
6. Schedule legitimate access (days off); document all optimisations in your plan; iterate via COBES as you discover new tendencies.

**Common mistakes:** doing the bare minimum (phone on DND behind you) and concluding environment design "doesn't work"; changing the behaviour directly while leaving the environment intact; making excuses instead of finding solutions; removing access entirely instead of friction + scheduled access; underestimating your own procrastination skill (you WILL fetch a reachable phone).

**Examples:** Olivia moved a desk into the garage and studied there because there was nothing else to do; students locked phones in safes with a parent holding the password.

**Cross-refs:** the behaviour-change frameworks that share this lever (V-ABC antecedents, BEDS-M's E, PEER Prep) live in [habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md); focus zones and the distractions cheat-sheet maintenance loop, and "prevent the action, not the thought", in [focus-attention](focus-attention.md).

## OFF-Rest Timing (Optimised For Focus)
A personalised work/rest method that times work and rest to keep focus optimal across the whole day, replacing the fixed Pomodoro cycle by resting when focus goes **dull** (not **tired**) and choosing rest type by how fresh you are.
- **Pomodoro (25 work / 5 rest)** pros: clock awareness boosts focus; focus naturally decays after ~20 min; structured breaks prevent overtime. Cons: fixed timing ignores variability; breaks deep flow; forces work when tired (training the brain to be inefficient); ignores break quality (a 5-min argument isn't restorative).
- **Optimise across ALL cycles in the day, not one:** being 5–10% less productive in cycle 1 is worth it if cycle 4 is 50% more productive; a 3-hour blast that leaves you dead ruins the day.
- **Rest while reserve is high** (cup analogy: holding even an empty cup for 3 hours wrecks your arm; a full cup for 3 minutes with 30-second rests lets you last 6–7 hours). The beginner precursor — frequent short breaks (work ~20–30 min, rest 5–10 min while fresh, always timed) — lives in [sleep-rest](sleep-rest.md).
- **Knife-edge focus:** rest when focus goes DULL (understanding takes longer, more effort to focus, noticeably slowing), not when TIRED (eyes hurt, headache, dehydrated, drained). Waiting until tired means a long recharge and a low-efficiency "piddly" state where you feel busy but produce little.
- Work capacity shrinks through the day (e.g. 45→30→20 min); **rest = work time ÷ 3** (45→15, 30→10, 20→~6.7 min).
- **Rest type depends on freshness:** Fresh → PRODUCTIVE BREAK (stay in semi-flow): topical (relaxed study-related activity + environment change) or miscellaneous (calm chores). Tired (usually end of day) → ACTIVE RELAXATION (focus training ~5 min) to recharge fast; passive relaxation (scrolling) is slow and only fine without a time limit ([focus-attention](focus-attention.md)).
- **Environment/eyes:** change environment to look at things far away; eye fatigue strongly drives sleepiness, so resting eyes restores energy; greenery/trees are especially restorative (Cave-Theory link, [learning-science](learning-science.md)).
- Only applies when you have an extended block (≥~1.5 hours) fitting multiple cycles; for a single short block (e.g. 45 min before dinner) just do your best.
- Result: ~5–6 hours of deep focus/day vs the usual 2–3.

**Technique:**
1. Start a timer when you begin work; monitor focus.
2. When focus goes dull, stop the timer; note elapsed work time.
3. Rest = work time ÷ 3; time the rest too.
4. Ask "how fresh am I?": fresh → productive break (topical or miscellaneous); tired → active relaxation (focus training).
5. Restart the work timer; repeat, with cycles naturally shortening through the day.

**Tips:** topical break = a relaxed stroll with a notebook, mentally mind-mapping what you studied and jotting anything you can't recall → you return refreshed with a focused revision list (casual retrieval practice). Miscellaneous break = calm, methodical chores or non-stressful delegate-quadrant tasks; avoid anything that stresses you. Beginners time everything meticulously; experienced users eyeball work/rest lengths. **No prerequisite:** you can adopt OFF-rest directly without first mastering Pomodoro (Clinic 19). **Tired ≠ drowsy** and gaming-break cautions: see [sleep-rest](sleep-rest.md) (Clinic 28). Under OFF-rest you should only reach "very tired" at the end of the day; if it happens after cycle 1, you rested too late.

## Decisional Delays
The untracked time spent deciding what to do next between activities — typically 5–10 minutes per transition, stacking to **30 minutes–2 hours (sometimes 3) per day**.
- Invisible, because we notice time passing while doing an activity but not while deliberating.
- The **"evil zone"** / dark alleyway of time management: where procrastination, laziness and "maybe I'll do it tomorrow" live — sprint through it.
- Reclaiming one hour is a whole extra activity, or a 20-minutes-each-way commute.
- Even with a good schedule: finishing 15 minutes early → "what now?" → an hour of YouTube → late for or skipping the next activity; finishing 10 minutes late → "do I have time? I need a break" → another delay.
- First prevention is better plans, schedules and task management, so you know what comes next.
- **Awareness is the prerequisite for removal**, and it is also the core skill attention management needs.

**Technique:**
1. At the end of each day, review your schedule and mark every transition where you stopped and wondered "what should I do next / should I do this or that?"
2. Log it in a diary. Expect to spot nothing for the first couple of days.
3. Keep looking; awareness arrives and you'll catch yourself mid-delay ("I'm in a decisional delay right now").
4. Measure how long it actually takes.
5. When you notice one, skip the deliberation and flow straight into the next task without hesitation.

**Common mistakes:** assuming a full schedule means no wasted time; trying to remove delays before you can detect them. Across a few hundred observed cases (students and professionals), 30 minutes to 2 hours per day is typical.

## Attention Management
Replace clock-based scheduling with continuous awareness of where your attention is, flowing from one intentional activity to the next with no gap, anchored only by a few fixed daily checkpoints. This is the advanced endpoint of the time-management progression.
- You manage your **perception** of time, which is wildly inaccurate and dependent on the activity and your mood. Without clocks you'd still know the milestones (school starts/ends, dinner, bedtime); between milestones the clock value is near zero — you only need to know you're not late.
- **Manage attention, not activities:** an activity done without focus produces nothing.
- **Attention gaps are free time** (commuting, walking to tennis, showering, settling in). Filling 20–30 minutes of an hour-long commute creates time at zero cost.
- Combine with Priority 0+1: finish P1, flow to P2, and so on — it doesn't matter if you finish 15 minutes early or late. This **removes decisional delays entirely**, because clock time no longer governs transitions. Only the fixed checkpoints must be obeyed.
- It is provably the fastest possible day: you already work at near-maximum quality speed and there is no waste between activities. A "perfect" time-managed schedule would, on reflection, look exactly like attention management; over- and under-scheduling become meaningless.
- **Intentionality includes rest:** "my attention is on relaxing" is a valid, deliberate allocation. Don't soak up every minute with study.
- Enables 10–12 hour flow states when needed — but take breaks when you lose your edge; capability is not obligation.
- Requires high self-awareness; work through the worksheet stages and don't rush them. **Not a beginner technique** — it needs decisional-delay awareness first.

**Technique:**
1. List only the day's hard checkpoints.
2. For every remaining block ask "where is my attention?" and mark genuine attention gaps.
3. Fill gaps with due flashcards, a brief Aim step, pre-study, or a mental mind map (interleaving).
4. Set priorities via Priority 0+1 and work them in order, flowing immediately from one to the next.
5. If a lower priority fits a short gap exactly (e.g. 30 minutes), do it there, then return to priority 1.
6. Continuously ask "is my attention where I intend it to be?" If not (e.g. Facebook), move it deliberately.
7. Take breaks using OFF-rest timing (10–15 minutes), then return attention to the task.
8. At day's end, confirm every minute was spent as intended — including relaxation, friends, family, movies or YouTube.

**Example day:** 7:30–8 morning routine; 8–9 commute (gap → flashcards or pre-study); 9–3 school (lunch with friends is intentional rest); 3–3:30 travel to tennis (gap → mental mind map of the day's learning); 3:30–4:30 tennis; 4:30–5:30 travel home (40 min intentional relax, 20 min Aim/Shoot planning); 5:30–6:30 settle and shower (mental mind map in the shower); 6:30–7 dinner; 7–9 homework; 9–10 buffer; 10–11 relax; 11+ sleep. Three gaps found ≈ one extra hour of study, meaning one less hour later in the day.

## Microlearning & Attention Gaps
Using small, otherwise-wasted windows (commutes, waiting, breaks) for learning **"creates time out of thin air."** Match the activity's required immersion to what the pocket actually allows, and prepare resources during focused blocks so micro-sessions are ready to run.
- **Match task to achievable engagement:** a pocket where you must stay alert (waiting for a bus with no arrival signal) cannot support deep immersion; a captive hour (on the bus) can.
- Low-order retrieval (flashcards) suits interruptible, low-immersion pockets (start/stop friendly). Mid-to-higher-order interleaving suits longer captive windows — plain flashcards there underuse immersion and may bore you to sleep; consult the interleaving table ([revision](revision.md)).
- Light environmental optimisation (headphones + white noise) is often enough to enable focus in a pocket.
- Skill level gates feasibility: many techniques are impossible until your cognitive level develops; practice makes the Shoot step quicker to enter in small windows.
- **Rest is legitimate:** a commute may be better spent breathing/relaxing. Microlearning is about having the option available, not filling every moment.
- **Mechanism:** a hard deadline creates urgency → the brain treats the material as more relevant → focus, connections and retention rise (the same reason cramming feels focused). In 5–15 minutes you can't reach flow or pull out many resources; flashcards fit the window perfectly. **Batching destroys the mechanism** (a six-hour flashcard day has no urgency or deadline).
- Run as a **parallel stream whenever due**, not batched — 50–100 cards a day without freeing up any extra time.

**Technique:**
1. Identify predictable windows (e.g. eight or nine 5-min pockets ≈ 40 min) plus any focused block (e.g. 20 min).
2. Classify each window by achievable immersion (interruptible vs captive) and constraints (motion sickness, noise).
3. Assign low-order retrieval to interruptible pockets; mid/higher-order interleaving to captive windows.
4. Use focused blocks to PREPARE resources for the micro-sessions (make flashcards, set up interleaving materials) rather than only studying conventionally.
5. If reading causes motion sickness, choose cue-based techniques where most time is spent thinking, not reading.

**Common mistakes:** trying to immerse deeply where you must monitor for an event; doing only low-order flashcards in a long captive window; spending a scarce focused block on conventional study while leaving daily pockets unused; assuming every moment must be productive.

**Tips:** for a time-poor competitive athlete with one weekend day, spend 3–4 of 6 Saturday hours creating micro-learning resources for the week and 2–3 hours on focused deep big-picture encoding (Clinic 34). Using a 20-min focused block to prepare interleaving resources makes the rest of the day's 40 min of pockets usable — a positional decision that changes future capacity (Clinic 34; [decision-making](decision-making.md)).

**Cross-refs:** digital flashcards and active flashcard management ([memorisation](memorisation.md)); the full Prime–Map–Explore–Dive–Consolidate professional microlearning system ([professional-learning](professional-learning.md)); attention management fills gaps with these activities.

## Batch & Bulk Studying
Studying a whole topic (or a whole semester's material) in a concentrated batch up front, then maintaining it through natural use and interleaved retrieval, instead of spreading study evenly across every class/week.
- Justin's master's: 1–2 weeks at the start of each semester to finish ALL studying for the semester; afterwards only assignments remain, and the material is naturally interleaved through constant use — spacing worries largely disappear.
- He batches by paper: 3–4 days on one paper, finish it, then the next — staying in one mode beats daily subject-switching (also helps cognitive-load fatigue and the second-topic quality drop; [sleep-rest](sleep-rest.md)).
- Highly proficient high-school students finish the entire year's curriculum via self-study in term 1–2, with very good grades.
- Batch studying is what makes heavy extracurricular loads possible. University is "professional life 0.5", not "high school 2.0".
- Batch size must still respect technique capacity and overload limits ([learning-science](learning-science.md)).

**Tips:** for high-schoolers with many extracurriculars, study whole topics in one go (a month of school content per set) rather than class-by-class — the techniques are not "overkill", the scope was too small (Clinic 28). Insert real rest before switching domains (Clinic 28).

**Cross-refs:** "cram early" ("if your test is in August, cram it in February") and the multipass system ([revision](revision.md)); front-loading the term (Intentional Living below); exam meso planning.

## Intentional Living & Front-Loading
Efficiency frees time — decide intentionally what to do with it instead of drifting into procrastination or urgency-driven "rat race" living. (Clinic 16)
- If you're still getting the results you want and have free time, **relaxing is legitimate** — you earned it ("it's called freedom").
- **Intentional living:** choose what to spend time on (rest, extra papers/certifications, hobbies) rather than living by urgent need; your chained moments ARE your life.
- **Front-loading** is one option: complete all study at the start of a term, then be done (Justin finished his entire master's semester — all learning + assignments — in the first 4–6 weeks, then forgot about it; high distinctions, exemplar work). Mechanics: Batch & Bulk Studying above and [revision](revision.md) (cram early).
- If procrastination persists even when work is needed, that's a habit problem: use the course's habit-building tools (marginal gains, distraction sheet, BEDS-M, PEER) — the people who need them most ignore them ("tried once, didn't work" → do it again) ([habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md)).

**Common mistakes:** drifting into procrastination with freed time because no intentional choice was made; ignoring habit techniques while chasing more "learning" content; selectively learning (you won't know what you needed until the issue bites — [course-guide](course-guide.md)).

**Tip:** ask "what do I actually want to be doing?" — then either enjoy the freedom deliberately or front-load the term (Clinic 16).

## Intentional Breaks, Rest & Leisure
Breaks are strategically necessary and should be **intentional decisions**, not guilt-driven avoidance or unintentional procrastination. Efficiency exists to earn longer, earlier breaks. (Clinic 19)
- Taking a break may be the single most productive thing you can do toward your goal.
- **Intentional vs unintentional:** deciding to take the day off (phone off, present, hike/beach/show) is rest; intending to work but watching 4 hours of YouTube is procrastination (makes you more tired, worse mental health).
- **Schedule leisure** into the calendar (e.g. 2 hours of gaming at day's end) to stave off daytime urges and defer them guilt-free.
- **Rest is rest:** do NOT run productivity techniques while relaxing (Clinic 15).
- Don't defer happiness/mental health to "after the goal" — you might never arrive; enjoy the journey. Mental health is literally your experience of life.
- On a low-energy day, intentionally take it off and hit harder the next day. The energy-first recovery rule (choose the activity that maximises energy + engagement; walk reset; distinguish lost-edge from truly-low-energy) lives in [sleep-rest](sleep-rest.md) (Clinic 34).

**Tips:** reframe "when should you NOT take breaks" rather than "when should you" (Clinic 19). Timed naps (~20–25 min) and 30-second eye-closed exam resets: [sleep-rest](sleep-rest.md), [exam-technique](exam-technique.md) (Clinics 13, 14).

**Common mistakes:** treating chronic daytime sleepiness as normal and napping around it instead of fixing sleep; refusing micro-breaks in exams "because there's no time"; guilt over intentional rest.

## Diagnosing Overload & Overrun
When time feels short, diagnose before grinding harder. Most "time problems" are efficiency, prioritisation or encoding problems. (Clinic 10)
- **The deadly spiral:** feeling overwhelmed by assessments is usually not a time shortage but a **learning-efficiency shortage** — low learning-per-hour forces more hours, hitting the hard time ceiling and leaving no freedom to improve the method. Analogy: driving so fast you can't check the GPS → running late → driving faster → even less ability to check direction. The counter-intuitive fix is to **slow down** to regain the freedom to verify direction.
- Everyone gets roughly the same assessments and the same hours; the difference is how long tasks take you. **Two escape routes:** better prioritisation (consequence-of-failure) and better learning efficiency (the course techniques) — which reinforce each other. Protect course-learning time first even when behind; it is the GPS check that makes all other speed useful.
- **Schedule overrun** (one subject eats the whole block, others dropped): ask two questions in order — (1) *Is the studying effective?* (2) *Am I prioritising by importance?* If effective, the overrun is a temporary growth-phase cost — keep grinding effectiveness into efficiency. If not effective, get feedback and run COBEs. If effective but on the wrong things, apply consequence-of-failure prioritisation.
- **Effectiveness before speed:** early correct execution is slow and effortful; speed emerges with practice. Don't panic about slowness while the technique is working, and don't grind harder on an ineffective method.
- Distinguish a task/time-management problem from slow base encoding — the fix differs ([learning-science](learning-science.md), measuring efficiency).

**Common mistakes:** responding to overload by working faster/longer with the same ineffective method; dismissing the deadly-spiral lesson as irrelevant (assessment overload is exactly its real-world case).

**Cross-refs:** positional decisions and the overwhelm "death spiral" sacrifice mechanics ([decision-making](decision-making.md)); the efficiency equation and COBEs ([learning-science](learning-science.md)).

## Learning Time vs Session Time
Absolute time spent in a single session is the least meaningful measure of study value. **Judge time by learning returned across the whole timescale of the goal** — you want the outcome (usable knowledge/results), not the "drill bit". (Clinics 14, 15)
- **Drill-bit story:** nobody wants a drill bit; they want the hole → screw → picture → feeling. Likewise you want knowledge in your head to apply (or the exam result) — not "hours studied".
- 5 hours of pre-study that saves 10 hours over the next month (15 min/day, barely noticeable) is a net 5-hour win. Justin: 1.5 hours of setup saving 30–40 hours across two months, despite covering little content in that session.
- **"Taking too long" must be defined:** too long per session, or too long to reach the goal? Only the latter matters.
- Same logic for slow question-answering in Aim: don't count time per question; count learning happening — what would you do instead, and would it learn as much? If not, the "faster" alternative wastes time.

**Common mistakes:** judging pre-study (or any technique) by session length and cutting it because it "feels slow"; optimising per-question speed instead of learning yield.

**Tips:** high-quality learning in 5 hours beats shallow coverage in 2 — evaluate over the goal's entire timescale. Technique speed improves with practice anyway; early slowness is investment, not waste. See measuring learning efficiency ([learning-science](learning-science.md)).

## Time-Limited Studying: Thin & Wide
With very limited time, go **thin and super-wide** with perfect technique accuracy at each step, and layer multiple topics across sessions rather than completing one topic per session. (Clinic 27)
1. Keep the pre-study/frame within its ~20-min limit; don't go longer ([aiming](aiming.md)).
2. If you have e.g. 5 hours across Mon–Fri for 5 topics: hour 1 = pre-study/frame all 5; day 2 = add detail to all; day 3 = add more detail; etc.
3. Prioritise breadth and accuracy of each step over depth on one topic.
4. Reuse mandatory lecture time for learning (leave details out of pre-study); redirect saved time to interleaving previous material ([exam-technique](exam-technique.md), in-class technique).

**Key principles:** same total time, different distribution — leverages limited time while keeping correct technique. Going narrow/deep with limited time risks learning the wrong technique (a bigger waste).

**Tip:** batch-studying exception — advanced students covering 1–2 weeks in a weekend can reconsolidate/rechunk unexpected additions, even rebuilding a map from memory as interleaving (Clinic 26).

## Exam Scheduling: Meso Planning & Strategic Cramming
Plan exam preparation at a **"meso" level**: map how you'll cover top-priority areas across subjects relative to exam dates. When cramming, be strategic. (Crippling Anxiety Clinic)
- **Daily plan vs schedule:** if you can't keep track of much, make a daily plan of priorities (not necessarily time-bound). If your encoding is already good you have room for error and any approach works; if not, be strict and strategic.
- **Meso level:** map coverage of highest→lowest priority areas across subjects relative to exams; split into days/half-days/bi-daily as natural.
- **Cramming reality:** least-studied material won't get high-quality encoding or spacing/high-volume retrieval → you rely on short-term memory, so time it strategically. **Study the subject of your FIRST exam LAST** (Biology Monday, English Tuesday → study Biology last) so the final thing you study is the first exam.
- **Worst cramming mistake: treating all information as equal.** Curriculum learning objectives are legally tested → anything in the objectives WILL be tested. Prioritise fundamental objectives and frequently-recurring variations first, then details.
- **Mark yield:** written/short-answer questions score on a spectrum, not binary. Better 2–3 marks on EVERY question (pass) than 5/5 on one and 0/5 on another.
- **Multi-pass strategy (Summit):** understand all concepts across the board first → sweep ~30% of marks; then high-priority details/facts → another ~30–40% (≈70%); then fill remaining detail. Requires lower-level skills first ([revision](revision.md)).
- **Tools:** Amazing Marvin for tasks + Google Calendar for Priority 0+1 / attention management; pre-list practice papers/revision activities 1–2 months ahead so you know your Priority 1 at each point.
- A clear exam plan reduces exam anxiety by reassuring you that you've covered what matters.

**Technique:**
1. List subjects and exam dates; rank topics by priority (learning objectives, fundamentality, frequency, mark weight).
2. Map meso coverage highest→lowest priority across subjects, split into days/half-days.
3. If cramming: order so the first exam's subject is studied last; rely on short-term memory for it.
4. First pass: understand all main concepts (~30%). Second pass: high-priority details (~30–40% more). Later passes: fill remaining detail.
5. In exams, secure partial marks across all questions before perfecting any single one.

**Common mistakes:** treating all information as equal when cramming; studying the first exam's subject too early (no spacing possible → wasted); perfecting one 5-mark question while scoring 0 on others; attempting multi-pass without the lower-level skills.
