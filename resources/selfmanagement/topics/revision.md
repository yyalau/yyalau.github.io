# Revision, Reconstruction, WPW & Multipass
> Re-engaging already-learned material through active gap-seeking, reconstruction and spaced, varied retrieval — the final safety-net layer of the study system, plus strategic cramming and the multipass system for compressed timelines.

## Overview
No first-pass encoding is perfect. In the [bear-hunter-system](bear-hunter-system.md), Aim/Shoot/Skin plus flashcards and the modified method of loci ([memorisation](memorisation.md)) inevitably leave holes; revision is the extra Swiss-cheese layer that catches them, consolidates, and makes knowledge applicable to assessment and real-world problems. Done correctly it is skill-based and self-generated (not dependent on question banks), buys peace of mind upstream (you stop fussing over small details during encoding because revision will catch them), and makes you relatively immune to curveball questions. This note covers the revision strategy and its pitfalls, the relearn-vs-revise encoding check, the recall→reconstruction hierarchy, gap-seeking and re-Aiming, the spacing ladder and high-volume-retrieval sequencing, Whole-Part-Whole (WPW) reteaching with its execution rules and worked dissections, brain dumps, teaching, self-made questions and question banks as retrieval, when to stop, exam-season strategy, cramming and the multipass system. The underlying science (spacing, interleaving, retrieval, Bloom's, forgetting curve, Swiss cheese model) lives in [learning-science](learning-science.md); diary mechanics and exam meso-planning in [time-management](time-management.md); breaching questions, group-study roles and exam execution in [exam-technique](exam-technique.md).

## Revision Purpose: The Safety-Net Layer
Revision is re-engaging already-learned material to expose gaps, find weaknesses, consolidate, and make knowledge usable under assessment conditions.

- Misses are inevitable, not a personal failure — design for them. A robust revision strategy lets you relax during Aim/Shoot instead of anxiously over-covering detail.
- Revision must be **skill-based and self-generated**: you create the questions and find the gaps yourself, rather than hoping external past papers happen to surface your specific weaknesses.
- The goal is the **highest level of learning per unit of time**, not hours logged or questions completed.
- Correct revision makes curveballs stop being curveballs: dense logical connections plus fluid reconstruction mean novel applications feel as easy as familiar ones ([exam-technique](exam-technique.md)).
- Revision is sequenced last in the course because everything upstream (encoding, notes, time management) is being changed first; doing it earlier creates disarray.

## Revision Pitfalls
Four failure modes make revision slow, low-yield and blind-spot-prone:

1. **Repeating the same method.** Identical re-exposure finds nothing new; once you are reasonably confident, yield plateaus. Even recall of the same thing over and over is passive. The plateau creates **blind spots** — angles, approaches or applications you systematically never cover — which surface in exams as "curveballs".
2. **Mindless / passive revision.** Recognition instead of recall; doing questions "because they are there to be done" rather than because you suspect a gap. Judge a method by whether it actively finds gaps, not by volume completed.
3. **Past-paper dependence.** Past papers are luck-dependent (you must happen to draw a question on your weakness), access-dependent (scarce at university and professional level), they don't develop the gap-finding skill, and they create dependency. Their efficiency decays: a 3-question paper may yield 1 useful question; by the third 10-question paper maybe only 2 are useful. They are genuinely useful for recall + application and for late-stage stylistic calibration ([exam-technique](exam-technique.md)) — just not as the engine of revision.
4. **Passive left-to-right coverage.** Starting at the left of the syllabus and waiting for a gap to appear. You can feel complete at 96% while systematically missing examinable angles.

Related spiral: with weak encoding, spaced-repetition systems (Leitner/flashcards) generate a review backlog that grows faster than it can be cleared — after 2–3 weeks you owe several weeks of reviews on one Saturday, and the overload itself lowers retention further. The fix is better first-pass retention ([learning-science](learning-science.md)), not more revision hours; mechanics in [memorisation](memorisation.md).

**The revision backlog spiral & choosing losses** (Clinic 18): the same trap at whole-study scale — low initial encoding → high revision need → revision eats all available time → less time to encode → worse encoding. Anxiety about revision time is a symptom of this spiral. Break it by investing in encoding and accepting short-term losses: it is a lose-lose swap of anxieties, so choose the loss that hurts for a while (near-term tests may suffer) over the loss that compounds forever (every semester gets worse). Near a critical exam the choice is personal — smash revision to survive now, or tank the near-term result and permanently fix encoding; the instructor recommends the latter where possible. Interleaving is the one win-win: good interleaved retrieval re-encodes while revising. A large backlog is usually clearable with efficient technique (often easier than feared); the real danger is it growing faster than you consume it.

## Revision Time: BHS Buys the Hours
The advanced revision techniques are deliberately time-consuming but high-yield; the Bear Hunter System's efficiency gains are what make them affordable.

- BHS gives less study time and higher retention, freeing hours for slow, deep, gap-seeking revision. These techniques can expose holes within minutes that past papers would take hours — or never — to find.
- Higher retention makes old methods feel ineffective: past papers in particular start to feel "very meaningless" once BHS is working. Keep an open mind in both directions — methods you relied on may now be unnecessary; methods you dismissed may now be highly effective.
- Budget real blocks of free time rather than trying to speed these techniques up. Most students cannot afford application adjustment and breaching questions because they lack revision time; BHS efficiency is what buys it.

## Relearn vs Revise: The Encoding Check
Before revising, ask one question: **was it encoded properly?** "Covered it with your eyes" or "wrote notes on it" is not learning; poorly encoded material is effectively unlearned and may be fully gone a month later (Clinic 15).

- **If it was never encoded, relearn it from scratch — don't "revise" it.** Study it as if new: topic-wide pre-study, find the main chunks, build the backbone, then re-encode with correct technique. It is faster the second time because you partially studied it before; you can realistically cover ~4–10 lectures in a day and retention rises immediately (Clinic 23).
- **Re-encoding is itself powerful interleaved revision.** High-efficiency encoding hits information from many angles, so relearning properly doubles as retrieval practice; whatever gaps remain, interleaved retrieval sorts them. Good encoding directly produces application ability across problem types (Clinic 15).
- **Scale of failure dictates the fix** (Clinic 21): ~80% of a segment forgotten → it likely has no good structure/place to belong → redo the mind map from scratch with simpler/different chunks; isolated pockets → targeted re-encoding + interleaved retrieval. If relationships/chunks are already good, repeated forgetting is rare — then vary the retrieval form.
- **Don't re-run full BHS on well-encoded topics** — use interleaved retrieval instead. But for old topics studied without the technique (e.g. half a year of inferior notes), patching gap-by-gap takes LONGER than a clean restudy: low retention, narrow recall, minimal curveball protection. With moderate fluency, six months of material restudies in ~1–2 weeks (Clinic 12).
- Interleaving is the win-win: good interleaved retrieval re-encodes information, training initial encoding while revising (Clinic 18).

Common mistakes: "revising" material that was never encoded; trying to salvage old poor notes gap-by-gap; endlessly re-perfecting notes instead of moving to retrieval; feeling daunted by "six months of material" without accounting for fluency + prior exposure.

## Effective Revision: The Three Principles
Effective revision combines three elements; techniques incorporating all three are rare and demand an already-high skill level. Prerequisites: inquiry-based learning, order control, effective note-taking, and confidence in your technique ([aiming](aiming.md), [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md), [note-taking](note-taking.md)).

1. **Expanding-gap spaced repetition** (macro weekly/monthly scale). Each review gap grows — rule of thumb ~2–3× per step, more in later repetitions ("one week-ish, then one month-ish"; e.g. day 2 → gap 4–5 days → 8–12 days → 21–30 days). The early phase matters most: at least one or two revisions inside the first ~10 days. Approximate is fine; if a day is overloaded, insert a midweek review and lengthen the following gap. Full spacing science in [learning-science](learning-science.md).
2. **Interleaving — deliberate variation** (macro scale and day-to-day 10–20 minute scale). Practise repeatedly *with variations*: vary the mode of expression (mind maps, reading, visualisation, doodling, speaking aloud, teaching, drawing), not the source. Pure read/write practice for a read/write exam is weaker than read/write + speaking + teaching + drawing. BHS is inherently interleaving — following Aim/Shoot/Skin already varies expression. A mental mind map while walking with a notebook is a strong variation that doubles as a productive break: jot what you cannot recall and you return with a focused revision list (OFF-rest details in [time-management](time-management.md)). Vary at a sensible granularity, not frantically every minute. See [learning-science](learning-science.md).
3. **Active gap-seeking** — the single biggest time-saver: start at weaknesses and move outward (next sections). Techniques that find gaps faster are more efficient; passive techniques are slower *and* less effective.

Common mistakes: attempting these techniques before the encoding skills are in place; rushing the course and arriving here without practice; varying every single minute instead of meaningfully.

## The Test–Target–Teach Triad (Beginner Revision)
The fundamentals-level revision framework: **Test** (apply knowledge), **Target** (weakest areas first), **Teach** (explain to make sense) — in any order; even imperfectly executed it yields strong benefit. It matures into gap-seeking + WPW later in the course.

- **TEST** = apply knowledge, a different cognitive process (especially problem-solving). Past papers/questions force application; creating your own examiner-style questions also tests; apply at unfamiliar angles.
- **TARGET** = weaknesses first. We are biologically biased to revise strengths (admitting weakness feels destabilising); spending 6 of 10 hours on strengths is ineffective. Build the weakest until strong, then the next weakest. This removes the "hope they don't test that" niggle and gives confidence to be tested on anything.
- **TEACH** = explain to an imaginary student. Forces compare/contrast, ordering/flow, analogies, making sense. Listing facts is narrating/dictating, NOT teaching. Teaching is technical: group vs single vs imaginary student, topic vs concept vs subject, mental vs aloud, writing/drawing or not, order, frameworks, terminology — each changes retention.
- Recommended placement: teaching for the 1-week revision (broad coverage, finds gaps); practice papers and hard problem-solving for the 1-month revision.
- Revision technique = WHAT you do in sessions; study scheduling = WHEN ([time-management](time-management.md)). Baby steps: work with what's comfortable; the only way to fail is to do nothing.

## The Recall Hierarchy: Recognition, Cued Recall, True Recall
Revision quality is a spectrum; exams and real life almost never give you recognition conditions.

- **Recognition** — identifying a $1 coin on sight, or re-reading notes and feeling they make sense. No reconstructive process at all ("even a turtle could recognise"); below Bloom's taxonomy. The learning-per-minute is so low it is never a good option given alternatives; it does mostly harm.
- **Cued recall** — read notes, close them, recite. It is recall, but weaker: cue-response pathways fire in ~0.2 seconds, so a recent cue tricks you into thinking you know it. This produces the frustrating "I could do it in revision but not in the exam" feeling. Acceptable very early in revision or for particularly tricky material; push toward true recall.
- **True (full/active) recall** — retrieving without having recently seen the material. Guideline: **~48 hours since last laying eyes on it**. Start every revision session with everything closed — recall first, see what you can find, only then check and correct. Flashcards naturally deliver true recall because you are on the move and haven't seen your notes.
- **Reconstruction** — above even true recall (next section). Active recall is the MINIMUM any decent student does, not the secret to the top 1%.

Vary the recall modality each week/month: explain aloud, write it out, mind map, draw, teach, practice questions or papers. Small adjustments to how you run a technique have profound effects on its efficiency.

Common mistakes: re-reading notes as "revision"; skimming the notes right before attempting recall (most people do this and it defeats the purpose); read-then-recite treated as equivalent to true recall; believing active recall alone puts you in the top 1%.

## Reconstruction
Testing yourself by building **new** knowledge and relationships from the nodes you already hold, rather than recognising a cue or reproducing a stored answer. The highest-yield revision mode and the direct antidote to curveballs.

- Hierarchy: recognition < active/true/pure recall < reconstruction. Recall reproduces a memory you already know can be built; reconstruction attempts connections you have **never built** and checks whether they make sense.
- Memory is reconstructed from nodes and relationships every time you remember ([learning-science](learning-science.md)), so testing reconstruction is a purer test than testing recall. It requires knowing each node well enough to judge which connections are possible and which are impossible.
- It tests almost exclusively the top of Bloom's (analyse/evaluate/create) — and cannot be done without mastery of the levels below ([learning-science](learning-science.md)). It lets you weigh the importance of one idea against another, test whether a fact applies elsewhere, whether an equation is interchangeable in a new situation, and generate new hypotheses.
- Hobby analogy: ask "what if these two characters met?" — you can generate freely because you own the knowledge. People who truly know a subject can sit the exam years later and still score 100.
- Reconstruction is hard, and that difficulty is the signal it is working ([learning-science](learning-science.md) — desirable difficulty). BHS-trained students (inquiry, order control, processing) are already equipped for it.
- Under extreme stress the reconstruction machinery shuts down (fight-or-flight tunnel vision) even when connections are strong — protect a calm state ([exam-technique](exam-technique.md)).

**Derive it yourself** (Clinic 42): rather than reading a proof or worked result, attempt to prove/derive it yourself first (Feynman's habit; Bloom's "create"). Compare with the resource, locate exactly what you don't understand, expand steps the book skips, summarise. The napkin/whiteboard is the space to make it yourself. Reading a logical proof and feeling it "makes sense" is the illusion of competence — anything logical makes sense; the only real test is generating it unaided.

**Generative questions** (Clinic 42): when you hit a question you can't answer but must advance, write it down instead of stopping the whole subject. These conceptual, hard questions deserve to be answered on their own later — they double as high-quality retrieval practice. Cost-benefit on depth: if truly understanding "why" would take years and you actually need the result, memorising it (e.g. into Anki) is viable — only then.

Common mistakes: confusing cued recall with pure recall; believing you know a relationship because you can recall that it exists — reconstruction exposes that you cannot *use* it; reading the answer and concluding "I knew that".

WPW is the principal reconstruction-based technique taught in the course.

## Active Gap-Seeking & Re-Aiming Old Material
Deliberately hunting for weaknesses and starting revision there, rather than covering material left-to-right and hoping gaps appear. The biggest single time-saver in revision; gap-seeking techniques have **no ROI plateau** because time is only spent where gaps are.

**Re-Aiming** is the identical Aim step run on already-studied material in **Hipshot form** ([bear-hunter-system](bear-hunter-system.md)): skip keyword extraction, go straight to questions, deliberately generating questions you *cannot* answer.

- New vs old is only relative: photosynthesis with 3 background points, then 10, versus a PhD's 500 — all are "new" relative to what can be known. Hence the same technique applies.
- Inputs are your existing resources **plus** your mind maps, GRINDE maps and flashcards — not fresh material. The curiosity of Aim is now aimed at weaknesses; it functions as a second Skin pass probing relationships you may have missed.
- It produces "a past paper made purely of what you don't know" — without needing any past papers. Many generated questions will be out of exam scope; that is fine — they still flag a weak vicinity worth exploring.

Steps:
1. Pick a region you suspect is weak; gather familiar resources and your existing notes/maps/flashcards.
2. Hipshot: ask questions directly, no keyword pass. Deliberately ask cross-link questions you cannot answer.
3. Treat each unanswerable question as a compass pointing at a nearby gap.
4. Return to the source, find the missed paragraph or relationship, learn it. (A missed relationship often turns out to be an entire paragraph you never read.)
5. Re-teach / re-test; optionally answer the generated questions that are relevant enough. Move outward from the confirmed gap into related material.

Examples: maths — "Could differentiation be connected to incomplete integration?"; biology — "Could finding a common ancestor be related to speciation?"; chemistry — "Could collision theory be related to the particular catalyst used?"

- Your confidence predicts gap count: very confident → few gaps; recently studied, rushed earlier steps, or skipped Skin → more gaps. If you cannot find many gaps, check your Skin quality first ([chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md)).
- Curiosity is the brain's gap radar: self-made, highly connected questions are by construction your weak points, and one question revises multiple linked points simultaneously — a full hour spent purely on weaknesses versus perhaps 1–2 isolated gaps per hour of past papers, which may miss the surrounding gaps entirely. Getting everything right on a past paper after two hours of study is suspicious; the point is to find gaps ([aiming](aiming.md)).
- Hunt **higher-order gaps first** (Clinics 38, 39): a lower-order gap is a forgotten isolated fact (easy to fill); a higher-order gap is usually something *never covered* (how/why concepts relate) — harder to find, slower to fill, and discovering it right before an exam is costly. Filling higher-order gaps integrates previously "random" details and slashes rote volume. Success metric: **gaps found per hour**. See [learning-science](learning-science.md).

Common mistakes: stumbling on gaps passively via endless past papers; concluding "no gaps" when the method was never gap-seeking.

## Self-Testing Signals: Uncertainty Over Correctness
When self-testing, whether your answer was right or wrong is relatively meaningless; the signal that matters is your **uncertainty** and the thinking the attempt engaged (Clinic 31).

- Unsure but got it right = a gap exists (you got lucky; a slightly different variation would fail). Wrong while thinking you were right = you didn't even perceive the gap. The valuable data is the uncertainty, not the checkbox.
- Two gap situations: (a) confident but shouldn't be (hidden gaps) — testing at the appropriate level makes you fail to perform, revealing the gap and correcting confidence downward; (b) not confident — interleaved retrieval locates the gaps so you can fill/re-encode them.
- **MCQs can trigger cued recall** (an option looks right that you'd never have generated). Method: cover the options, read the stem, try to produce the answer; if you can't deduce it, that's a gap. This raises the challenge toward higher-order retrieval.
- Creating an answer sheet is itself valuable — a generative learning activity (generation effect), distinct from merely checking right/wrong. Don't skip it.
- You don't need to objectively analyse every mistake: correct interleaved retrieval at the right level finds gaps naturally; subjective confidence feelings are valid signals. How to know interleaving is working: a sense of knowledge consolidating, time well spent, improved confidence, a clearer map of weaknesses.
- **Confidence-check upgrade** (Clinic 39): generate an answer, rate confidence; if low, don't just regenerate or peek the answer sheet — go back to the material (not answers), build a new relationship/analogy/perspective or re-chunk to patch WHY it was weak, then regenerate. Direct filling tends to reoccur.
- **Practice-paper certainty review** (Clinic 12): leave a gap between answering and checking; sort answers into definitely-correct / on-the-fence / probably-wrong. Don't recheck the definitely-correct ones. On-the-fence answers signal a fundamental deficit even if the answer happened to be right (any question tests only ONE angle). Whether you got it right is irrelevant; whether you KNEW you were right is the signal. Study each uncertain item until you can confidently say "that's right/wrong", THEN check the key. Near real exams, do some papers timed under exam-like conditions for graduated exposure ([exam-technique](exam-technique.md)).
- For every error, assume a knowledge gap first and diagnose the cause; "silly mistake" is the last resort, never the default. Binary marking (peer marks right/wrong only, no answer shown, then redo) separates process issues from knowledge gaps (Clinic 41) — full protocol in [exam-technique](exam-technique.md).

## The Tunnel: Revision ROI & Plateau
The region of a conventional revision return-on-investment curve where extra hours produce roughly zero new learning, while the exam can still test material outside it.

- Conventional revision: high ROI early (e.g. 2 hours → +20% knowledge), then a rapid plateau into the tunnel. Diagnostic: for the hours invested, why only +3–4%? You could probably have scored 92 back at the high-ROI stage with a third of the time.
- Tunnel danger: you feel complete ("I got 96, that's good enough") while systematically missing examinable angles. Especially common in university and higher-level subjects; high-school students carry the tunnel habit forward.
- The tunnel forms because you outsourced gap-finding to something that gets less effective over time. Normal revision tests isolated parts of Bloom's; that isolation is why it plateaus ([learning-science](learning-science.md)).
- WPW / gap-seeking: moderate initial ROI (time-consuming) but **consistent** — no plateau, because time is only spent where gaps are. A tunnel is impossible unless you genuinely have no gaps; at that point you can score 100 on a paper made entirely of curveballs.
- If you cannot consistently score 100 on any paper for the subject, you are in the tunnel. The instructor knows of no revision technique with better ROI per hour invested than WPW.

**Nick Fury blind spots** (Clinic 12): a systematic, well-demarcated hole in an otherwise confident student — everything around it is so well known the hole becomes invisible. Cause: insufficient interleaving. Unconscious competence for ONE question variation (100% accuracy, bored of repeating it) is not domain-level competence. Revision naturally drifts toward what you know well because feeling less competent is unpleasant, so biases protect the blind spot. Boredom / "too easy" is a signal to CHANGE TACKS, not repeat. The remedy is deliberately seeking failure: "how could I be asked this in a way that would make me get it wrong?" — think like an examiner against yourself. Only after testing multiple angles, plus conversations and related material beyond the curriculum, and still finding it easy, can you trust domain mastery. "Be uncomfortable with so much comfort."

## Spacing the Revision Cycle
**Beginner minimum (must-do):** a fixed **weekly** slot reviewing everything studied that week, plus a fixed **monthly** slot reviewing everything covered that month — a simple, perfectly good starter form of spaced repetition. Sporadic "wing-it" revision is unstable and stressful; monthly-only review drowns in accumulated forgetting. Even a couple of weekend hours goes a long way; a little is better than nothing. Master this comfortably before adopting advanced systems. Rule of thumb: revise ~1 day, ~1 week, ~1 month after learning; lock the blocks into a diary with accountability ([time-management](time-management.md)).

**The canonical end-to-end ladder** — each checkpoint maps to a scope, a target layer of learning ([learning-science](learning-science.md)), and a technique:

1. **Pre-study — topic-wide.** Aim–Shoot cycle, no Skin yet; layers 1+2 (optionally bits of 3). Relational priority + importance-based chunking = high-efficiency first-pass encoding ([aiming](aiming.md)).
2. **Class/lecture — daily.** Consolidate layers 2+3 and parts of 4; make relationships accurate and explicit — not "they're linked" but exactly how, and how that compares with other relationships.
3. **Same-day review — daily.** Consolidate and add layers 3+4; Skin the mind map now; bring in textbook auxiliary details; reach ~90–100% understanding. Add memorisation aids: modified method of loci ± flashcards with active management ([memorisation](memorisation.md)).
4. **Midweek revision (optional) — 2–3 days of content.** Already Skinned, so repeating is lower yield: create **challenge questions**; optionally a mini brain dump of the day's material and questions from its weaknesses; swap questions with friends.
5. **End-of-week — topic-wide** (or as close as feasible). High-volume retrieval: **WPW reteaching preferred**; brain dumps (linear, mind-map, mini-topic) as alternatives or additions. In younger school years where a topic spans weeks/months, use a quarterly scope instead.
6. **End-of-month — multi-topic-wide.** Answer the challenge questions; another high-volume retrieval pass; past papers, practice questions and practice essays if available — always gap-seeking.
7. **Pre-test — entire subject-wide** (or up to the assessed area). Stylistic calibration: past papers, quizzing friends, group study; application adjustment and breaching questions ([exam-technique](exam-technique.md)).

**Concrete retrieval-spacing ladder** (don't stack retrieval practices): Day 0 mind map → Skin → GRINDE map; **+3–4 days** WPW reteach; **+4–5 days** create challenge questions; **+1–2 weeks** answer them; **+2 weeks** group study / group quizzing. Each mode is both a variation (interleaving) and a spaced repetition. Revising the same material every day never lets you forget, so you cannot measure true retention.

- Interleave everything at every stage; BHS is naturally interleaving. Flashcards run in **parallel whenever due** (microlearning), never batched into this ladder.
- University pace is roughly one topic per week, so topic-wide end-of-week revision works. Target weaknesses as early in the cycle as possible.
- Spacing follows encoding quality: better encoding → slower decay → wider gaps (beginner first retrieval 1–3 days; strong encoding 3–4 weeks) (Clinics 36, 38). Late-day exposure: scaffold immediately, consolidate next morning, pre-study predictable material (Clinic 28). Details in [learning-science](learning-science.md).
- Review after an interleaving session need not be immediate — it can wait for the next dedicated revision slot if the gap isn't too long and you use expanding gaps (Clinic 31).
- **Microspacing & flexibility** (Clinics 22, 23): spacing need not be exact to the day — batch/group sessions roughly (Wednesday, Saturday, next Saturday); don't min-max. Microspacing is casual spaced retrieval/interleaving right after learning (letting the brain "simmer" on just-learned ideas during a break) — basic consolidation that needs no formal schedule.
- **Re-encoding variations when retrieval finds a gap** (Clinic 21): don't just re-read the answer — output it differently (teach/explain from a new angle, hybrid questions mashing two past-paper questions together, group answer sheets). The generation effect means producing the answer contributes to learning even when imperfect; varying the form prevents the same-pathway plateau.

Common mistakes: no schedule at all (first real review just before the test); end-of-week revision on only half a topic when a full topic is feasible; re-Skinning at midweek instead of switching to gap-seeking retrieval; daily revision of the same material; doing WPW the day after the GRINDE map.

## High-Volume Retrieval First, Past Papers Last
**High-volume retrieval (HVR)** — comprehensive, detailed, non-cued, purposeful/synthesized recall of a large body of material (brain dumps, chunk-map dumps, WPW teaching) — is the most time-efficient gap-finder. Sequence revision around it (Clinic 12):

- **Start with HVR** at the earliest opportunity once enough material is encoded — BEFORE past papers and BEFORE answering your self-made challenge questions. Early gaps are numerous and findable without papers; starting with past papers wastes them.
- **Finish with past papers** (timed, exam-like where practical) for late-stage stylistic calibration ([exam-technique](exam-technique.md)). After a proper HVR pass, papers should feel noticeably easier (Clinic 13).
- **HVR checklist:** comprehensive (covers a lot, not one small chapter); detailed (tests the nitty-gritty); non-cued (cued recall is rare in academic testing — mainly languages/oral exams); purposeful + synthesized (an objective drives how you structure the output). Different synthesis formats test different Bloom's levels: a linear dump synthesizes readable text but activates less higher-order thinking; a mind-map/chunk-map dump adds structure; WPW adds comparative chunk-based flow plus fluent navigation.
- **Upgrade the objective to raise the level:** "explain the most important concepts, their main relationships, and how they relate to the rest of the topic" — equally comprehensive and detailed, but exercising order control, chunks and relationships.
- **Order weakest → strongest** across the schedule: every concept gets tested at least once, so a 10%-sure item is nearly a guaranteed lost mark while an 80%-sure item earns partial credit. Never start from strengths.
- **Fix gaps immediately** when HVR surfaces them (it lets the rest of the dump synthesize coherently). There is no answer sheet: fact-check anything you're unsure about yourself — time-consuming but highly effective learning. Weak encoding → many gaps → slow, but those gaps must be filled anyway, so the time is unavoidable, not wasted.
- If HVR found many gaps, run another round after filling them; if mostly clean, go straight to past papers. The instructor's own HVR of one old paper ran to 30+ pages over ~2 days (~30 hours) — feasible because first-pass encoding was efficient.

Common mistakes: starting with past papers; mindless coverage instead of weakest-first; checking an answer sheet instead of fact-checking yourself.

## Pre-Revision Detail Sweep (Layer-4 Clean Sweep)
Before formal revision, sweep your resources for arbitrary layer-4 details you never captured, and route each one to the right tool. You must pick up these details anyway; doing it at flashcard/loci creation time is efficient rather than leaving it as a separate later task.

1. If you collected details while studying, go through that document and pick them out.
2. If not, flick through all resources (course guides, textbooks, class notes) asking: "Is there anything I missed that I don't already understand or have memorised?"
3. For each find, decide: reconnect and integrate it into the mind map, or rote-learn it.
4. If rote: choose modified method of loci versus flashcard ([memorisation](memorisation.md)).

The same clean-sweep logic is the final pass of the multipass system, checked against learning objectives (below).

## Whole-Part-Whole (WPW) Reteaching
A reconstruction-based revision technique: **teach an entire topic out loud from pure recall to an imaginary student who knows nothing**, constantly oscillating between the whole (big picture and importance) and the parts (concepts and details), using reverse explanations.

Why it works:
- **Tests all six Bloom's levels at once** — big-picture understanding, decomposition, relationships between components, sub-concepts, detail recall, fluent explanation. You start at the top of Bloom's and work down; one of the only techniques that does this ([learning-science](learning-science.md)).
- **Become your own best teacher.** Results that track how good your teacher was mark a low-level learner; the whole-part-whole teaching model (attributed in the lesson to Malcolm Knowles) is presented as the gold standard of effective teaching. You get both the good teacher (yourself) and the skill (because you are being the good teacher) — see [learning-science](learning-science.md) for good-teacher dependency.
- **Gap → inquiry loop:** when you cannot explain something, that is a gap; go learn it so you can teach it; return and re-teach. That loop *is* inquiry-based learning ([aiming](aiming.md)), and it is why the technique is so effective.
- **Repeatable:** unlike most techniques, WPW stays effective on the 2nd, 3rd, even 4th run because your knowledge keeps changing. You do not need four more revision techniques for the rest of the year. After a few runs, past papers feel "a joke". Students have learned more about a topic than their teacher knew, in the same year they first learned it.
- **Time cost is a feature:** 1–2 hours of teaching can become 5–10 hours across two days because of the back-and-forth gap filling — the time is spent on effective material. A semester's material ≈ a full weekend (12–14 hours for a master's) (Clinic 31).
- An imaginary student beats a real friend: a friend already knows things, so you skip explanations and they never ask the hard questions.
- It should mirror your importance-chunked mind map closely: chunks are groups of things important for the same reason ([chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md)).

Steps:
1. Wait **at least 3 days after the GRINDE map** (ideally end of week) so weak relationships soften and become detectable; GRINDE retention is high enough that little is lost.
2. Choose the whole topic — topic-wide, as comprehensive as time allows, to full exam depth.
3. Teach out loud (or whisper) from pure memory. No notes, no looking at the mind map.
4. Open with the whole: why this topic matters, in lay terms, built from what the listener already knows.
5. Break it into main chunks; justify each chunk's importance *before* naming it.
6. Drop the label, then go deep into the part with full exam-level detail.
7. After every part, return to the whole ("that mattered because…") and give a mini summary/checkpoint.
8. Move to the next chunk and repeat: whole → part → whole → sub-part → part → whole … constantly re-anchoring (the detail level can still be relatively linear).
9. Doodle a throwaway mind map at speaking speed purely to track thoughts.
10. On any gap: pause, go to mind map / GRINDE map / notes / source, learn it, return, re-teach.
11. If the first run was full of gaps, run the whole thing again on a later day.

- Continuous self-check: "Is what I'm saying relevant to the big picture? Is it clear why this detail matters?" If not, zoom back out. Roughly 99.9% of mental effort goes to the big picture: you navigate relationships at ultra-high speed internally while speaking slowly externally. Slow speech does not mean slow revision — you are examining the entire topic continuously from many angles while creating a new verbal expression of it.
- Linear teaching (term → definition → example, next term…) is the old habit to break: you do not hold knowledge linearly, so do not teach it linearly.
- If you cannot explain the opening big picture without diving into details, your understanding is fact-based — go back to the early Shoot mind map (layer-1 logic) and revise that.
- **It should be hard.** Even top-1% students consistently struggle the first time. If it feels easy, there is an overwhelming chance you did it wrong (desirable difficulty, [learning-science](learning-science.md)). It is "advanced" mainly for people with no initiation into learning-how-to-learn; for BHS practitioners it is not that hard.
- You will know it is working when you are in the find-gap → figure-out → re-explain cycle and your confidence keeps rising until no exam question could surprise you.

## WPW Execution Tips
The practical rules that make WPW work and keep it honest:

- **Off pure recall only.** No external reference while teaching; do not give yourself a chance to look at the mind map.
- **Speak or whisper out loud.** Whispering saves your voice. Verbalisation forces you to slow down and adds an unfamiliar expression mode, which confers extra learning.
- **Slow down deliberately.** Like running a hand over a surface to feel bumps — slash too fast and you feel nothing. Sometimes go slower than necessary; do 3–4 retakes of one concept until every explanation is reverse.
- **Doodle a mind map at speaking speed**, purely to track thoughts: not neat, legible, or wordy — symbols and doodles are fine. It is not an archival note set; it is also a feedback artefact (coaches can read WPW correctness from patterns in it). **Never teach to the mind map**: a 3–5 second pause to finish a keyword is acceptable; minutes of drawing is not.
- **Don't get locked in** to teaching it the way you first learned it — that loses the interleaving benefit. Doodling a throwaway map forces a new expression of the relationships.
- **Allow yourself to break off and revise mid-teach**, then return and re-teach. If the first run is full of gaps, just run it again — two to four runs remain effective.
- **Mini checkpoints and summaries** (intermediate-to-advanced upgrade): after a set of parts, "so we've covered X, Y, Z and they matter because…", then signpost the next chunk. Without them the listener (and you) lose track of which chunk you are in.
- **Go topic-wide and to full exam depth.** If the topic is biodiversity, teach all of biodiversity, not just species diversity. Use layer-3/4 details during the lay explanation when they make it tangible, rather than saving everything for the end.
- Keep a written prompt in front of you: "Why is it important? How does it relate to everything else? Is that clear?"
- Use a **fixed weekly teaching day** (Saturday or Sunday). Thursday–Friday material can wait until the next weekend — an 8–9 day delay is fine given GRINDE retention.
- Bring recordings to Live Clinics or submit them for monthly feedback; this is a complex skill and individual feedback helps.

Common mistakes: rushing (people who rush almost always do it badly; it cannot be sped up without losing the gap-finding — see [habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md)); producing an archival-quality mind map; missing mini summaries; teaching only half the topic or not to exam depth; doing it the day after the GRINDE map when nothing has had a chance to weaken.

## Reverse Explanations
Explain an idea through its relationships and importance first, and attach the term or label only at the end — the inverse of "here is the term, here is the definition".

- A conventional explanation says "yoink, here is a new piece of information, good luck connecting that" — isolated, fact-based, memorisation-flavoured. A reverse explanation extends what the listener already knows until they realise something must exist, then names it — relational, intuitive, more memorable.
- **Integral to WPW, not optional:** a symptom of doing WPW correctly is that reverse explanations appear naturally. Not using them is a sure sign you are doing it wrong. They force you to justify importance with no definition to fall back on, explicitly testing your knowledge of associations and relationships.
- If you cannot build a reverse explanation for a concept, you do not know it as well as you think — go and learn it.
- **Record yourself and listen back for term-first patterns** — the single most useful self-diagnostic.

Steps:
1. Start from what the imaginary student already knows.
2. Build the need and importance progressively in lay terms, with examples.
3. Let the listener reach "I understand this, but I don't know what it's called."
4. Drop the label.
5. Re-explain in full technical detail using the now-anchored terminology.
6. Return to the whole.

Examples:
- **Oxygen, normal:** "There is something called oxygen. Oxygen is a molecule made of two oxygen atoms, involved in cellular respiration, which is why we breathe."
- **Oxygen, reverse:** "Animals need to breathe → because of a specific molecule in the air → that molecule keeps our cells alive → that energy-making process is called cellular respiration → the molecule is formed from two atoms of the same element → and that is called oxygen."
- **Balance of payments:** explain the "country's giant two-way receipt" fully in lay terms, then name entry / structure / economic transactions at the end.
- **Biodiversity:** "factors not produced by living things… biotic just means produced by living things… and that is called abiotic vs biotic" — label arrives last.

Common mistakes: slipping into terminology crutch mid-explanation (very common even in top-achieving students); using a term in its expert sense before the lay foundation exists.

## Terminology Crutch & Leading with Importance
The most common WPW failure: naming and defining chunks ("there are three concepts: entry, economic transactions, structure…") instead of first establishing why each matters. The fix: **lead with importance, always**.

- Correct order: big picture → why it matters in lay terms → specify the importance of each sub-area with examples → drop the labels → deep technical detail → back to the whole. In the economics example, none of the explanation required knowing the terms — so the terms should have come last.
- Importance-based chunking and WPW are the same skill: chunks are groups of things important for the same reason, and WPW should mirror that chunking ([chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md)).
- This calibrates the same **"importance radar"** used when reading, listening in class, or asking questions — two sides of the same coin. A brain not tuned to recognise when importance has been communicated will over-explain when teaching and under-detect when reading. It also lets you traverse from the logic layer down to details and back up fluently, without justifying every single point.
- Different subjects communicate importance differently (chemistry ≠ biology ≠ physics ≠ economics ≠ philosophy ≠ politics ≠ English ≠ maths). Don't expect every subject to feel the same or need the same lay build-up.
- Conciseness comes from knowing exactly what must be communicated to justify importance — not from ticking off a chunk checklist. Ask only: "Does the listener understand why what I'm about to say matters?" If yes, move on.
- Example (electromagnetism): if the audience already knows forces, say "electromagnetism is another type of force, like gravity" and move on — three minutes of build-up could be 30 seconds. Likewise: "you remember opposites attract and likes repel with bar magnets — but why?" is enough.

Common mistakes: treating WPW as a checklist of chunks to tick off; over-justifying because you have not decided what actually communicates importance; long drawing pauses that break the speaking flow. Practice plus self-review of recordings is what tunes conciseness; expect a learning curve even at the top 0.1% level.

## WPW Example Dissections
Four coached student submissions used to train your ear and eye for WPW errors. Listen actively and try to spot the issue before the dissection — otherwise you will not detect the same errors in your own recordings. These are near-final attempts: your first attempts will fail at more obvious things (e.g. forgetting a layer-3/4 detail) that you can usually fix yourself.

Judge each attempt on: reverse explanation present? terminology crutch? chunk and sub-chunk anchoring clear? returning to the whole? full depth and breadth? concise? done from memory? Self-correction mid-teach ("two concepts… actually three") is normal, not an error. A gap caught live is the technique working: the correction is retained far better because it was learned to fix a teaching gap.

- **Economics — balance of payments.** Good importance-first opening using the shopping-receipt analogy (a country's giant two-way receipt; exporting coal, importing Toyotas; Woolworths' receipt is all positive entries while yours is all negative). Failure: names "entry / economic transactions / structure" as a list before explaining why each matters. Coach re-teaches with true reverse explanation, labels last: money/goods/services in and out → some transactions gain, some lose → many different types (education ≠ coal ≠ money transfer) → needs a clean analysable record → three components (entry = gain/loss classification; structure = grouping; economic transactions = the record itself). Verdict: a good first attempt; the student clearly knows the content.
- **Chemistry — equilibrium.** Good reverse explanation of limiting/excess reactions vs equilibrium (label at the end); nice return to the whole ("they combine, as in they bump into each other"). Early "atoms/molecules" terminology is a grey area — permissible because conceptually light and introduced inside a story; better still to build more lay foundation ("look at your phone, desk, mug, the ocean — all made of tiny building blocks; why is water different from your skin?"). **Calibrate the imaginary student to your own level:** Year 9–10 assume nothing; Year 12/university may reasonably assume atoms and molecules. Failure: terminology crutch when listing "systems, calculations, Le Chatelier's principle, Gibbs free energy and entropy". Fix: justify chunks by shared importance — the environment and reagents; predicting/measuring/calculating so industry doesn't waste money or cause danger; the underlying principles — and notice it is three chunks, not four. Give real-world applications (body, industry, vaccines, bleach) *before* naming equilibrium, not "I'll talk about that later". The pause before "why is this important?" is the technique forcing you to think about importance — where memorised-only knowledge is exposed.
- **Physics — electromagnetism.** Good technique: using the word itself ("electro" + "magnet") to derive the first big chunks; good curiosity chain: desk lamp → power point → power lines → power station → how is electricity generated → the laptop charger brick. Failures: long drawing pauses that break flow; over-long justification because the student has not decided exactly what communicates importance. Fix: frame electromagnetism as another force like gravity if forces are known; recall "opposites attract, likes repel" then ask why.
- **Biology — biodiversity / species interactions.** The best-rounded of the four. Strong reverse explanations (abiotic/biotic labels last); excellent use of specific details inside the lay stage: the furless wolf; clownfish hiding in coral rock and trading nutrients ("fish poop") for anemone protection = mutualism; turtle carrying barnacles = commensalism, which becomes parasitism as the load grows; meerkat/boar/tick multi-species web. Repeatedly re-anchors to the big picture of "not dying"; shows the four interactions on a spectrum: mutualism → commensalism → amensalism → parasitism. Live gap caught: student mislabels amensalism, self-corrects — the correction will be retained at much higher quality. Coach's suggested mini summary: "biodiversity refers to all these factors, living and non-living, biotic and abiotic, that contribute to the adaptive pressure for species to not die, plus the interactions between them." Failures: missing mini checkpoints/summaries so chunk anchoring is unclear; possibly not full exam depth or topic breadth.

Always confirm three things about your own attempt: was it from memory, was it topic-wide, and was it deep enough for the exam? Verbal fluency lags behind logical flow at the start; a shunted but clearly directed flow is still good progress.

## Brain Dumps
Free reconstruction of a topic from memory onto a blank page or map — a high-volume retrieval option alongside WPW. Variants: **linear**, **mind-map**, and **mini / semi-topic**.

- WPW is preferred when you have the skill: broader, tests all Bloom's levels seamlessly, finds gaps faster than a linear dump. A linear dump can follow WPW if time allows (e.g. end-of-week WPW plus a fortnightly dump). A mini brain dump of a single day's material can seed challenge questions.
- **The reconstructive standard** (Clinic 38): replicating your old map exactly is lower/mid-order; **recalling AND rearranging into a different structure/grouping** is strongly higher-order — one of the most gap-finding-efficient techniques because it forces recall + re-chunking simultaneously.
- 48-hour rule: don't look at the notes you'll dump for ~48 hours; start blank, everything closed. Refreshing short-term memory right before is "coddling" and invalidates the test.
- Check your old notes only AFTER your best attempt, and keep thinking higher-order while checking ("would this connection work? can I re-group this part?") — this keeps the brain "in gear". Peeking flashcard-style stops generation and loses most of the learning benefit.
- Use at end-of-week / end-of-month as high-volume retrieval, and always gap-seek rather than dump mindlessly. At early course stages, simple brain dumps (mind map, linear, learning-objectives) are the right starting challenge — measure retention (Clinics 28, 29).
- **You revise knowledge, not mind maps** (Clinic 17): a map used as a retrieval tool is a "mind-map brain dump"; visuals are unnecessary in dumps because their job is encoding. The dump need NOT replicate the original map's shape — recalling AND rearranging into a different grouping is the higher-order, more gap-finding form (see reconstructive standard above).
- **Learning-objectives brain dump** (Clinic 13): a fast pre-exam diagnostic — get the official objectives, come in fresh (no notes), and under each write everything you know from memory. Every moment of uncertainty marks a weakness: relearn it to solid strength, slightly OUTSIDE exam scope for a safety margin. Higher coverage than past papers when time is short; a whole topic can be dumped in a day. If everything is weak there is no shortcut — the first encoding never happened, so go through it all. Then do past papers, applying the same uncertainty rule.

## Teaching as Retrieval: The Repackaging Standard
Teaching is a mid-spectrum (declarative–procedural) interleaved retrieval technique whose value depends on the standard you hold (Clinics 38, 39).

- Learning = teaching yourself (repackage for your brain); teaching others = repackage for THEIR brain. **Genuinely repackaging** — re-group, re-label, re-connect, re-evaluate into a structure that makes sense for the audience, different from how you learned it — forces higher-order thinking and finds more gaps than regurgitating your own learned structure (which gives only low/mid-order benefit). Slightly more time, but more gaps per hour.
- You must do the heavy lifting of grouping yourself — even if you end at the textbook's categories, you must examine alternatives and DECIDE.
- An imaginary student who knows nothing can beat a real friend: friends who know the topic fill your gaps and nod, masking weak explanations.
- For your OWN higher-order benefit while teaching, use analogies to common knowledge/your prior knowledge, or implant prior knowledge in the imaginary student and connect to it (Clinic 39).
- Teaching procedural content requires DOING it (you can't teach coding without showing/running code), so it tests procedural elements too.
- **Feynman-technique caveat** (Clinic 40): explain-to-a-novice is great retrieval but high cognitive effort; if your knowledge is lower-order you can't build the higher-order explanation. Simplifying the audience (15-year-old instead of 10-year-old) removes the higher-order aspect — the "easy" version becomes ineffective. Use tiers instead: 5-year-old → 15-year-old → university student, paired with flashcards for exact recall.
- Break teaching into available time pockets (e.g. 30 min now, 30 min later) — lectures themselves are time-boxed this way; you needn't cover everything in one go.
- If teaching works as revision, migrate it into encoding: consume AND try to teach as you go (Clinic 38).

## Challenge Questions & Curveball Construction
Questions you author from your own detected weaknesses, used as a midweek retrieval step and answered later in the cycle. The examiner-mindset creation method lives in [exam-technique](exam-technique.md); the revision-cycle mechanics:

- **Created at the midweek revision, after Skin** — repeating the same encoding would be lower yield. Optionally seed with a mini brain dump of that day's material, then build questions from the weaknesses it exposes. Target weaknesses as early in the cycle as possible.
- **Answered at the end-of-month stage**, respecting spacing. Swap with friends for diversity and interest; in group study, share and create challenging questions with each other or use pre-made questions ([exam-technique](exam-technique.md)).
- Distinct from **breaching questions**: challenge questions probe known weaknesses; breaching questions deliberately exceed your current ceiling ([exam-technique](exam-technique.md)).
- **Building curveballs** (Clinics 29, 31): changing values → adding a step → adding factors is OK early but slow. The more effective route is importing OTHER CONCEPTS as new variables: "if this happens but that goes down/up, how is this affected?" — this ratchets difficulty up substantially and tests distant relationships.
- Questions must make sense: relating two fundamentally unrelated things (eye drops vs TV) shows you understand neither. Use reference material to validate that a new variable plausibly interacts before integrating it; read up briefly, confirm, then build the question. Pad remaining uncertainty with the reference to consolidate/re-encode.
- The **extended method** (full-recall answer sheets, out-of-scope answering) raises load, identifies leaks, and exploits the hypercorrection effect ([learning-science](learning-science.md)). Creating questions without notes is good but limited to what you know (unknown unknowns) — sometimes scan the source to build challenge questions that expose hidden weaknesses (Clinic 31).
- Inflexible knowledge (good retention but overwhelmed by curveballs) indicates lower mastery — partly encoding quality, partly the natural pre-interleaving state. As encoding improves, seeing these pathways gets easier.
- **Designing legitimately challenging questions** (Clinics 26, 27): combine concepts and add variables/conditions — pick concept 1, write an isolated question; pick related concept 2; combine ("how does concept 1 affect concept 2?"); add concepts 3–4 (already very challenging); calibrate by adding variables/ifs ("if the xylem were 50% leakier, in a high-salinity atmosphere, how would transpiration affect water transport?"). "Challenging" is subjective to you — don't write questions you already know you can answer. **Write the answers down**: mental answering triggers the illusion of explanatory depth; a step-by-step attempt reveals missed pockets (generation effect). This is only one interleaving form — use several from the table.

## Complex Application Bank
For procedural/applied knowledge, build a personalised bank of "hardest possible exam questions" during first encoding, then attempt them cold in later retrieval sessions (Clinics 38, 39).

1. While forming networks/relationships at first encoding, jot possible complex applications alongside them (which software feature relies on this set of functions; which reaction pathway converts A to B; a composite maths problem). This aids initial chunking AND creates the bank.
2. In a retrieval session (e.g. 3 days later), pick one and attempt it from scratch; reason through the logic (target the weakest bond, catalyse with heat, introduce a reagent), then check against answers/material.
3. Programming variants: compare alternative implementations (costs/benefits), build an app needing two interrelating functions, or vary frameworks, client priorities and deadlines to force comparison of approaches.

Common mistakes: only doing isolated practice problems without composite, multi-relationship applications; not capturing application ideas during encoding. Execution-type techniques in the interleaving table suit procedural subjects; the same higher-order techniques apply to maths/physics with no real exceptions.

## Question Banks & the Answer-Sheet Method
Practice questions and question banks come **after encoding**, used as interleaving — not as the core encoding method (Clinics 22, 23). A question bank is a time-locked, linear, repetitive method (flashcards in question form): more questions → learning rises only linearly, so volume alone cannot improve efficiency.

- **Encode-first vs question-first depends on prior knowledge:** low (e.g. 6/10 wrong) → too many gaps, learn the theory first (bank gap-filling is inefficient and sacrifices structure); high (e.g. 8/10 right) → questions can trigger gap-filling. Don't smash through a bank (e.g. 3000 USMLE questions) before encoding (Clinic 23).
- **The high-learning way to use a bank** (Clinic 18): (1) **answer** the question fully (no peeking); (2) **create your own answer sheet/explanation** — this forces further study and exposes that you can't explain it properly, where much learning happens; (3) **compare** with the real answer. Each step is a generation event. With enough practice, steps 1–2 deliver so much that step 3 feels almost redundant. No official answer sheet (common at uni)? Not a problem — steps 1–2 carry the learning.
- **Never check answers before genuinely attempting** — it skips the generative benefit and falsely elevates confidence via the illusion of fluency (worse than wasted time). Looking at the solution whenever stuck is terrible (Clinics 18, 23).
- **Problem-solving subjects (maths/physics):** constantly forgetting hard-won solutions signals missing *conceptual* encoding (not knowing how/when concepts apply), not insufficient repetition. Fix the chunking/relationships of the underlying concepts, then drill with interleaved problem variations plus the answer-sheet method. Knowing three concepts are related and usable in a certain way makes the approach come to mind; then practise the procedure (Clinic 18).
- **Getting stuck is a diagnostic, not a one-off** (Clinic 25): a question you can't solve reveals a gap in how you think about the concepts. Don't check the answer until you've genuinely tried; step back, map the concepts/relationships, revisit the material for the missing angle. If you can't even map them, that's a more fundamental gap. After a solid hour or two of real effort with no progress you may check — but never say "that makes sense" and move on: identify the relationship/pattern you were missing, then dive back in. Prefer 3 hours on one question that unlocks 100 others over 30 minutes that answers 5 variants. Small conceptually-unimportant gaps can be filled with a flashcard; significant "can't even approach it" gaps deserve deep investment.

Common mistakes: using questions as the core learning method; checking solutions early; re-memorising solutions instead of encoding concept applicability; blocking practice by problem type (no interleaving).

## Revision Variety & the Interleaving Table
Revision must vary the retrieval method across sessions; repeating one method is low-yield after the first pass (Clinics 28, 29, 31).

- Re-explain-from-memory + error-log revision is not bad, but it walks the same pathways with little interleaving. Do it once (e.g. the day after), then switch methods for the 1-week and 1-month reviews (Q&A/answer-sheet creation, other table techniques). "Don't do the same thing multiple times" is a good general rule.
- The interleaving table is **not a chronological ladder and not level-gated**: start with the most challenging-but-doable techniques; keep a mix because low-level methods may still be needed for assessment. Flashcards run as a parallel stream, not replacing higher-level sessions.
- Mind maps are not the tool for verbatim definitions — use memorisation techniques for those ([memorisation](memorisation.md)). At higher levels there is less to memorise and definitions are easier to justify logically; formal curricula still require some rote, but ~50 flashcards instead of ~2,000 leaves time for them.
- Ask "what is my technique achieving (which order, which gaps)?" rather than "is it OK to do X?" — fill the gap if there is one. Diagnose and design your own techniques with "how does this make me think?" and the order/declarative-procedural manipulation rules ([learning-science](learning-science.md); Clinic 39).
- Practise at least 3–4 interleaving techniques during Fundamentals; a mature learner comfortably uses ~5–6 (up to 7–8 of the table's 20–30). Efficient interleaved retrieval requires decent initial encoding covering all levels — otherwise you forget too fast to afford the good techniques. Efficiency compounds over 2–4 weeks and frees time to reinvest (Clinics 28, 31).

## When to Stop: Endpoints & 80/20 Gap-Targeting
Where you stop depends on your required outcome (Clinic 35). Three signals:

1. **Genuine, tested confidence** — good schema, noted down, interleaved retrieval confirms good mastery AND retention.
2. **Curriculum learning objectives as guideposts** (only if a formal curriculum exists); often only ~40% of available information is relevant, especially postgrad.
3. **Interleaved-retrieval gap-finding** across multi-day/week learning: locate weak subsets and target depth there. Poor higher-order mastery + low retention on a subset → go deeper/reconceptualise; poor lower-order → pad with lower-order strategies or lift it into higher-order so it's memorable.

**Pareto:** reach ~80–85%, then let retrieval target the remaining gaps rather than fine-tooth combing to 100% first — scattered tiny gaps can take longer than the first 80%. Fine-tooth combing is the most comprehensive but very time-costly; do it only if time allows, and after retrieval. Always test yourself to find gaps regardless of which criterion applies. Professionals without a curriculum rely on confidence + real-life testing; their endpoint is rarely exam-level recall of everything — see [professional-learning](professional-learning.md).

Common mistakes: trying to learn every detail up front before testing; chasing a tiny missed detail worth ~0.5 mark with a ~0.001% chance of appearing.

## Exam-Season Revision Strategy
When exams are close, **stabilize and optimize — don't add** (Clinics 12, 13, 24).

- **Don't learn new techniques** (especially encoding) within ~2–4 weeks of exams. A new technique costs efficiency until ~70% through competency; you can't reach its pay-off zone before near-term exams. "We built the engine beforehand; we don't upgrade the car during the race." Stay in Fundamentals; revision/scheduling techniques are near plug-and-play and the right investment ([course-guide](course-guide.md)).
- **If material is brand-new/hard and time is short, accept the limit:** go deep on Fundamentals with heavy interleaving; don't rush into growth-phase techniques. You probably won't go from poor to excellent encoding in two weeks — the real goal is to not repeat the mistake next exam (Clinic 24).
- **While literally sitting exams**, switch from practice to pure execution optimization: add no new variables; spend ~5 minutes daily running big-metric CoBS on your current system ("how did today's revision/exam go?") and design ~3 simple experiments for tomorrow. After exams, do one thorough reflection, then resume course progression (Clinic 12).
- **Mirror revision to exam order when encoding is weak** (Clinic 13): study the LAST exam's subject first and the FIRST exam's subject immediately before it, so recency replaces spacing for the nearest exam. Space the remaining subjects between exam dates as best you can. Don't study a subject once at the start and leave it untouched until its exam weeks later. (The Crippling Anxiety Clinic gives the same first-exam-last ordering rule.)
- **Bouncing back from missed/poor revision** (Clinic 21): fix the root cause first (COLBE why you missed it — often missing main-idea framing), then rebuild compliance gradually with marginal gains (e.g. 0% → 80%). "No point bouncing back if the thing that made you fall is still there."

Common mistakes: starting advanced encoding weeks before exams; rushing the course to cram techniques; resuming revision without diagnosing why you fell off.

## Cramming: Why Conventional Cramming Fails
Conventional cramming = the same familiar low-efficiency technique × more hours (3 h/day → 12 h/day) × more speed × rushing, usually right before the exam after a period of procrastination.

- Genuine upsides: time pressure raises focus, which slightly raises retention and flow; urgency raises perceived relevance; the short horizon means low retention matters less; rushing does skip some low-yield content.
- But retention only rises from ~30% to ~45–50%, and that gain is **stress-dependent** — neither healthy nor sustainable. BHS-level retention is 90–99%.
- **You are not speed-limited:** with a given technique you already work at ~80–90% of maximum speed. Speeding up raises the error rate and the amount skipped.
- **You skip the wrong things:** difficult and important concepts (which would make the details learnable) get skipped because they look time-consuming, while easy random details get covered.
- More hours → lower efficiency → less sleep → lower retention → higher error rate: a downward spiral. Sleep-dependent consolidation also fails, so you cannot use what you learned ([sleep-rest](sleep-rest.md)).
- No order control: linear topic 1 → 10; run out of time and the tail is simply unlearned. Low ability to apply information → curveballs out of reach; shallow logic → fewer first principles to reason from.
- Not sustainable: high-school crammers get crushed at university, where one lecture can contain a week of high-school content — especially in medicine and dentistry. Cramming can pass degrees, but the cost is paid later as years of professional catch-up ([professional-learning](professional-learning.md)).

Common mistakes: still writing scribbly notes because you are married to "you must write notes to learn"; believing more hours compensates for low retention (it compounds the loss); linear, no-order-control coverage.

## Effective Cramming: Three Guidelines
Keep cramming's upsides (pressure, focus, strategic skipping) and remove its downsides by changing the technique and the timing:

1. **Skip strategically, not linearly.** Ask: what is going to be assessed, and what can I afford to miss? The 80% argument: covering 80% of material *across the whole spectrum* can cover ~99% of what is examinable; covering 80% front-to-back can miss ~20% of the questions. Curricula are legally bound to learning objectives (some universities require at least one question per lecture), so missing the tail of a linear pass misses whole lectures' worth of marks. Use learning objectives as the yield filter.
2. **Maximise retention per hour.** Use the highest-retention technique available (BHS-level, 90%+), not a 30–50% one. Retention maths: at 50% retention you need ~2× hours, but extra hours drop retention to ~30%, needing ~3×, dropping to ~25% — you can never catch up. At 90% retention, +10% hours covers everything; +50% hours at 85% retention still works. When time is limited, spend it only on what converts directly into exam answers.
3. **Protect optimal sleep.** Consolidation and next-day access depend on it; it is a guideline, not an optional extra ([sleep-rest](sleep-rest.md)). If you must pull a late night, do it BEFORE the final night — never the night immediately before the test.

Result: less stress, less sleep deprivation, saved time, and results that may not suffer at all. The **multipass system** is the technique that satisfies all three guidelines.

Strategic ordering under exam pressure (Crippling Anxiety Clinic): study the subject of your FIRST exam LAST so the final thing you study is the first exam; prioritise fundamental objectives and frequently-recurring variations over details; secure partial marks across all questions rather than perfecting one. Full meso-planning in [time-management](time-management.md) and [exam-technique](exam-technique.md).

**Cram only lower-order material** (Clinics 38, 39): isolated facts CAN be crammed in the final ~2 weeks (forgetting afterwards doesn't matter). NEVER cram higher-order learning — you would work under optimum the whole lead-up. Higher-order network building belongs in the early phases; lower-order memorisation belongs nearest the assessment (Clinic 40).

## Cram Early
Use a high-efficiency cramming technique (multipass) at the *start* of the learning period instead of the night before the exam: **"If your test is in August, cram it in February."**

- Finish the year's content in the first couple of months, leaving ~8 solid months of low-stress, high-quality revision. Results still reach 90–100% because good revision principles run throughout; the remaining months turn ~72% coverage into "120%", so 99–100% becomes unavoidable.
- Only possible with strong technique and efficiency — inconceivable for most students. Related: batch/bulk studying whole topics or semesters up front ([time-management](time-management.md)).
- Spend the freed time on hobbies, sport, music, friends — the year is less stressful because the work was front-loaded.

## Multipass System
A layered cramming method built on the Swiss-cheese model ([learning-science](learning-science.md)): repeatedly pass over the **entire block** of material at increasing depth, so each pass's holes are unlikely to line up with the previous pass's holes.

- Each pass is one layer. You study the same whole block (e.g. all five weeks of a subject) each time, **not topic-by-topic** — topic-by-topic loses the layering benefit.
- Only *what* and *when* you study changes. All existing technique — inquiry, traffic-light system, Hipshot, the note-taking checklist, processing, cognitive habits — stays identical.
- **Worked 5-day example:** Day 1 pass 1 across all 5 weeks (~10% understanding) → Day 2 pass 2 (~25%) → Days 3–4 pass 3 split into halves (~60%) → Day 5 revision / gap sweep (~80%). At 90%+ retention that is ~72% of total material learned in 5 days, which can map to ~95% of examinable content. Conventional cramming reaches only 30–40%.
- Typical run: 3–4 passes, in 70–80% of cases. Big topics may need 2 days on the concept passes, or 3 days on details (passes 3–5).
- Even if you only reach pass 2 before time runs out, you have enough logic and concepts to derive roughly 70% of unfamiliar answers in the exam — and some details come memorised for free.
- Always finish with a **clean sweep** pass: check the learning objectives for anything missed, then patch via flashcards, modified method of loci, re-consolidation into the mind map, or active flashcard management (same logic as the Pre-Revision Detail Sweep above; tools in [memorisation](memorisation.md)). You will find there is not much left.
- Prerequisites: extremely good order control, inquiry-based learning, effective note-taking, and confidence in your technique. Most students cannot learn it without BHS-level skill; taught at Summit because it is overwhelming earlier (Crippling Anxiety Clinic).
- Multipass is designed for rapid sprints and burst studying — one ready-made personalisation of the system ([professional-learning](professional-learning.md)).
- **Break in layers, not linearly** (Clinic 22): each sitting covers the whole topic shallowly; breadth always, depth comes with repeated layers. Don't reduce breadth for depth in a short session. The full system requires ~100% technique accuracy to be worth its time-efficiency gain — don't attempt it early.
- **Large resources by skill level** (Clinic 18): beginners segment into concept blocks and apply known techniques; intermediates rank segments easiest→hardest (order control) so a more knowledgeable version of you meets the hard material; advanced learners slice into ~20 layers of increasing detail, one pass per session. Prerequisites for the advanced form: intuitive mastery of layers of learning, automatic importance-based chunking, higher-order learning virtually all the time, very high cognitive-load tolerance, order control as second nature.

Steps:
1. Define the whole block to be covered (topic-wide, unit-wide, or multi-week).
2. Choose the inquiry-led (default) or objective-led (exam-pressure) variation.
3. Run pass 1 across the entire block at the shallowest useful layer.
4. Run subsequent passes across the entire block, each deeper.
5. Run the final clean-sweep pass against the learning objectives; patch remaining gaps with memorisation and revision tools.
6. If you crammed early, spend the following weeks/months on effective revision.

**Why it works — the shape of knowledge:** most knowledge (and most exam marks) lives in logic and concepts, while details consume half or more of study time but yield few marks (usually low-mark questions). Conventional linear studying inverts the allocation: lots of time on details, very little on logic — so logic and concepts rise slowly, details don't make sense and aren't retained, and total time balloons. Multipass flips it: pass 1 logic; pass 2 logic + concepts; pass 3 mainly concepts; pass 4 details. Logic rises fast first; concepts follow exponentially once logic exists; details become learnable only after concepts. **Derivation beats memorisation under uncertainty:** a 100-question exam could draw from 1,000–10,000 possible facts; memorising 400 leaves at least a 60% chance of missing what is needed (~40% hit rate even with perfect recall), while strong logic and concepts give roughly a 70% chance of deriving the answer on the spot. Curveballs stop being curveballs when logic is dense. Layers of learning are a way of *thinking* about information, not a direct map for multipass or WPW ([learning-science](learning-science.md)).

## Inquiry-Led Multipass
Each pass is defined by whatever you are currently most curious about, so the passes naturally track the layers of learning. The fastest, most enjoyable and most retention-efficient variation — and the most skill-demanding.

- A pass equals the prominent question whose answer would most improve your understanding right now. With zero knowledge your questions are basic and about big chunks → pass 1 builds the logic-layer framework (the Shoot mind-map skeleton). With the framework in place, questions get more specific → pass 2 lands in the concepts layer → pass 3 straddles concepts plus important details (sub-sub-concepts, processes) → pass 4 covers layers 3–4 nitty-gritty.
- Passes are **not deliberately assigned to layers**; the progression emerges because knowledge unravels from big chunks to small. Hence "layers of learning", not "layers of studying".
- You must cover the entire block each pass (the whole biology topic at once, if that is the block). Very large blocks may need multiple days per pass and high skill; at some point it is more sensible to break into topics.
- Technically fastest because it follows the brain's own highest-retention order and covers highest-yield material first. Even if you miss the details, you have the logic and concepts — the most accessible and most examinable material.
- Requires comfortable Hipshot with 90%+ retention applied off active recall. If you are not there yet, keep practising BHS or use the objective-led variation.

Steps:
1. Take the whole block; ask the biggest-chunk questions (what is this, why is it important, how does it fit).
2. Aim–Shoot until a basic framework mind map exists → pass 1.
3. Ask the next tier of questions on that framework; add concepts → pass 2.
4. Ask about sub-sub-concepts and processes; add to the mind map, flashcards and modified method of loci → pass 3.
5. Ask about fine detail → pass 4 (add more passes if needed).
6. Final clean sweep against learning objectives; patch gaps.

Common mistakes: forcing each pass to equal exactly one layer; using it when Hipshot and inquiry skill are not there yet; skipping the keyword-free Hipshot and reverting to full keyword Aim.

## Objective-Led Multipass
Each pass covers the **easiest remaining learning objectives** across the whole block. Psychologically less effective than inquiry-led, but far easier to make time strategic when exam pressure is high.

- Every curriculum has learning objectives; it is a legal requirement, and assessment must be based on them. Interpretation can be loose — which is where "curveball" questions come from.
- Learning objectives replace the keyword Aim step: you Aim from objectives, Shoot to build knowledge, then almost-Skin it into a structural mind map.
- Pass 1 builds random, fragmented "spikes" of knowledge across the whole curriculum. Pass 2: attempt a basic structural mind map from what you know; the gaps generate questions ("how does this objective relate to that one?") → an aim–shoot cycle bridges them. Chunking here recovers some of the retention lost by not self-generating chunks. Pass 3: re-scan the objectives — some that seemed hard are now easy because of what you learned; cover them and add to the map. Pass 4: re-run aim–shoot on the new material and bridge it to what you already know. Repeat until done.
- When time is short and concepts feel solid, jump to the final clean-sweep pass and grind the remaining details onto flashcards and memorisation tools.
- Lower retention than self-chunked inquiry-led, mitigated by the chunking done while building the map. Use when exam urgency is strong and you must be maximally strategic; otherwise prefer inquiry-led. The instructor has used both in different situations — build the skill of running your inquiry techniques under different systems.

Steps:
1. List all learning objectives for the block.
2. In the available session (e.g. 6 hours), cover the easiest objectives first, broadly across the whole block.
3. Attempt a basic structural mind map; note every relationship gap.
4. Aim–Shoot to answer those gaps and bridge the spikes.
5. Re-scan objectives for the next-easiest tier; repeat as passes 3–4.
6. Final clean sweep; memorise the remaining details.

## Notebook & Revision Diagnostics
Very specific questions the instructor asks during student evaluations, because small artifacts of how someone studies reveal fundamental gaps in their learning:

- **"How much time between reading something and writing it down?"** → reveals how much thinking, processing and organising is happening in their head.
- **"What type of notebook do you use?"** → an A4 lined book implies linear notes, no mind maps, relationships not prioritised.
- **"How many colours do you use?"** → few colours means little visual emphasis on main points (priority on information rather than relationships). Many colours plus an A4 lined book = pretty linear notes with no relationship building at all.
- **"When you revise, how long do you wait between reading your notes and actually recalling?"** → most people skim first, which defeats true recall.

These artifacts diagnose learning quality, not intelligence or effort. Audit your own (notebook type, colour use, read→write delay, skim→recall delay) as a fast diagnostic, and use the answers to decide which technique to fix first.
