# The Bear Hunter System (Aim / Shoot / Skin)
> The course's three-step encoding system — Aim (prime the brain with questions), Shoot (build logical, interconnected understanding), Skin (consolidate into a clean, chunked map) — that turns any subject into durable, usable knowledge with minimal rote memorisation.

## Overview
The Bear Hunter System (BHS) is the encoding pillar of the whole iCanStudy system: one refinable process that consolidates inquiry, order control, layers of learning, mind maps and chunking into a single workflow for any subject (sciences, maths, English, languages, law, accounting, medicine, etc.). The hunting metaphor is deliberate: like a cave-era hunter, you aim your brain at the topic for maximum relevance, shoot it down with logical answers, then skin it into a clean, usable structure — exploiting the brain's evolved learning tendencies rather than fighting them. This note covers the three steps, aim–shoot cycling, the scan pass, the Hipshot variation, subject ratios, when to restudy vs redo, how BHS is scheduled across the study cycle, and how every other technique plugs into it. Step-level detail lives in sibling notes: the full Aim procedure in [aiming](aiming.md), note quality (VPREFRE) and the Shoot note-taking workflow in [note-taking](note-taking.md), chunking and GRINDE maps in [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md), rote leftovers in [memorisation](memorisation.md), and gap-catching revision in [revision](revision.md).

## BHS Overview: Aim / Shoot / Skin
A single three-step encoding system exploiting the brain's cave-era learning tendencies (see Cave Theory in [learning-science](learning-science.md)): inquiry-based problem solving plus pattern finding satisfy both halves of the brain's relevance checklist. It becomes the encoding pillar for any subject.

- **Aim** — scope the topic, collect keywords, and drive inquiry with "Why is this important?" and "How does this relate to ___?" to hypothesise a backbone structure and prime a web of questions. Targets Bloom levels 3–5 from the very start.
- **Shoot** — analyse resources in detail, answering the primed questions with logical, interconnected answers and building the mind map in layers (logic → concepts → details). Normally lands at Apply/Analyse (levels 3–4).
- **Skin** — re-chunk, weigh, critique and prioritise relationships; redraw as a GRINDE map. Lifts learning to Evaluate (level 5); advanced revision can reach Create (6). "Aim higher and you land higher."

Key principles:
- Three pillars of the full system: (1) encoding = BHS; (2) retrieval = spaced interleaved retrieval (foundational, upgradable later); (3) self-management enablers (time/task management, prioritisation, procrastination reduction, goal setting, stress management — [time-management](time-management.md), [habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md), [mindset-emotion](mindset-emotion.md)). The experiential cycle (COBES / Kolb: experience → reflection → abstraction → experimentation) binds all three and drives lifelong refinement.
- Higher-quality encoding → stickier knowledge, easier retrieval sessions, longer spacing gaps, and use of knowledge in more diverse, complex problem-solving.
- BHS replaces the traffic-light inquiry system and basic collect-and-process. Keep using and refining your current note-taking/mind-mapping (with COBES) until Ascent II upgrades it to augment Aim.
- BHS handles large volumes quickly, so some back-and-forth and error is inevitable and efficient — do not perfect every structure during Aim/Shoot; relationships realised later (e.g. in a fifth hour of study) get fixed in Skin.
- Readiness prerequisites: keyword scoping, curiosity-driven inquiry, grouping/chunking and relating chunks, non-linear notes/mind maps, interleaving experience, a COBES habit, and self-management skills at least at conscious competence.

Common mistakes:
- Entering BHS before basic evaluation, prioritising, grouping and non-linear note-taking are comfortable — slow down first.
- Trying to make every structure perfect during Aim/Shoot instead of deferring cleanup to Skin.

## Aim — Scope and Prime (Role in BHS)
Aim is the first encoding step whenever it happens: it points the brain at the topic for maximum relevance so that Shoot is fast and higher-order learning feels less overwhelming. Its outputs are:
- An unordered keyword list covering ~80% of the major ideas (typically 15–30 keywords per topic, ~25 minutes — not 100%, not 2 hours), collected deliberately out of the taught order to avoid framing bias.
- Recognition-level meanings only (a glance at each definition so questions make sense — never memorisation at this stage).
- A web of why-important / how-related questions across keywords and headings, with multiple candidate relationships and groupings.
- Several hypothetical backbone structures: a shallow but wide net (main ideas, chunks, concepts — no key details). Everything is a hypothesis, constantly tested and revised through Aim, Shoot and even Skin; avoid the sunk-cost fallacy.

Aim quality directly determines Shoot quality. The full procedure, question-quality rules, barriers, the Aim-vs-pre-study distinction, the topic-size sweet spot and subject-specific examples live in [aiming](aiming.md). Speed up the easy part — keyword collection — by skimming slides/course guides, scrubbing video, or using transcripts/AI extraction; the evaluative thinking is the desirable difficulty and must stay yours. Aim is purely textual (keyword collection + chunking); visuals are added later during Shoot (Clinic 18).

## Shoot — Logical, Interconnected Answers
Shoot satiates the curiosity created in Aim: it answers the high-quality questions with logical, interconnected answers, building a firmly connected web of knowledge. It must stay aligned with the aimed direction, or retention collapses back into passive linear studying.

- **Logical** — you know why the answer is what it is; not accepted because the teacher/textbook said so, not merely memorised.
- **Interconnected** — maximise connections to/from each node; the brain keeps what is relevant to many other things, so a web signals "don't throw this away".
- **Relationships-focused, not information-focused** — facts and exam details should be logical consequences of relationships; isolated "what/definition" facts get forgotten.
- Calibrate to organically learned expertise (a violinist's bow length, a footballer's positioning): such detail is never rote-memorised, it "just makes sense". Aim for most learning to be logical; limit rote to the leftover.
- Good shooting looks explorative — lots of thinking, flicking pages back and forth, writing a few notes, connecting them, generating the next questions. Outwardly "chaotic" but inwardly logical; following someone else's prescribed order is the true chaos for the brain.
- Note-taking is most of studying; the VPREFRE guidelines ([note-taking](note-taking.md)) are the main safeguard that keeps Shoot aligned with Aim instead of slipping into old habits.

Processing vs Shoot (Clinic 25, Clinic 26, Clinic 27):
- **Processing** = any note-taking that involves thinking ("think before you write"); it happens at every stage. Passive/unprocessed notes (sensory in → random out) are only useful for a transcriber.
- **Shoot** is a specific, refined method of processing that activates greater cognitive effects (processing = "think where to kick the ball"; Shoot = practising a specific penalty/cross-field pass).
- Shoot replaces your *processing* of notes, NOT your *processed notes* — keep writing processed notes for whatever you Aim. Aim replaces the traffic-light system (upgraded question quality), not processed notes.
- Single session: still do Aim + Shoot (even if you skip Skin). Multiple sessions: pre-study may only partially Aim, but still represent/process; continue Aim/Shoot in the lecture and review. Always flesh out the 3–4 main ideas (backbone) first.

Standard workflow (demonstrated across subjects; note-quality detail and subject examples in [note-taking](note-taking.md) and [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md)):
1. Build the most basic backbone from the major headings first — ask heading-level questions too. Expect 20–30+ minutes even for four headings (beginners 2–3 hours). Do not rush: everything hangs on this; reaching a clean backbone is ~80% of the battle.
2. Hang sub-concepts on the backbone using the Aim questions; rearrange as understanding changes.
3. Add full detail in a further pass (e.g. a different colour) — the material has now been passed through 3–4 times.
4. Keep arbitrary details (equations, constants, definitions) off the map → flashcards ([memorisation](memorisation.md)).
5. Hand over to Skin.

Benchmarks: whole biology topic ~5–6 h beginners vs ~1.5–2 h experienced; a chemistry area of study under 10 h total with 90%+ retention; a maths calculus conceptual map ~1.5 h from near-zero. ~95–100% of what is on the map is retained days later; much detail is learned inadvertently.

Common mistakes:
- Aiming well, then opening the textbook and reverting to linear passive reading/writing because bad habits take over; thinking "I started Aiming, so I stop processing/writing notes"; treating processing and Shoot as identical.
- Information-focused notes that leave facts isolated; rushing the backbone to "get into the detail"; memorising details during structure passes.
- Forcing weak connections between unrelated clusters — keep them as separate mind maps instead.
- The four classic objections, rebutted: "mind maps don't work for me" (they were done wrong — usually jumping layers into detail); "it takes too long" (structure-building time IS learning; at unconscious competence it runs on autopilot); "it's too difficult" (effort and confusion are the brain problem-solving — the sign of correct technique); "what if I miss something?" (learn ~80% via inquiry at high retention, then rote-learn the remaining ~20%; nothing is lost by the end).

## Aim–Shoot Cycling
Studying proceeds as a continuous loop: aim (ask questions) → shoot (answer them) → new questions emerge from what was learned → aim again. It is not a rigid top-to-bottom pass; you cycle back and forth across keywords, jumping/skipping and rearranging as understanding grows. Cycling every few minutes keeps learning inquiry-driven and produces a densely connected mind map.

- Demonstrated cycle frequency: every 3–5 minutes (maths), every 5–10 minutes (biology, chemistry). Roughly half of all questions asked arise from shooting, not from the initial Aim.
- Initial written questions are a springboard; as knowledge grows, questions become more specific and navigation fluid. Beginners write questions down (to practise creating them and audit quality); advanced learners ask them aloud or mentally.
- Only read what answers a current question — never read something "purely because it's there" (order control). Learn "what is it" only deeply enough to answer "why is it important" and "how does it relate".
- Early keywords take longest (many possible relationships only known in retrospect); it speeds up as patterns build (e.g. 100 keywords: first 50 ≈ 10 h, next 50 ≈ 2 h). Keep hypotheses in mind about relationships to concepts not yet on the map — the most important should already be primed in pre-study (an educated guess, not a blind stab). Explore a suspected relationship now (skip ahead) or test it when you naturally reach it (Clinic 26, Clinic 27).
- Fill in the main concepts under every chunk before going deep on any one part; stay big-picture in early passes and park premature details for later layers. Tolerate mid-topic overwhelm — it consolidates and "clicks" later, making subsequent layers much faster.
- Represent each answer minimally on the map (a star, doodle, arrow, colour); skip transcribing lists you don't need to memorise yet; rearrange spatially as understanding changes; park uncertain connections ("maybe related — come back later") instead of forcing weak links.
- "I don't care about writing notes, I care about developing knowledge" — don't write down what you won't forget.
- Use targeted Google queries when the resource doesn't answer (Google Images is fast for structural pictures). When the big picture is covered, repeat at the next layer (sub-concepts), then details; close leftover gaps with revision and memorisation aids for ~100% coverage.
- Relating new info back to the whole constantly makes much detail intuitive without trying: less to memorise, higher automatic retention, and a logical later detail pass.

Unanswered questions and the switching penalty (Clinic 28):
- Going back from Shoot to Aim when you discover missed keywords is normal, not failure. The cognitive switching penalty only matters if you constantly miss "a truckload" of keywords — then Aim more comprehensively up front. Don't over-minimise switching out of fear of the penalty.
- Valid reasons to leave an Aim question unanswered: it is out of scope for the problem you're solving; the question doesn't make sense; you already know/covered the answer. Questions create potential relationships that may turn out invalid — not all must be answered.
- The requirement: questions must propel you to find multiple relationships that can be compared to yield chunks.

Example: in the metacognition demonstration, cognition vs intelligence was drawn as skill/process vs ability (how fast the car is vs your skill driving it), fluid vs crystallised intelligence contrasted, and metamemory placed as an application of metacognitive knowledge — each answer generating the next question.

## Skin — Consolidate to Evaluate
Skin is the third step: it consolidates the post-Shoot mind map into a cleaner, more relational, higher-mastery structure by re-chunking and re-evaluating relationships, lifting learning from Apply/Analyse up to Evaluate (Bloom level 5). It yields a large retention increase and is not risky — nothing to lose, only gains.

- Correct end-state after Shoot: the topic "makes a lot of sense", big-picture understanding is solid, retention already decent, and only arbitrary layer-4 details remain unplaced. Park those on digital flashcards or a doc for now — BHS should cut the load from ~50 flashcards to ~10–20.
- First-pass maps are chaotic because weighing, critiquing and prioritising every relationship in one go is very hard. ~95% of students need Skin for the first few months; if Shoot is second nature you can reach Evaluate in one pass and skip Skin (even the instructor needs Shoot→Skin for some subjects).
- Content split: the mind map / GRINDE map holds ~90–95% of the topic; the remaining ~5–10% of arbitrary details goes to flashcards.
- A skinned map should be clean (no overlapping arrows/unknown intersects) and fuller (interleaving should have added gaps) (Clinic 25, Clinic 26, Clinic 27).

Procedure:
1. Complete Aim + Shoot; confirm the topic makes sense and only arbitrary details remain unplaced.
2. Park those arbitrary details on digital flashcards (Anki/Quizlet) or a doc.
3. Re-open the Shoot map and re-ask "why is it important?" and "how does it relate?" of each node.
4. Scan for chunking opportunities (4+ branches off a point) and group them.
5. Weigh, critique and prioritise: what is most important, second, third? Encode that as direction and emphasis.
6. Re-arrange/re-draw into a GRINDE map and audit it against the GRINDE checklist ([chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md)).
7. Optionally repeat from pure recall days later — this doubles as a high-value revision activity, better than a practice question and far better than rereading/rewriting notes.

Timing and form:
- Skin can be immediate, ~10 minutes later, or a week later; it can itself be scheduled as a form of interleaving.
- Skin is subject-specific: for English it means practice essays and model-essay analysis (recombining themes and techniques from many angles); for Maths it means practising and creating your own problems; for languages it is often optional (etymology/radical pattern-finding). These run on the Kolb experiential cycle rather than map editing — demonstrations and mechanics in [chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md).
- Skin on a tablet/digital infinite canvas so re-arranging is cheap; treat it as iterative — a map that passes at 90% can be skinned another 10%.

Example: a cardiovascular Shoot map becomes a skinned map with new chunks and thicker arrows on the important pathways; the same pattern is demonstrated for asexual reproduction, the blood system and chemistry intermolecular forces.

Common mistakes:
- Forcing Evaluate on the first Shoot pass and getting frustrated — it is normally a two-pass job.
- Rereading or rewriting notes instead of re-chunking from recall.
- Leaving arbitrary details on the map, or rote-learning things that already make sense logically.
- Doing all of Aim/Shoot only after the topic is taught in class, instead of taking control of the timing.

## Scan — the Consolidation Pass
In the early Live Clinics the third BHS step is called "scan": the consolidation sweep of the mind map that finishes off initial encoding — the same role as Skin.

- Typical placement: a couple of days after the learning event, midweek or at the end of the week, scan the mind map (or otherwise consolidate) to complete initial encoding; at that time make your flashcards and your challenge questions for later retrieval rounds (Clinic 12).
- Aim, Shoot and Scan are each naturally interleaved techniques — the BHS system as a whole is inherently interleaving (Clinic 12).
- Visual processing: while writing notes, decide per item whether to represent it verbally — if not, use visuals; you can add more visual processing during the scan pass (Clinic 14).
- Mastery progression: Aim + Shoot blends into seamless aim–shoot cycling, then into Hipshot + scan; eventually you may not need scan at all, or your scan is much shallower (see integration below).
- Not to be confused with scanning/skimming resources for keywords and relevance, which is an Aim-side skill — see [aiming](aiming.md).

## Hipshot Variation
The first BHS variation and the Base Camp end-goal: Aim and Shoot merge into one fast, mostly mental cycle (Hipshot → Skin), building the mind map as you go instead of writing a full keyword/question list first. "Hipshot" = shooting from the hip (no aiming down the sights) — not truly simultaneous, but rapid switching between Aim and Shoot.

- Only the most important keywords/themes are collected — the Layer-1 fundamentals that form the backbone; ignore the rest initially.
- Questions are asked mentally (the same high-quality what/why/how-relate), answered immediately from resources (course guides, videos, Google) and placed straight on the map.
- Layers spiral upward: early keywords/questions are basic (Layer 1), later ones more advanced (Layers 2→4) until the topic is finished.
- The map starts messy; Skin later cleans it into a well-connected GRINDE map (sometimes skippable if already clean).
- Relationships remain the #1 priority; the cycle must feed back to generate better questions (free exploration/discovery), not become a one-directional information dump.

Procedure:
1. Collect only the most important (Layer-1) keywords.
2. Take the first keyword; mentally ask "what is it?" → search/study → answer and put on map.
3. Mentally ask "why is it important?" → answer and map.
4. Mentally ask "how does it relate to X/Y/Z and to what is already on the map?" → answer and map, building spatial awareness.
5. Take the next keyword and repeat, always relating back to the existing map.
6. Skin the resulting messy map into a clean GRINDE map.

Prerequisites and readiness:
- Spatial organisation/space planning, relational thinking, note-taking principles (VPREFRE), layers of learning and order control must be automatic; standard BHS must be comfortable first. Do not attempt Hipshot straight away. Aim for ~100% accuracy and ~80% toward unconscious competence; the instructor would want to see a full Aim/Shoot/Skin at high level first (Clinic 27).
- **Logic-layer diagnostic (Clinic 15):** if you are stressed about getting the logic layer wrong during Hipshot, you are not ready. A consistent logic layer is the reliable output of proper inquiry (correct parameters) + order control + good chunking + relational thinking. Hipshot's legitimate difficulty is running the processes *simultaneously* and processing to simplicity *very fast, very early* — not accuracy. Stress about accuracy = undesirable difficulty in a fundamental component; go back and secure it.
- **Mental-chunking lever (Clinic 18):** 9/10 Hipshot struggles mean standard BHS is not done strictly/accurately enough — Hipshot exposes under-the-radar flaws (with standard BHS the logic is visible because information is laid out; Hipshot removes that scaffolding, so the skill must be internalised). To raise cognitive load, chunk as much as possible IN YOUR HEAD before writing anything down — that capacity is the main lever. Natural progression: the Aim–Shoot cycle becomes so fast a dedicated set is no longer needed, and Hipshot emerges easily. If it fails, submit work to check standard BHS execution.
- Keep chunking intuitively as long as possible; don't drop into abstract terminology too early at layer 3. Hitting cognitive load at the important-details layer is fine (difficulty is part of the process), but the logic/concepts layers must be clear first (Clinic 27).
- Readiness test: if you compromise relationships, layers or order control, you are not ready — revert to standard BHS (which already works better than prior techniques) and retry later. Even a "glimmer" of Hipshot on an easy subject is fine; it takes consistent practice and deep familiarity. Practise on a topic big enough to exercise the technique properly.
- Checkpoint standard: Hipshot output map quality at least equal to a dedicated Aim+Shoot map; Skin is topic-dependent — skip it if the Hipshot map is already logical, importance-chunked and well-layered; Skin large/complex/new topics to consistently high quality. Later course variations assume full BHS + Hipshot mastery.
- Base Camp itself is a consolidation plateau: keep practising and scheduling BHS consistently for a few weeks to acclimatise before layering on advanced skills — do not "ascend too quickly".

Practical tips from the accounting demonstration (VCE Accounting, a subject the instructor had never studied):
- Environment first: phone silent/away, water and snack ready, distractions settled before starting.
- Constant "why is this important?" gating: never move on until you can answer it; if unclear, Google it (image search often explains better) or think harder. Focusing solely on this question also keeps you relaxed; take breaks when tired (look outside, walk).
- Constant zoom in/out: after taking in knowledge, zoom back and find tighter fits — "time spent simplifying information is always time well spent."
- Represent words with images/non-words to manipulate information mentally: deeper encoding plus immediate retrieval practice on the go.
- Snowball effect: a well-connected foundation makes each new piece click faster — ~100% retention of material covered; what isn't retained is understood well enough to reconstruct.
- Definitions need not be rote-learned: deep understanding lets you generate an accurate definition. Skip lists derivable from the concept (e.g. examples of current/non-current assets). Write repeated identical content once, not every time it recurs. Proper importance-based chunking can even make an existing mnemonic unnecessary ([chunking-grinde](chunking-grinde.md)).
- Mini-skim the next ~10 pages before dry/dense sections so "why important" can be answered earlier.
- Arbitrary detail with no conceptual hook (e.g. GST tax-invoice requirements) → rote technique (modified method of loci or managed flashcards), not the mind map ([memorisation](memorisation.md)).
- A drawn connection may be kept provisionally and removed later if it becomes too obvious/messy, or moved to a flashcard.
- Pace: ~1 hour covered ~17/55 pages with near-total retention — depth early, speed later. The instructor's speed comes from tens of thousands of repetitions; do not feel pressured to skip steps. If consolidating-as-you-go is too hard, revert to explicit Aim → Shoot → Skin.

Common mistakes: trying Hipshot before standard BHS is comfortable; getting lazy on relationships; dropping into memorisation; getting sucked into just answering questions (information dump) instead of building an interconnected, question-generating map; using a topic too small/narrow to practise properly.

## Subject Ratios: Maths and Procedural Subjects
The same BHS works for every subject because it is built on how the brain processes memory; only the RATIO of time spent on conceptual vs procedural components changes. Maths/physics (and chemistry to a degree) add a heavier procedural problem-solving layer on top of identical conceptual encoding (Clinic 10, Clinic 12).

- Subjects test different mixes: fact recall, concept/relationship understanding, application/problem-solving, and multi-concept problem-solving. You must hit all of them; the ratio shifts by subject. High-school biology is ~80–90% concepts/relationships with rare direct application; maths/physics need substantially more procedural problem-solving to interleave.
- For maths, conceptual understanding of equations and their inter-relationships comes FIRST: without it you cannot recognise which combination of formulae a contextual problem requires (no mid-exam eureka). Importance-based chunking then tells you which approaches are relevant; procedural practice follows. Maths questions are always importance-based — real-world contexts mirror how equations are actually used.
- **Equations balance (Clinic 12):** get a conceptual gist that "makes sense" (e.g. KE = ½mv² — mass × velocity squared feels right); you do NOT need to derive why the ½ or the square (a lengthy pure-maths proof), and asking "why is every variable important" is too time-consuming. Exact memorisation and usage of equations come purely through practice/repetition; BHS saves the time that makes this repetition affordable. Conceptual fluency = knowing what each equation/theorem lets you DO (Clinic 24).
- Efficiency comes from big-picture understanding, not truckloads of practice questions — the UCAT insight: endless questions don't teach you how to do the question; understand first, then the approach is logical. The maths Shoot demo locks in the purpose/application structure before learning any equation, flipping the practice ratio from 1:1 to ~10:1 ([note-taking](note-taking.md)); procedural practice itself is interleaved retrieval ([revision](revision.md)).
- At university maths level the focus shifts even more toward deep connections between concepts (Clinic 10).

Common mistakes:
- Learning the system only on science subjects and creating a distorted science-specific variant that "doesn't work" for maths — return to the original principles, which carry through exactly. (If you learned it on maths first, biology would feel like overkill — that asymmetry explains why science-first students doubt its universality.)
- Practising only simple procedural problems and expecting multi-concept curveball ability to appear.
- Memorising equations by rote without conceptual sense, or over-analysing every variable's derivation.

## BHS Is First-Pass Encoding: Restudy vs Redo
BHS is used for the FIRST study of material to achieve high-quality first-pass encoding; subsequent retrieval uses different, interleaved methods. Don't re-run the full system on well-encoded topics — and material learned before you had the technique should simply be restudied properly with it (Clinic 12).

- BHS is naturally interleaved itself (aim, shoot and scan), but revision should leverage the same thinking through other techniques (re-Aiming/Hipshot revision, reconstruction, WPW, challenge questions) rather than re-running the full system — see [revision](revision.md).
- **Restudy, don't patch:** for old topics studied without BHS (e.g. half a year of inferior notes), patching individual gaps takes LONGER than relearning properly. A poor first pass means low retention, narrow recall, narrow non-interleaved revision and minimal curveball protection — too many gaps to fix one by one. "Covered it with your eyes" or "wrote notes on it" is not learning; poorly encoded material is effectively unlearned (Clinic 15).
- BHS is encompassing: doing it once properly handles prioritisation, comparison, higher-order learning, relationships, chunking, organisation and retrieval-matched structure together. Proper re-encoding is intensive and hits information from many angles, so it is itself powerful interleaved revision (Clinic 15).
- With even moderate BHS fluency, restudying is fast: six months of previously-learned material should take ~1–2 weeks, because your study speed is higher AND it is effectively revision of known content. After restudying, proceed to high-volume retrieval, then past papers/challenge questions.

Common mistakes: re-running full BHS on well-encoded topics instead of interleaved retrieval; trying to salvage old, poorly-encoded notes gap-by-gap instead of a clean restudy; feeling daunted by "six months of material" without accounting for fluency + prior exposure. "Just study it again" is the honest, simple answer for pre-technique material — it's faster than it sounds (Clinic 12).

## BHS Across the Study Cycle
BHS is applied across the spacing ladder with rising layer targets (full ladder and retrieval rounds in [revision](revision.md)):

1. **Pre-study (topic-wide, before class):** simple Aim–Shoot cycle, no Skin yet; target at least layers 1+2 (optionally bits of 3). Relational priority plus importance-based chunking gives high-efficiency first-pass encoding (working → long-term memory), flattening the forgetting curve.
2. **Class/lecture (daily):** consolidate layers 2+3 (maybe parts of 4, and reinforce layer 1). Make relationships accurate and explicit — not just "they're linked" but exactly what the relationship is and how it compares to others.
3. **Same-day review:** add layers 3+4; Skin the mind map now (great time to do it); integrate auxiliary textbook details; reach ~90–100% of the topic well understood; add memorisation aids (modified method of loci, ± flashcards with active flashcard management) so the rote load stays manageable.
4. **Midweek (optional, post-Skin):** repeating the same is lower yield — switch to gap-hunting (challenge questions, mini brain dump).
5. **Weekly/monthly/pre-test:** high-volume retrieval, gap-seeking and stylistic calibration — see [revision](revision.md) and [exam-technique](exam-technique.md).

Timing is flexible: Aim can double as pre-study (before class, even before term); Shoot can be layered across class, that evening and the weekend; Skin can be immediate or delayed. Aim and pre-study are not the same thing — Aim is a cognitive-order concept, pre-study a timing concept ([aiming](aiming.md)). BHS is inherently interleaving at every stage: following it varies expression automatically. As skill grows, rigid day-based scheduling matters less — plan sessions by which layers you will cover (layer 1 only? 1–2? 1–4?) and let Aim–Shoot cycling fill the time available (Clinic 18). Shoot can also run in small pockets of dead time; ease depends on cognitive proficiency and becomes a natural habit of thinking with practice ([time-management](time-management.md), [professional-learning](professional-learning.md)) (Clinic 34).

Common mistakes: weak/implicit relationships instead of explicit, accurate ones; overwhelming memorisation load by not integrating aids seamlessly; re-Skinning at midweek instead of switching to gap-seeking retrieval; doing all encoding only after class instead of controlling the timing.

## How the Techniques Integrate into One System
BHS is not a standalone tip but the core of one interlocking system: each technique's weaknesses are caught by another's strengths, and only in conjunction do they produce the total effect (a revolution, not an evolution — [course-guide](course-guide.md)). Skipping component techniques because they seem irrelevant ("selective learning") breaks the composites built on them ([habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md)).

Routing by layer of learning ([learning-science](learning-science.md)):
- **Layers 1–3 (logic, concepts, important details):** handled conceptually via inquiry, order control, mind maps and GRINDE maps — reaching Apply/Analyse and then Evaluate (weighing, critiquing, ranking, prioritising ideas).
- **Layers 3–4 (rote and arbitrary content):** digital flashcards with active flashcard management and microlearning, plus the modified method of loci (including numbers via pre-assigned digit images, and mini-loci drawn inside maps). Together these cover everything rote — the instructor has never encountered anything they could not memorise this way. Method of loci is so retention-efficient it needs far less revision than flashcards (next day, next week, next month). See [memorisation](memorisation.md).
- **Residual gaps:** revision is the final Swiss-cheese layer — re-Aiming old material in Hipshot form, reconstruction, WPW and challenge questions catch what encoding inevitably missed. No first pass is perfect; misses are expected, not personal failure. See [revision](revision.md).

Replace, don't add (Clinic 22, Clinic 24):
- Each new technique REPLACES part of what you already do rather than being bolted onto old habits. The base system is spaced/interleaved retrieval + pre-study; everything in between is mostly replacement. "Everything you learn replaces something."
- Correct order: identify topics → pre-study (frame) → traffic-light/AIM questions (find relationships) → chunks → non-linear representation → THEN interleaved practice. Don't touch practice questions until you have framed, chunked, found relationships and encoded.
- Running old techniques that work against the new ones alongside them is a tug-of-war — it takes 2–3x longer to retrain thinking. If you are struggling under load, check whether old habits are still running. The AIM step only changes question types (replaces traffic-light); keep processing notes and mind-mapping.

System-level principles:
- Both sides of the coin are required: high-efficiency encoding AND spaced interleaved retrieval. Encoding is easy to fall in love with; retrieval is easy to forget. Students with poor encoding but religious retrieval still get decent results at more time and risk; strong encoders who skip retrieval lose the full benefit.
- High retention means no rewriting and relearning — the freed hours are what make the slow, deep, gap-seeking revision techniques and exam preparation (application adjustment, breaching questions) affordable ([exam-technique](exam-technique.md)). Keep an open mind in both directions: once BHS works, old standbys like past papers may feel unnecessary, while methods you dismissed may become highly effective ([revision](revision.md)).
- Keep COBES/Kolb cycles running on the system itself; it is designed for continuous refinement. As competence grows, keep raising the challenge — cognitive load should never drop, or learning quality drops with it ([learning-science](learning-science.md), [habits-behaviour](habits-behaviour.md)).
- After unconscious competence, personalise by adjusting principle weighting (more interleaving-heavy, microlearning-heavy or relationally heavy) to subject, season and life constraints. Multipass is a ready-made sprint/burst variation ([revision](revision.md)); the microlearning and clinical systems plug BHS/Hipshot into professional environments ([professional-learning](professional-learning.md)).
- Downstream systems presuppose BHS + Hipshot mastery (Base Camp checkpoint) — attempting them without it is "100% chance of failure". Practise consistently so the whole arsenal stays smooth.

Effect-first, principle-level mastery (Clinic 10, Clinic 42):
- At upper-intermediate/early-advanced mastery, individual techniques stop being separate steps and condense: mind mapping merges with multipass, aim–shoot becomes seamless cycling, then Hipshot (with scan, then shallower or no scan), and Whole-Part-Whole, layers of learning and mind maps fuse into one principle-driven system. Mind maps starting to feel like multipass sessions — or Hipshot feeling like WPW — are milestones, not errors.
- At this level you no longer hang onto individual techniques: you master the underlying PRINCIPLES well enough to modify and optimise each one, creating the technique you need on any given day. This blending is a destination, not a shortcut for beginners; repeated exposure to the same advice adds ~1% nuance each time.
- Reverse the usual "what's the best method?" question: start from the EFFECT you want, choose the method and cognitive process, then refine by experiment. There is no single best method for everyone, but there is a best direction for figuring it out. Be "the mechanic of your own brain" — understand the conditions so you can diagnose which to fill rather than depending on a coach or copying a fixed method.
- Build a system of techniques where one's gap is filled by another (gaps are expected); grow your toolbox rather than shifting it, but stay minimalist (seven simultaneous methods is redundancy). A general core: one fairly higher-order approach + one low-to-mid-order approach + interleaved retrieval + enough procedural practice if needed. As your cognitive processes improve, the methods you need get more relaxed. Don't copy another student's subject-specific adjustment as a blanket rule.
